## safety | Safety: identify the evidence before choosing an action
Sections: source-01 source-02
### Ms. Luminara
First find what the question actually tells you: the material, the operation, the stated hazard and the assigned instruction. A container's appearance does not establish its identity. Safety reasoning connects a specific condition to a specific precaution; it is not a contest to invent a reassuring answer.
### Worked example
Two bottles contain clear liquids. Looking at them cannot establish that they contain the same substance. The useful next evidence is the label and the assigned handling information. “They look alike” is an observation, not permission to exchange them.
### A nearby contrast
Identifying a hazard and deciding what controls it are different steps. A glove that suits one material is not automatically suitable for every material. Use the actual course procedure and safety information for the decision.
### Try another
Choose a worksheet scenario. Point to the given condition, name the information still needed, and explain how the assigned rule addresses that condition. Do not perform a new experiment to fill a missing answer.
### Sources and scope
Conceptual support for the two supplied safety worksheets. The assigned lab instructions, instructor and material-specific safety data govern actual handling. No procedure, exposure limits or emergency treatment is supplied here.

## polymers | Polymers: connect a structure model to an observation
Sections: source-03 source-13
### Ms. Luminara
A polymer contains large molecules with repeated structural units. The arrangement and interactions of chains can affect how a material flows or deforms. Describe what your sample did before deciding what microscopic explanation the evidence supports.
### Worked example
Imagine loose chains made from paper clips, then add a few links between the chains. The second arrangement can constrain their movement. This is a model for thinking about connections; paper clips are not chemical bonds and do not reproduce the recipe's chemistry.
### A nearby contrast
“The sample bounced” is an observation. “A network of connected chains explains the response” is a proposed model. A bounce by itself does not prove which chemical interaction occurred or whether a new substance formed.
### Try another
Compare two recorded observations under the same stated conditions. What changed, what stayed constant, and which extra measurement could distinguish your proposed explanations? Leave absent experimental data blank.
### Sources and scope
Authored structural analogy for the polymer and bouncy-ball worksheets. It does not establish the class recipe's reaction mechanism or provide instructions for mixing materials. Use the recorded procedure and actual observations.

## notation | Scientific notation: change the written scale, preserve the value
Sections: source-04 source-19
### Ms. Luminara
In a × 10ⁿ, the coefficient carries the significant digits and the exponent tells you the scale. Standard scientific notation uses a coefficient whose magnitude is at least 1 and less than 10. Moving the decimal is a rewrite of the same number, not a change to the quantity.
### Worked example
0.000432 = 4.32 × 10⁻⁴. Four decimal places separate the new coefficient from the original value. Multiplying 4.32 by 0.0001 recovers 0.000432. For 43,200 written to three significant figures, use 4.32 × 10⁴.
### A nearby contrast
An exponent's minus sign means a small positive scale when the coefficient is positive; it does not make the number negative. −4.32 × 10⁴ is a negative number. Also, value, notation and measurement precision are different checks.
### Try another
Rewrite 0.00670, then multiply your coefficient back by its power of ten. Keep the final zero when expressing the supplied three-significant-figure precision. Explain what each check establishes.
### Sources and scope
OpenStax Chemistry 2e 1.4–1.5 and the preserved Q3 records. These are authored examples, not replacements for your entered answers. Trailing zeros in an unqualified whole number can be ambiguous; state the intended precision.

## electrons | Electron configurations: count electrons before filling orbitals
Sections: source-05 source-11 source-12 source-16
### Ms. Luminara
Atomic number counts protons. A neutral atom has the same number of electrons. For an ion, electron count is proton count minus the signed charge. Orbital notation distributes that inventory: s holds at most 2, p 6, d 10 and f 14 electrons across their orbitals.
### Worked example
Neutral sodium has 11 protons and 11 electrons: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹, or [Ne] 3s¹. Sodium's +1 ion has 10 electrons and configuration [Ne]. Magnesium's +2 ion also has 10 electrons, but its 12 protons make it magnesium, not neon or sodium.
### A nearby contrast
A shell, a subshell and an orbital are different organizational levels. Each orbital holds at most two electrons with opposite spins. Hund's rule favors separate occupation before pairing in degenerate orbitals in the introductory ground-state model.
### Try another
Count protons and electrons for neutral oxygen and its −2 ion before writing configurations. Check the sum of all superscripts. For a transition-metal question, consult the appropriate ground-state/removal rule rather than extending the sodium example blindly.
### Sources and scope
OpenStax Chemistry 2e 2.3, 2.6 and 6.4. Ground-state configuration ordering has exceptions, including chromium and copper. Transition-metal ion formation does not always remove electrons in the order a simple filling mnemonic suggests.

## precision | Significant figures: the operation decides the reporting rule
Sections: source-06
### Ms. Luminara
Digits report measurement precision; they do not tell you whether an instrument was accurate. For the course's introductory rules, addition and subtraction follow decimal place, while multiplication and division follow significant-figure count. Retain guard digits until the final reporting step.
### Worked example
1.20 + 0.034 = 1.234 before rounding. The first measurement ends at the hundredths place, so report 1.23. For multiplication, 3.4 × 1.22 = 4.148 before rounding; the two-significant-figure factor leads to 4.1.
### A nearby contrast
The zero in 1.20 contributes to reported precision. Leading zeros in 0.0012 locate the decimal but do not add significant figures. Defined conversion factors and counted exact quantities do not limit significant figures like uncertain measurements do.
### Try another
Write “decimal place” or “significant-figure count” beside the next operation before calculating. Explain which input limits reporting and why. If a source number's precision is ambiguous, keep that question visible.
### Sources and scope
OpenStax Chemistry 2e 1.5–1.6. These introductory reporting conventions are not a complete propagation-of-uncertainty model. A displayed matching numerical value alone does not prove correct precision, units or experimental accuracy.

## matter | Matter: follow composition through the classification tree
Sections: source-07
### Ms. Luminara
Ask first whether a sample has uniform, fixed composition as a pure substance or variable composition as a mixture. Within pure substances, distinguish one element from chemically combined different elements. Within mixtures, inspect uniformity at the scale relevant to the example.
### Worked example
Pure liquid water is a compound. Dissolved sodium chloride in water is a mixture, even when it looks uniform. The salt and water can occur in different proportions; looking clear does not make the solution a pure substance.
### A nearby contrast
Melting ice changes physical state while retaining water's chemical identity. A chemical change produces a different chemical composition. Dramatic appearance, bubbles or irreversibility alone are not universal tests for reaction.
### Try another
Take one Chapter 1 example and explain every branch in its classification. Then distinguish an attribute of the sample from an event that changes it. Keep uncertain handwritten classifications as notes, not official keys.
### Sources and scope
OpenStax Chemistry 2e 1.2–1.3 and the six existing workbook lessons. Classification assumes the stated sample and scale; missing composition or context can prevent a unique answer.

## factors | Factors and fence posts: make the structure explicit
Sections: source-08 source-09 source-14
### Ms. Luminara
A conversion factor is a ratio of equal quantities. Orient it so the unwanted unit cancels and the wanted unit remains. For counting layouts, draw the connections first: the relationship between sections and endpoints depends on the topology, not just a remembered extra one.
### Worked example
2.50 h × (60 min / 1 h) = 150 min, reported as 1.50 × 10² min to keep three significant figures. The hour units cancel; the defined factor 60 is exact. Separately, four panels in one open straight fence need five endpoint posts if neighboring panels share a post.
### A nearby contrast
Four panels in a closed loop need four shared posts in the simple model, not five. A conversion with squared units needs the whole linear factor squared: 1 m² = 10,000 cm², not 100 cm². Temperature scales with an offset are not handled by a simple scale-only ratio.
### Try another
Show the starting quantity, each equality and each canceled unit. For a fence problem, mark shared corners before counting. An annotated earlier attempt is evidence of a strategy; test its assumptions rather than copying its result.
### Sources and scope
OpenStax Chemistry 2e 1.6; authored fence-layout examples with explicitly stated assumptions. The native factor tool checks the selected problem's reviewed model, while physical layouts may require additional supports or boundary conditions.

## isotopes | Isotopes and ions: separate the nuclear ledger from electrons
Sections: source-10
### Ms. Luminara
Atomic number Z is the proton count. Mass number A is protons plus neutrons, so neutrons = A − Z. Charge compares protons with electrons. An isotope change concerns neutrons; an ordinary ion change concerns electrons. Neither makes the atomic number change.
### Worked example
Neutral carbon-14 has 6 protons, 8 neutrons and 6 electrons. Magnesium-24 with charge +2 has 12 protons, 12 neutrons and 10 electrons. The 24 is a count of nucleons; the +2 is a net charge, not a neutron count.
### A nearby contrast
The periodic table's average atomic mass is not the mass number of one chosen atom. In a simple supplied isotope mixture, calculate a weighted mean using abundances that sum to 1. Do not round an average to infer an individual atom's neutrons.
### Try another
Write three separate lines headed protons, neutrons and electrons. Identify the given Z, A and signed charge before calculating. Then change only the charge and say which of your three counts must stay unchanged.
### Sources and scope
OpenStax Chemistry 2e 2.3 and the existing Q6 inventory model. Mass number is a whole-number count; an isotope's measured mass is not exactly that integer in atomic mass units. No nuclear decay or radiation procedure is implied.

## periodic | Periodic patterns: use a family as a guide, not a universal charge
Sections: source-15
### Ms. Luminara
A period locates a row; a group locates a column. For the common main-group patterns used here, group 1 commonly forms +1 monatomic ions, group 2 +2, and halogens −1. Neutral-atom valence electron counts and an ion's net charge are different quantities.
### Worked example
Neutral calcium has 20 protons and 20 electrons. Losing two electrons leaves 18: 20 − 18 = +2. Its group helps you anticipate the familiar ion, while the electron ledger explains the sign. It remains calcium because the 20 protons did not change.
### A nearby contrast
Ammonium is NH₄⁺, a whole polyatomic ion; its charge is not read from nitrogen's group number. Its introductory Lewis budget is 5 + 4(1) − 1 = 8 valence electrons. Losing negative charge makes a cation; adding a proton to neutral ammonia is another route to this particular ion.
### Try another
Compare chloride with neutral chlorine: what stays the same, and what changes? For a variable-charge metal or unfamiliar polyatomic ion, use an established charge or the prompt's evidence instead of guessing from one column.
### Sources and scope
OpenStax Chemistry 2e 2.6 and the admitted Ion Language references. These common monatomic-ion trends are not a table of every oxidation state. Helium's filled shell has two electrons; transition metals and many chemical contexts need more information.

## measurement | Measurement: keep a number, a unit and its uncertainty together
Sections: source-17 source-18
### Ms. Luminara
Say what was measured and with which instrument. For an ordinary analog scale, record the certain digits and one reasonable estimated digit under the course convention. A digital display provides its reported resolution; it does not make the measurement exact.
### Worked example
Suppose an authored uniform ruler has millimeter divisions and its marker is halfway between 12 and 13 mm. A plausible course reading is 12.5 mm, with the tenths estimated. This is a hypothetical display, not a recovered value from your earlier worksheet.
### A nearby contrast
Resolution is the smallest displayed or discernible step. Repeatability concerns consistency across measurements; accuracy concerns agreement with a suitable reference. A finely marked but miscalibrated instrument can be precise and inaccurate.
### Try another
Identify the unit and spacing between marks before interpreting the marker. State which digit you estimated and what limits the reading. Use your actual observations; missing data stay missing.
### Sources and scope
OpenStax Chemistry 2e 1.4–1.5 and the two preserved measurement records. Scale geometry and the instrument's actual procedure matter. Extra printed digits do not establish accuracy, and this example is not an official lab result.
