## Lesson 1 An electron ledger comes before a picture

Valence electrons are the outer electrons used in this model of bonding. For this set, memorize a small reference row: H 1; Al 3; C and Si 4; N and P 5; O and S 6; F, Cl, Br, I 7. These are electrons per neutral atom. A subscript multiplies the contribution: Cl₃ means three Cl atoms, each contributing seven.

A superscript charge changes the whole species' electron budget. Add one electron for each negative charge; subtract one for each positive charge. A minus sign means extra negatively charged electrons, not fewer electrons. In symbols, total valence electrons = sum of neutral-atom contributions − signed charge. For a −1 ion, subtracting −1 adds one.

In a complete Lewis structure, a single line means two electrons shared by two atoms. A lone pair means two electrons located on one atom in the drawing. A double bond contains two shared pairs; a triple bond contains three. Do not count each bond's electrons twice in the whole-species ledger. When checking an individual atom's octet, however, that atom counts both electrons in each bond touching it.

Ms. Luminara: “The same pair belongs in both atoms' octet checks, but it is only one pair in our total inventory. Think of one bridge connecting two towns; counting connections from each town doesn't build a second bridge.”

## Lesson 2 Build and audit a Lewis structure

Use this sequence for Q13 and Q14:

1. List each atom and its valence contribution. Adjust once for overall charge.
2. Choose the skeleton. H is terminal. In these examples, the non-halogen atom is central; HI has only two atoms.
3. Connect the skeleton with single bonds. Subtract two electrons per line from the budget.
4. Finish terminal octets using lone pairs. H needs only two electrons total and gets no extra lone pairs here.
5. Put remaining electrons on the central atom.
6. If a second-period central atom lacks an octet, move an existing terminal lone pair into a multiple bond. Do not create electrons. Check for a known exception before forcing an octet on every atom.
7. Audit total electrons, local shells, and formal charges. Enclose a polyatomic ion in brackets and label its overall charge.

Formal charge is a bookkeeping check, not a claim that every bond is fully ionic. Use formal charge = neutral valence count − nonbonding electrons − half the bonding electrons. Equivalently, subtract lone-pair electrons and the number of bond lines attached. Formal charges must sum to the overall charge. Small formal charges and a negative charge on a more electronegative atom are useful guides, alongside octet and course conventions.

### Staged worked example LG02 tied to Q13a

Prompt: Build NCl₃. Before revealing a stage, predict what changes and what stays constant.

Stage 1, budget: 5 + 3(7) = 26 electrons. The three in Cl₃ multiplies only Cl.

Stage 2, skeleton: N is central with three N–Cl single bonds. These use 6 electrons; 20 remain.

Stage 3, outside first: Give each Cl three lone pairs. Those nine pairs use 18 electrons; 2 remain.

Stage 4, finish: Put the final pair on N. N now has three bonds and one lone pair. It counts 6 bonding electrons + 2 nonbonding electrons = 8 around it.

Stage 5, audit: 6 in bonds + 18 on Cl + 2 on N = 26. Every Cl has an octet. N formal charge is 5 − 2 − 3 = 0; each Cl is 7 − 6 − 1 = 0. There are three shared pairs, not thirteen. Thirteen is the total number of pairs, including lone pairs.

Draw it yourself: put N in the middle; attach three Cl atoms; add one pair of dots to N and three pairs to every Cl. A flat T-like sketch is acceptable as a connectivity drawing; it is not a claim that the molecule is flat.

Hints if rebuilding without stages:
1. Account for bonds before adding dots.
2. Finish all three Cl atoms before N.
3. After the three bonds and Cl octets, exactly one pair remains for N.

## Lesson 3 The octet is a model with boundaries

An octet is eight electrons around an atom in a Lewis drawing. It is a strong guide for C, N, O, and F in ordinary introductory closed-shell examples. It is not permission to add electrons beyond the budget.

Three important patterns:
- A duet: H has only two electrons in its filled first shell. A single bond can satisfy it.
- An incomplete octet: examples include Be in a simple BeCl₂ model, B in BF₃, and Al in monomeric AlCl₃. Their central atoms can have fewer than eight electrons in the introductory structure.
- An expanded-valence Lewis representation: examples include P in PCl₅ and S in SF₆. Their central atoms are drawn with more than eight electrons. Do not explain this as compulsory use of d-orbital hybridization. Such Lewis pictures are bookkeeping models, not a full quantum description.

Odd-electron species form another exception category, beyond the closed-shell structures in Q11–14. An element is not permanently an “exception”: P in PBr₃ satisfies an octet, even though P in PCl₅ is commonly drawn with ten electrons.

## Lesson 4 Count regions to predict shape

Electron pairs repel, so VSEPR places regions of electron density as far apart as practical. Here, “domain” means one region around the central atom. Any single, double, or triple bond counts as one bonding domain because it points toward one neighboring atom. Each central lone pair also counts as one domain. Lone pairs on terminal atoms do not enter the central-domain count.

The worksheet's “orbital geometry” is treated here as electron-domain geometry; confirm that terminology with your instructor. Electron geometry describes all central domains, including lone pairs. Molecular geometry describes only the positions of atoms.

- Two domains: linear electron geometry; ideal separation 180°.
- Three domains: trigonal planar electron geometry; ideal separation 120°.
- Four domains: tetrahedral electron geometry; ideal separation about 109.5°.

For the molecular shape, now ask how many of those positions are atoms. With three domains but only two attached atoms, the shape is bent. With four domains but only three attached atoms, the shape is trigonal pyramidal. With no central lone pairs, molecular shape and electron geometry have the same name for these examples.

Lone pairs often occupy more angular space than bonding regions and can compress a bond angle. These are model predictions, not exact measurements. Different surrounding atoms can also produce unequal actual angles. State an ideal angle or a justified comparison, rather than inventing a measured value.

### Staged worked example LG06 tied to Q14f

Prompt: Draw NO₂⁻. Predict its shape before revealing the final stage, and state the rule you used.

Stage 1, budget: N contributes 5, two O contribute 12, and −1 adds one: 18 electrons. NO₂⁻ is a polyatomic ion; its internal bonds can still be covalent.

Stage 2, skeleton: Draw O–N–O. Two bonds spend 4 electrons. Complete both outer O octets with 12 electrons; put the remaining 2 on N.

Stage 3, local check: N has only 6 electrons around it. Move one O lone pair into an additional N–O bond. This gives one double bond and one single bond. N keeps its one lone pair; no electrons were added.

Stage 4, audit: bonds use 6; the double-bonded O has 4 nonbonding electrons; the single-bonded O has 6; N has 2. Total 18. N formal charge is 5 − 2 − 3 = 0. Double-bonded O is 6 − 4 − 2 = 0. Single-bonded O is 6 − 6 − 1 = −1. Enclose the complete structure in brackets with − outside.

Stage 5, resonance: Draw a second contributor with the double bond on the other O. Keep the same atom positions and total electrons. Connect contributors with a resonance double-headed arrow. The actual ion is a resonance hybrid with equivalent N–O bonds, not a molecule switching back and forth between a permanent single and double bond.

Stage 6, geometry: Around N there are two bonding domains and one lone-pair domain. Three total domains give trigonal planar electron geometry. The two attached O atoms occupy a bent molecular shape. Predict an O–N–O angle below 120°, not an exact measured angle.

Complete text specification for either drawing: one N–O single bond, one N=O double bond, one N lone pair, three lone pairs on single-bonded O, two on double-bonded O, formal −1 on single-bonded O, whole-ion brackets and overall − charge. A second contributor exchanges the O bond roles.

Hints:
1. What does the negative charge do to the electron budget?
2. Count attached atoms and lone pairs, not lines.
3. Two bonded atoms plus one lone pair means three domains, but only two atom positions to name.

Targeted hints for each card:
- SiF₄: H1 Count four F atoms. H2 Four single bonds use eight electrons. H3 After four F octets, the central atom has no lone pair; count four domains.
- AlCl₃: H1 Check Q10 before forcing an octet. H2 The neutral budget is 3 + 3(7). H3 Use three single bonds, no Al lone pair, and three domains in the monomer model. Real AlCl₃ can associate into dimers; that is outside this card's scope.
- CO₂: H1 Carbon needs an octet without extra electrons. H2 Convert O lone pairs into bonds. H3 Use two double bonds and count two regions around C.
- SO₃: H1 The budget is 6 + 3(6). H2 Decide whether your class preserves S's octet or uses an expanded-valence drawing. H3 Either discussed convention has three bonding domains and no central lone pair; the geometry result is the same.
- CF₂Cl₂: H1 The central atom is C; there are four terminal halogens. H2 F and Cl each contribute seven. H3 Four single bonds and no central lone pair give four domains; identical geometry does not guarantee identical measured angles.
- NO₂⁻: H1 Include the extra electron. H2 Keep one lone pair on N and use resonance contributors. H3 There are three domains but only two attached atoms; name electron geometry before shape.

For SO₃, do not assume the software or answer key can know your instructor's preferred drawing. A common octet-preserving treatment uses three equivalent contributors, each with one S=O and two S–O bonds; S has formal +2 and two O atoms have −1. A common expanded-octet drawing uses three S=O bonds with zero formal charges. Both preserve the 24-electron budget and yield trigonal planar geometry. Follow the explicitly chosen classroom convention for a required drawing.

### Drawing self assessment

For each drawing, mark yes, revise, or uncertain:
- I used the correct number of atoms and preserved connectivity.
- My drawn electrons match the valence budget exactly, including charge adjustment.
- Each bond line counts as two electrons in that budget; all lone pairs are shown.
- H has a duet; applicable atoms have octets; any exception is identified rather than hidden.
- Formal charges sum to the species charge; a polyatomic ion has brackets and its overall charge.
- I included equivalent resonance contributors when requested and did not move atoms between them.
- I counted central regions, with multiple bonds counted once.
- I named electron geometry and molecular shape separately.
- My angle statement distinguishes an ideal prediction from an actual measurement.

Do not auto-grade hand drawings from a filename, an uploaded image's existence, or an unverified recognition result. Compare to the structural checklist and explain any uncertainty. A rotated or reflected correct drawing is still correct. Dot positions can vary when lone-pair counts and connectivity are preserved.

### Feedback that helps you revise

- Budget off by two for NO₂⁻: “Check the charge sign before changing bonds. A negative ion gained an electron. Show the adjustment explicitly.”
- Correct total, missing Cl dots: “Your count is right, but the drawing does not yet spend the full budget. Show the nonbonding electrons on each terminal Cl.”
- Right bonds, missing central lone pair: “Subtract bonds and outer lone pairs from the total. Where must the remaining pair go?”
- CO₂ called tetrahedral: “You counted lines. VSEPR counts directions to neighbors; merge the two lines of each double bond into one region.”
- NO₂⁻ shape called trigonal planar: “That names all three electron regions. Now name the positions occupied by the two O atoms.”
- SO₃ alternative convention: “Your drawing needs a convention check, not an automatic wrong mark. Audit electrons and charges, then compare with your class model.”
- Correct shape from an incorrect drawing: “The final word may match, but repair the electron ledger before treating the reasoning as secure.”
