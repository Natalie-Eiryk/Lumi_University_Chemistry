## Teaching contract

Warm, first-principles, adult-to-adult tutoring. Suggested opening: “Before we name it, let’s decide what kind of thing we’re looking at. Then we’ll give each symbol one job.” Honor the learner's physics-teaching background without assuming chemistry vocabulary is already automatic. Nursing applications can be brief optional context, never medication/dosing exercises.

The default experience should ask for a prediction and a reason, offer one small hint, invite revision, then reveal an explanation. Never reveal all worksheet answers at once by default. Keep the worksheet's numbering visible so the user can move between guide and paper.

A self-correction row contains: item; my first answer; the rule I used; checkpoint that failed; my revision; one-sentence explanation; confidence before/after. Error labels: species type, charge sign, ion recall, charge balance, parentheses, naming system, electron budget, Lewis structure, domain count, shape vs electron geometry.

## Recommended guide architecture

### 0. “What am I looking at?” warm-up (supplemental prerequisite)

A six-item untimed diagnostic: N, N³−, NH₄+, (NH₄)₂CO₃, CO₂, N₂. Ask neutral or charged, one element or several, then species category. Use the confirmed course legend: E = element, I = ion, IC = ionic compound, CC = covalent compound. Natalie explicitly supplied these meanings in an earlier worksheet discussion. This is supplemental continuity from that discussion; these abbreviations do not occur in the uploaded PDF.

Important distinctions: N₂ is elemental even though its atoms are covalently bonded; NH₄+ is a polyatomic ion; (NH₄)₂CO₃ is an ionic compound containing covalent bonds within its ions. A visible charge wins the initial “ion” branch even when multiple elements appear. A neutral formula alone does not establish ionic versus molecular bonding with certainty; the introductory composition heuristic has exceptions.

### 1. “Charge is an electron ledger” (Q1–4)

Mini-lesson: protons remain unchanged in ordinary ion formation; net charge in elementary-charge units = protons minus electrons. Losing negative electrons makes a cation; gaining them makes an anion. Ionic bonding is electrostatic attraction between opposite charges, not the transfer step itself.

Interactions: choose gain/loss, set electron count, write the resulting ion; then explain the sign. For Q2 accept both a clearly labeled ion and a balanced formation equation if that is how the instructor teaches “symbols.” Provide N + 3e− → N³− and Li → Li+ + e− as notation models after a first attempt. Do not infer titanium's “usual” charge from this question; it specifically says two electrons lost.

### 2. “Learn ions as named building blocks” (Q5–6)

Separate name→formula and formula→name retrieval. Show monatomic naming changes (-ide) and variable-charge metals with Roman numerals. Roman numeral = metal charge/oxidation state, not atom count. Fixed-charge names need no Roman numeral in the course's introductory convention.

Reference drawer, initially closed during recall: ammonium NH₄+; carbonate CO₃²−; cyanide CN−; sulfate SO₄²−; hydroxide OH−; acetate C₂H₃O₂− or CH₃COO−; hypochlorite ClO−; bicarbonate/hydrogen carbonate HCO₃−; sulfite SO₃²−; permanganate MnO₄−; nitrate NO₃−; phosphate PO₄³−. Preserve the ion's internal subscripts. Teach sulfate/sulfite as a paired pattern, not a universal rule for guessing unfamiliar ion charges.

### 3. “Build neutral formulas, then name them” (Q7–8)

Use the worksheet's existing 4×4 grid as the central self-correction activity. Reveal one row at a time. Before formula entry, require the two ion charges and the smallest total positive/negative charge that balances. Then ask for the smallest whole-number ion ratio, formula, and name.

Feedback must distinguish: wrong ratio, unreduced ratio, lost ion identity, missing parentheses, wrong Roman numeral. A charge-balance check is necessary but must also check that polyatomic-ion identities remain intact. Never reduce the internal subscripts of a polyatomic ion.

### 4. “Choose a naming system before naming” (Q9, Q15–16)

Branch question first: introductory ionic naming or molecular-prefix naming? Use a two-column rule card: ionic name = cation then anion, with Roman numeral when needed; molecular name = prefixes describe atom counts, second element ends -ide, usually omit mono- on the first. Molecular formulas are not reduced to empirical ratios.

Q9 expected introductory prediction: C/F and Si/F covalent; the other pairs ionic. Do not teach “every metalloid pair is always covalent.” Q15 intentionally mixes the systems (NaH and AlF₃ among molecular examples). NaH contains hydride, H−. OsO₄ warrants a marked exception note: osmium tetroxide is a molecular oxide with a metal; osmium(VIII) oxide is also a recognized name. Do not mark a chemically valid name wrong just because a simplistic metal/nonmetal rule was applied. Course-preferred name remains unverified.

### 5. “Spend every valence electron once” (Q10–13)

One reusable ledger: sum valence electrons; adjust for charge; choose skeleton; add single bonds; complete outer shells (H gets 2); place remaining electrons centrally; add multiple bonds if needed; audit total electrons and reasonable formal charges. A line = two shared electrons = one shared pair. A double bond is two shared pairs but only one VSEPR domain.

Q11–13 reusable set: NCl₃ (26 total e−; 3 shared pairs; central N one lone pair), HI (8; 1; I three lone pairs), CHCl₃ (26; 4; C no lone pairs), PBr₃ (26; 3; P one lone pair). Terminal halogens in these examples each have three lone pairs. Use answers behind individual checks, not as a front-page table.

Q10 is an instructor-convention checkpoint, not an auto-graded exact-four recall question. Common examples include H (duet), Be (electron-deficient, often 4 around it), B (often 6), and Al (often 6 in a monomer Lewis model); P and S are also standard examples of expanded-valence Lewis structures. Which four the course expects cannot be recovered from the sheet. Avoid claiming these atoms always violate the octet or explaining hypervalency as compulsory d-orbital hybridization.

### 6. “Electron geometry first; molecular shape second” (Q14)

Interpret the sheet's “orbital geometry” provisionally as electron-domain geometry; show both terms. Count every bond, single/double/triple, as one domain; each central lone pair as one. Electron geometry includes lone pairs; molecular geometry names atomic positions.

Reference outcomes for checker (not all visible initially):
- SiF₄: 32 valence e−; 4 bonding domains, 0 lone pairs; tetrahedral/tetrahedral; ideal 109.5°
- AlCl₃: 24; 3 domains, 0 central lone pairs in monomer model; trigonal planar/trigonal planar; 120°; incomplete Al octet. Real AlCl₃ can dimerize, outside this worksheet model
- CO₂: 16; two double bonds, 2 domains, 0 central lone pairs; linear/linear; 180°
- SO₃: 24; 3 domains, 0 central lone pairs; trigonal planar/trigonal planar; 120°. Octet-preserving resonance vs expanded-octet Lewis conventions should be explained and aligned with class expectations; geometry is unchanged
- CF₂Cl₂: 32; 4 bonding domains, 0 central lone pairs; tetrahedral/tetrahedral; idealized about 109.5°, not a guarantee every measured bond angle is exactly equal
- NO₂−: 18; 2 bonding domains plus 1 central lone pair; trigonal planar/bent; less than 120°. Include brackets, overall − charge, formal charges, and two equivalent resonance contributors

Angles are a learner-requested reinforcement, not explicitly asked on the source sheet. Treat exact measured angles as out of scope.

### 7. Mixed retrieval and return loop

Offer short adjustable sessions rather than promise completion in a fixed time. Sequence: warm-up → one weak-area lesson → two guided examples → four independent items → correction grid → one delayed retry. Re-test the mistaken rule using a new species before returning to the original item. End with “What changed in your reasoning?” and an honest confidence rating. No speed score or punitive streak loss.

## Three worked examples, staged for learner reveal

### A. A polyatomic ion becomes part of an ionic compound (Q7l/Q8l)

**Prompt:** Combine ammonium and carbonate. Name the product and explain every number.
1. NH₄+ has charge +1; CO₃²− has charge −2. Both are ions, despite containing several atoms.
2. Two ammonium ions contribute +2; one carbonate contributes −2. Total 0.
3. Preserve each ion intact: (NH₄)₂CO₃.
4. Name: ammonium carbonate. No molecular prefixes are needed.
5. The outside 2 counts whole ammonium ions; the 4 is H atoms in one ammonium; the 3 is O atoms in one carbonate. Whole-formula atom inventory: N₂H₈CO₃, but write the ionic formula with the ions recognizable.
**Hints:** What is the charge of each whole group? → How many +1 groups balance −2? → Parentheses keep the ammonium group together.
**Transfer:** Cu²+ with PO₄³−. Ask learner to explain why the neutral formula is Cu₃(PO₄)₂ and why the name uses copper(II), not copper(III).

### B. Same number, different job: charges versus subscripts

**Prompt:** Compare Fe³+ and FeCl₃; classify and name each.
1. Fe³+ is one iron ion with charge +3: iron(III) ion. The superscript is net electrical charge, not three Fe atoms.
2. FeCl₃ is neutral overall. Three chloride ions at −1 each require iron at +3.
3. Name FeCl₃: iron(III) chloride. The subscript 3 counts chlorides per formula unit; the Roman numeral III identifies iron's charge.
4. Compare with PCl₃ (supplemental): phosphorus trichloride; tri- counts three chlorine atoms in a molecule. Different naming route, same visual subscript position.
**Hints:** Is there an overall written charge? → What charge does each chloride carry? → The numeral in a metal's name describes which property?
**Transfer:** Original Q15c NI₃ vs Q15d NaH. Ask for naming system before the name.

### C. Charge changes electron budget; lone pairs change shape (Q14f)

**Prompt:** Draw NO₂− and distinguish its electron geometry from molecular shape.
1. Electron budget: N gives 5; two O give 12; the −1 charge adds 1. Total 18.
2. Start O–N–O, using 4 electrons. Complete the outer O octets using 12 more, leaving 2 on N as one lone pair.
3. N initially has only 6 electrons around it. Convert one O lone pair into a second bond: O=N–O−, with one lone pair on N. The double-bonded O has two lone pairs; the single-bonded O has three.
4. Audit: bonds use 6; O lone pairs use 10; N lone pair uses 2; total 18. Formal charges: N 0, double-bonded O 0, single-bonded O −1. Put the entire ion in brackets with − outside.
5. The other equivalent contributor swaps which O has the double bond. The actual ion is a resonance hybrid, not a molecule flipping between two arrangements.
6. At N: two bonding regions and one lone pair = three electron domains. Electron geometry: trigonal planar. Molecular shape: bent. Predicted O–N–O angle less than 120°, not 109.5°.
**Hints:** Does negative charge add or subtract an electron? → Count domains, not bond lines → Does the shape name include invisible lone-pair positions?
**Transfer:** CO₂ also has two attached O atoms but no central lone pair; explain why it is linear instead.

**Visual Lewis example for a monospaced reveal:** A colon or a pair of dots is one lone pair. Here is one complete resonance contributor, with the single-bonded O formal charge omitted inside the brackets because the overall ion charge is already shown:

```text
       ..      ..
  [    O = N – O :    ]⁻
       ..  ..  ..
```

The double-bonded O has two lone pairs (above and below); N has one lone pair (below); the single-bonded O has three lone pairs (above, right, and below). Audit: five O lone pairs + one N lone pair = 12 electrons; the double bond and single bond contribute 6 more, totaling 18. The second equivalent contributor swaps which oxygen is double-bonded. The flat Lewis drawing shows connectivity, not the actual bent shape.


## Answer/checker acceptance rules

- Accept equivalent plain-text and superscript/subscript notation (e.g., NO2^- or NO₂−); display normalized scientific notation after interpretation
- Accept charge sign conventions +1/1+ only if unambiguous; do not confuse S²− with S₂
- Accept acetate C₂H₃O₂− and CH₃COO−; bicarbonate and hydrogen carbonate
- Recognize Roman-numeral spacing/capitalization variants; explain meaning, not cosmetic penalties
- Avoid exact-text auto-grading for definitions and drawings; expose checklist-based self-assessment if drawing recognition is unavailable
- For Q10, SO₃ Lewis conventions, and course-specific OsO₄ nomenclature, flag instructor alignment rather than pretend the sheet contains an answer key
- Define “shared pairs” globally as bonding electron pairs across the molecule; for Q12 all bonds are single, so counting bonds and shared pairs agrees
- Geometry grader must separate electron geometry and molecular shape; never mark NO₂− “trigonal planar shape” as fully correct
