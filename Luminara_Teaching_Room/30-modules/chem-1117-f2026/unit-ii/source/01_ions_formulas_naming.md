## Station 0 Read the species before the name

Ms. Luminara: “A formula is a compact description. Before you calculate anything, tell me what its marks actually say.”

A chemical symbol identifies an element. A subscript counts atoms immediately before it, or repeats a whole group if it follows parentheses. A superscript reports the net charge of the written species. A missing subscript means one. These are different quantities: S₂ describes two sulfur atoms; S²− describes one sulfur ion with net charge −2.

Use the categories at the level of the entire written species:

- E means element: only one kind of element is present and the species is neutral. An elemental substance can contain molecules such as O₂
- I means ion: a single atom or bonded group carries a net charge. Multiple element symbols do not stop a charged group from being an ion
- IC means ionic compound: cations and anions occur in a neutral overall ratio
- CC means covalent compound: different elements are joined by covalent bonding in the course’s introductory classification

First look for an overall charge. If there is one, use I. If the species is neutral and contains just one element, use E. If it is neutral and contains several elements, investigate its bonding family. A familiar metal cation with an anion usually signals IC. Ammonium salts are IC even without a metal. Nonmetal combinations usually signal CC. The last step is a useful introductory heuristic, not a theorem that a formula alone proves.

Do not confuse a bond with a category of substance. A polyatomic ion contains covalent bonds internally and can still be the charged building block of an ionic compound.

Before moving on, say aloud: “I identified the whole species by ___, and the subscript tells me ___.” Complete NEW-S0-01 through NEW-S0-07 below.

## Station 1 Make charge an electron ledger

Ms. Luminara: “Let’s let the arithmetic carry the sign. You don’t need to memorize that ‘losing’ somehow means ‘positive’ if you keep track of what was lost.”

Each proton contributes +1 elementary charge; each electron contributes −1. Therefore, net charge in units of e is the proton count minus the electron count. A neutral atom has equal counts. Ordinary ion formation changes electrons, while the nucleus and element identity remain unchanged. Losing electrons removes negative charge, producing a cation. Gaining electrons adds negative charge, producing an anion.

In an ion-formation equation, atoms and total charge must balance. Electron gain places electrons with the reactants; electron loss places them with the products. A superscript labels the resulting charge. Do not use a subscript to show gained electrons.

There are three ideas to keep distinct: ions are charged species; ionic bonding is attraction between opposite charges; an ionic compound has enough of each charge to be neutral overall. Electron transfer is one route to forming ions. The attraction between the resulting ions is the ionic bond, rather than the transfer event itself.

For this worksheet’s common main-group ions, group 1 commonly forms +1, group 2 forms +2, aluminum forms +3, group 15 nonmetals commonly form −3, group 16 forms −2, and group 17 forms −1. Use this as the introductory monatomic-ion pattern. It is not a rule that predicts every oxidation state in every substance. When the prompt specifies an electron change, follow that change: “titanium loses two” is sufficient information even if you have seen other titanium charges elsewhere.

Prediction before practice: If an atom loses two electrons, should its charge move two units toward positive or negative? Explain using the sign of an electron. Complete Q1–4, then NEW-S1-01 and NEW-S1-02.

### Worked example to reveal after a first attempt

**Prompt:** A neutral atom has 20 protons and 20 electrons. It loses two electrons. What changes, and how would you test the sign?

**Reveal:** The proton count stays 20; the electron count becomes 18. Net charge is 20 − 18 = +2. Because the element with 20 protons is calcium, the ion is Ca²+. The formation equation Ca → Ca²+ + 2e− conserves charge: the left is 0 and the right is +2 − 2 = 0. If you initially wrote −2, preserve the correct magnitude and repair the sign reasoning. You removed negative charges.

**Transfer:** Return to Q2c. Use the stated electron loss, not calcium’s identity, to decide the titanium charge.

## Station 2 Retrieve ions as whole named units

Ms. Luminara: “Some of this is vocabulary. We can make it organized vocabulary rather than one long list of unrelated facts.”

A monatomic positive ion usually keeps the element name. For a variable-charge metal, a Roman numeral specifies the charge of that ion: iron(II) and iron(III) are different cations. The numeral is not the number of metal atoms. Introductory fixed-charge names such as sodium, calcium, aluminum, magnesium, strontium, and silver usually do not need a numeral. A monatomic negative ion uses the element’s stem plus -ide, as chlorine becomes chloride.

A polyatomic ion has an internal atom arrangement and a net charge. Recall both together. The internal subscripts belong to that ion’s identity and should survive intact when you build a salt. A charge applies to the whole group, not to the final atom alone.

Try recall in both directions: name → charged formula, then charged formula → name. Cover the reference drawer below on your first attempt. If you need it, use it deliberately, then close it and reconstruct the formula from memory. Recognizing a formula while looking at it is different from being able to retrieve it.

For oxyanion pairs already learned, a pattern can help: sulfate has one more oxygen than sulfite, and both have charge −2. Do not use that pair to invent the charge of an unfamiliar oxyanion. “-ide” alone also does not guarantee one atom: cyanide and hydroxide are established polyatomic-ion names.

## Station 3 Build a neutral formula and audit it

Ms. Luminara: “The formula is a compact record of a balancing argument. We’ll make that argument first, and let the subscripts record the result.”

Start with each whole ion and its charge. Find the smallest total positive and negative charge magnitudes that can cancel. That tells you the number of each ion needed. Write the cation first, then the anion. Use parentheses around a polyatomic ion when more than one copy is present. Do not write outside subscript 1. A neutral compound formula ordinarily has no overall charge label.

For charges +a and −b, look for small positive integers n and m such that n(a) = m(b). The least common multiple of a and b is a convenient target, but you can also reason by small multiples. This is why the familiar “cross the charges” shortcut sometimes works. The underlying requirement is neutrality plus the simplest ratio of intact ions; blindly crossing numbers can obscure both requirements.

Audit the result in this order:

1. Do the total positive and negative charges add to zero?
2. Is the ratio of whole ions in lowest terms?
3. Are all polyatomic ions still intact?
4. Do parentheses repeat the intended whole group?
5. Does the name identify each ion correctly?

Name an ionic compound by cation then anion. If the metal can have different charges, use the charge of each metal ion as its Roman numeral. The subscript counting metal ions is not that charge. Do not add molecular prefixes to this naming system.

### Worked example to reveal after attempting Q7l

**Prompt:** Combine ammonium and carbonate. Name the result and explain each number.

**Reveal:** NH₄+ contributes +1 per whole ammonium ion; CO₃²− contributes −2 per whole carbonate ion. Two ammonium ions provide +2, balancing one carbonate at −2. Write (NH₄)₂CO₃, ammonium carbonate. The outside 2 repeats NH₄, so there are two nitrogen atoms and eight hydrogen atoms per formula unit. The internal 4 and 3 preserve the identities of ammonium and carbonate. This is an ionic compound even though both of its ions contain covalent bonds and neither is a metal ion.

**Self-correction check:** NH₄CO₃ gives +1 − 2 = −1, so its 1:1 ion ratio does not make the intended neutral salt. NH₄₂CO₃ does not correctly express two ammonium groups. Parentheses are doing mathematical work here.

## Station 4 Choose the naming system before naming

Ms. Luminara: “The hard part is often choosing which rule applies. Once we choose the family, the arithmetic and vocabulary become much more predictable.”

Ask whether the formula fits familiar ionic building blocks or a molecular compound. The source deliberately mixes examples. NaH uses hydride, H−, with sodium; AlF₃ uses aluminum and fluoride. Those take ionic names even though neighboring questions use molecular prefixes.

In binary molecular naming, the first element keeps its name and gets a count prefix when its count is greater than one. The second element gets a count prefix and an -ide ending. Common count prefixes are mono 1, di 2, tri 3, tetra 4, penta 5, and hexa 6. Conventional vowel contraction gives monoxide and tetroxide. The repeated i in triiodide is retained.

Molecular prefixes tell you the actual number of atoms in the named molecule. Do not reduce the molecular formula to a simpler ratio. In contrast, the introductory ionic formula records the smallest neutral ratio of ions. A habit that repairs an unreduced ionic formula can damage a correct molecular formula.

For Q9, use periodic-table position to make an introductory prediction without numerical electronegativities. Metal/nonmetal pairs usually fit the ionic model. Carbon with fluorine and silicon with fluorine are covalent examples here. Do not turn this into a universal rule for every metalloid or every metal-containing compound.

**OsO₄ convention checkpoint:** Osmium tetroxide is a molecular metal oxide. “Osmium tetroxide” and “osmium(VIII) oxide” are recognized names. A checker must not reject a valid name just because “metal plus nonmetal” was used as an absolute rule. In the Stock name, VIII is an oxidation state; it does not assert that the molecule contains a free Os⁸+ ion. The instructor’s preferred naming convention is not specified on the worksheet. [PubChem compound identity](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/30318)

Complete Q9, Q15, and Q16. For Q16, translate the stated names; the exercise is not asking you to prove that every named substance is stable or readily prepared.

### Worked example to reveal after a first attempt

**Prompt:** Compare FeCl₃ with PCl₃. What job does the 3 do in each name?

**Reveal:** In FeCl₃, three chloride ions contribute −3. One iron must therefore be +3, giving iron(III) chloride. In PCl₃, the molecular prefix tri- reports three chlorine atoms, giving phosphorus trichloride. The visual subscript counts chlorine in both formulas, but the naming systems encode different information. The Roman numeral reports the metal’s charge; the prefix reports an atom count.

## Self correction feedback to reveal after an attempt

Ms. Luminara: “Keep the part that worked. Name the exact checkpoint that failed, then change only what needs changing.”

| Pattern in an attempt | Diagnostic response | Next action |
|---|---|---|
| A charged group is called CC | You noticed several elements. Now check whether the whole species is neutral | Classify the whole written species first |
| N₂ is called a compound | You noticed covalent bonding, but a compound requires different elements | Count kinds of elements separately from atoms |
| Electron loss gives a negative ion | The electron-change magnitude may be right; the sign reasoning is reversed | Recompute protons minus electrons |
| SO₄ becomes SO₃ while balancing a salt | The ion identity changed during the balancing step | Restore the known ion and change only whole-ion counts |
| A formula balances but all ion counts share a factor | Neutrality passed; the simplest-ratio checkpoint did not | Reduce the whole-ion counts |
| A repeating polyatomic group lacks parentheses | The count no longer applies to the intended entire unit | Put the repeated ion in parentheses |
| Cu₃ is used to choose copper(III) | The subscript counts cations, not charge per cation | Calculate total anion charge and divide among the metal ions |
| A neutral ionic formula retains ion charge labels | The ions were identified correctly, but the compound is neutral | Remove the overall charge label after checking the ratio |
| N₂O₄ becomes NO₂ | A valid ionic simplification habit was used in the wrong naming family | Preserve the molecular counts specified by prefixes |
| OsO₄ is rejected solely because it contains a metal | The composition heuristic was treated as exceptionless | Recognize the valid molecular or Stock name and flag course preference |

## Naming and notation acceptance

Accept equivalent unambiguous plain-text notation and Unicode notation: SO4^2- and SO₄²− can represent the same ion. Preserve element-symbol case: Co and CO are different. If a notation style is ambiguous, ask the learner what they meant before diagnosing a chemistry misconception. Ion names may include or omit the word “ion.” Definitions are evaluated by their concepts, not exact phrasing.

Acetate may be C₂H₃O₂− or CH₃COO−. Hydrogen carbonate and bicarbonate name the same ion. A correct established alternate name should be recognized, with the course’s preferred practice form explained. In Q15j, nitric oxide is a recognized common name; nitrogen monoxide demonstrates the prefix rule. Ordinary aluminum/aluminium spelling variations are acceptable.

For fixed-charge cations, coach the customary name without an unnecessary Roman numeral rather than treating that addition as a new chemistry error. For variable-charge metals, a missing numeral leaves the identity incomplete in the requested Stock convention. OsO₄ requires the explicit exception described above.
