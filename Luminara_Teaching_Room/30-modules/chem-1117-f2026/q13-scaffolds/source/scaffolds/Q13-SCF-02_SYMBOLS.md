# Read the badge before naming the passenger

**Scaffold ID:** Q13-SCF-02  
**Primary anchors:** CR-307, CR-308  
**Related:** CR-315, CR-316, PR-72; MAT-01, MAT-12, MAT-13  
**Time:** about 5 minutes  
**Model change:** “The English word that first comes to mind can guide the formula” → “The exact chemical symbol fixes element identity before naming.”

## 1. Teaching scene — a ticket for the wrong element

The bus stopped at a turnstile. A luminous **P** waited beside three **Br** badges.

**Natalie:** “Potassium bromide?”

Nothing exploded. The turnstile simply presented a periodic-table card.

**Henry:** “Read just the first badge. No compound yet.”

Natalie studied the card.

**Natalie:** “P is phosphorus. Potassium is K. I let the word I was expecting replace the symbol on the page.”

**Ms. Luminara:** “That is a different repair from changing a bonding rule. Start where the identity changed.”

She replaced P with K. The card now read **KBr**.

**Jackson:** “Now potassium bromide is appropriate.”

**Ms. Luminara:** “For that different formula. We are not renaming P into K; we have changed the element.”

Kàra pushed the original PBr₃ card beneath a scanner. It separated into a token list, not into free ions:

**P: one phosphorus atom. Br: three bromine atoms.**

**Kàra:** “Does the three mean phosphorus has a charge of three?”

**Ms. Luminara:** “Not from this notation. It is a subscript attached to Br, so it counts bromine atoms.”

**Natalie:** “Then this uses the molecular naming route: phosphorus tribromide.”

**Carlos:** “One mistaken badge, an entirely different passenger.”

**Jeff:** “Please tell me the driver has been checking ours.”

Ms. Luminara looked thoughtfully at the bus registration. Jeff leaned over to check it himself.

### Slow Turn

**Natalie:** “When I wrote a metal’s name, I also made ionic naming feel plausible. The second error may have followed the first.”

**Ms. Luminara:** “It may have. Or the type could have been a separate selection slip. We check the first badge, then ask the next question. We do not diagnose a whole mind from one line.”

## 2. The symbol-first routine

1. Copy the formula exactly, preserving case and subscripts.
2. Separate element tokens: an uppercase letter begins an element symbol; a following lowercase letter belongs to that symbol.
3. Say the element names before choosing the naming route.
4. Read each subscript as an atom count, not an ion charge.

For this example:

| Formula token | Element | Count |
|---|---|---:|
| P | phosphorus | 1 |
| Br | bromine | 3 |

Thus **PBr₃ → phosphorus tribromide**, covalent in this activity.

P and K are not interchangeable abbreviations. Likewise, CO and Co are not equal strings in chemistry. The app must not silently lowercase formulas or “fix” ambiguous handwriting into a different valid formula.

## 3. Multimodal passes

**Visual:** tap P to open the existing phosphorus card and K to open potassium. Keep the formula visible; do not send the reader to a different route and lose their place.

**Spoken:** “P, one; Br, three. Phosphorus, one; bromine, three.” Text must remain available; audio is optional.

**Written:** cover the names and write the symbol of each element. Do not require a time limit.

**Transfer:** compare the existing nitrogen tribromide item, NBr₃, with PBr₃. Keep Br₃ constant and change only the first element. This reveals what transfers without introducing a new ion-charge puzzle.

## 4. Diagnostic boundary

Ask “Which element is P?” before exposing the full answer. If the learner knows it, do not force a symbol lesson. Continue to type selection or prefix use. If the input is visually uncertain, ask for confirmation rather than assigning a symbol-confusion diagnosis.

## 5. Codex hook

**Offer when:** the formula contains P but a typed name resolves to potassium, or the learner requests a symbol reminder. This should be driven by a safe token/name comparison, not by fuzzy guessing.  
**Preserve:** the original input; show the proposed interpretation for confirmation.  
**Return:** CR-307 first; CR-308 remains its own classification field.
