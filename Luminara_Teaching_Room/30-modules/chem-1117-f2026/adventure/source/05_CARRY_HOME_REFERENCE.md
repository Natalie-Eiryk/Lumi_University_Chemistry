# Carry home — a small map, not another ion table

Companion to [The Loom of Chemical Meaning](01_THE_ADVENTURE.md). These are recall cues for the narrative, not new assessment questions. The existing reference bank remains the owner of the full ion vocabulary.

## First identify the object

An atom, a molecule, a polyatomic ion, a neutral salt, a sample, and a population are different boundaries. An answer that is right for one boundary need not answer a question about another.

## Three numbers on a formula

**2 NO₃⁻** means two complete nitrate ions. Each contains one N and three O atoms and has charge −1. Together: two N, six O, total charge −2.

- Subscript: atoms within a formula/group.
- Superscript: net charge of the indicated entity.
- Coefficient: number of complete copies.

## Where charge comes from

**Charge number = total protons − total electrons.**

Electron removal makes a chosen object more positive. Electron addition makes it more negative. Charge can also change by transferring a charged constituent across its boundary.

**NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺** is the central example. Neutral NH3 starts with ten protons and ten total electrons. H+ adds a proton, no electron. NH4+ therefore has eleven protons and ten total electrons: net +1. Its valence-electron ledger has eight electrons, not ten. The lone pair becomes a bonding pair; sharing does not annihilate net charge.

Nitrate and carbonate have established reference identities. Their atom counts alone do not derive arbitrary charge states. Given NO3^- and CO3^2-, count 31p/32e and 30p/32e respectively to explain the specified charges.

## Identify first; balance second

For a **neutral compound**, add whole-ion contributions until their sum is zero. Change the number of ions, not their internal identities.

**Al³⁺ and SO₄²⁻:** two aluminums total +6; three sulfates total −6 → **Al₂(SO₄)₃**.

**Ca²⁺ and NO₃⁻:** one calcium, two nitrates → **Ca(NO₃)₂**.

**Na⁺ and O₂²⁻:** two sodium ions, one peroxide → **Na₂O₂**, not NaO.

Neutrality does not prove a stable compound exists in every imagined ion combination. This is a naming/composition method with defined inputs.

## Read backward

For **Fe₂O₃**, known oxide ions contribute 3(−2) = −6. The two iron ions total +6, so each is +3: **iron(III) oxide**.

The Roman numeral is an oxidation-state label; in the simple monatomic-cation model it gives the per-ion charge. It is not the metal subscript. Missing charge information or a noninteger result under a single-charge assumption is a reason to clarify, not guess or round.

## Choose the naming route

**Recognized ionic constituents:** name the cation then anion; use charge balance for ratios and a Roman numeral when required.

**Binary molecular-prefix exercise:** read/preserve atom counts; first-element mono- is usually omitted; second element gets its prefix and -ide form. N2O4 is dinitrogen tetroxide, not reducible to NO2 in that task.

**NH₄Cl:** ionic naming between NH4+ and Cl^-; covalent N–H bonds inside NH4+.

**LiH:** lithium hydride, represented as Li+ and H^- in the solid; this is a specific contextual example, not a rule for all hydrogen compounds.

**BCl₃/BF₃:** molecular route in the supplied activities. Network solids, elements, acids, and unknown inputs can require other context.

## Suffixes compress families; they do not replace reference identities

Within the supported pairs: nitrate NO3^- / nitrite NO2^-; sulfate SO4^2- / sulfite SO3^2-.

The chlorine sequence is ClO^- hypochlorite, ClO2^- chlorite, ClO3^- chlorate, ClO4^- perchlorate; charge stays −1 in this sequence.

The ending -ate alone gives neither a universal oxygen count nor a charge. The ending -ide does not guarantee one atom: hydroxide, cyanide, and peroxide are counterexamples.

## Carry the earlier foundations too

**Weighted mean:** add all individual masses, divide by the atom count, then group repeated values. Population fractions are counts divided by total population.

**Valence:** for main-group Lewis counts, include the highest-shell s and p electrons, not only the last written subshell. Do not apply this as a complete transition-metal theory.

**Measurement:** a quantity includes a unit; a report includes a precision choice. Exact defined conversion factors and measured material properties have different evidentiary roles.

**Lab reasoning:** say what was observed, what was inferred, and what remains a prediction. Do not make a filled table stand in for an unperformed experiment.

## When uncertain, name the missing piece

“Do I not recognize the ion?”

“Do I recognize its formula but not its charge?”

“Did I balance ions but write the grouping unclearly?”

“Am I applying ionic charge balancing to a molecular-prefix task?”

“Do I need a different model or an unresolved source?”

Those questions locate a repair. They are more useful than deciding the whole topic makes no sense.
