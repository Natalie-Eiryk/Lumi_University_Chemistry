## What the learner is asking for

Natalie wants to understand *why* an answer follows, especially when telling ionic compounds from molecular covalent compounds and choosing an ion's charge. Recognizing terms and eliminating unsuitable choices helps the material stick. Matching and dropdown choices are useful first encounters; writing an answer from memory is a useful later option. A periodic table and an explanatory story can remain beside the questions in movable study windows.

Recent example: she recognized ammonium's formula, NH₄, but did not know why its charge is +1. A message saying “compare with the expected charge” did not supply the missing explanation. Similar needs occur with carbonate, variable metal charges, and choosing which naming rule applies.

## A connected content sequence

This order connects the existing material from physical meaning to naming and then back to explanation. The section codes below are inventory labels for organizing returned stories, not new assessment identifiers.

### MAT-01 — Matter, particles and the meaning of a chemical symbol

**Already available:** The early seven-page workbook material and six lessons: “Something can be there without being seen”; “One table, two questions”; “An attribute is not yet an event”; “What changed—and what stayed itself?”; “Do not jump to the leaves”; “A margin is allowed to hold a question.” The later route is “The particles keep their story.” Chapter 1 includes 72 numbered problems across several introductory topics.

**Content:** Matter; solid, liquid and gas; pure elements, pure compounds and mixtures; particle representations; physical versus chemical properties; physical versus chemical changes; observations versus interpretations. Letters, particle groups and connecting marks in a model have distinct meanings.

**Explanatory connections:** A visible change is not by itself proof of a new substance. A formula describes composition; a drawn group represents a model of particles. These distinctions prepare the difference between changing the number of ions and changing their identities.

**Existing exercise anchors:** Early workbook lesson IDs `matter`, `state`, `properties`, `changes`, `tree`, `questions`; workshop route `particles`; worksheet `source-07`.

### MAT-02 — Measurements, number size, significant figures and units

**Already available:** Q3 blank and annotated copies; significant-digit number record; two measurement records; Chapter 1 numerical work. Routes: “A number changes clothes” and “The unit returns to the measurement.” A number lens compares numerical values.

**Content:** Normalized scientific notation; positive and negative powers of ten; conversion between scientific and decimal notation; calculator results versus reported results; significant figures; ambiguous trailing zeros; units; reading a scale and reporting its last estimated digit. The measurement exercises use explicitly authored practice scales where the original instrument view is unavailable.

**Explanatory connections:** Equivalent numerical values do not necessarily communicate the same measurement precision. A unit is part of a quantity's meaning. A written number alone may not determine how many digits are justified.

**Existing exercise anchors:** `source-04` and `source-19`: 20 Q3 prompts each, comprising 8 scientific-notation conversions, 6 decimal conversions and 6 calculations; `source-06`: 10 significant-figure prompts; `source-17` and `source-18`: 9 measurement prompts each; routes `notation` and `measurement`.

### MAT-03 — Dimensional analysis: following a quantity through conversions

**Already available:** Two versions of a 20-problem fence-post assignment, Q4's 9 problems, the conversion-factor tool, and “Let the units show the route.”

**Content:** Given and requested quantities; choosing conversion factors; numerator/denominator orientation; cancellation of units; chained rates; area and volume conversions; distinguishing a correct setup from a plausible numerical answer. Practice includes fictional token chains and neutral material/sample contexts.

**Explanatory connections:** A conversion preserves the quantity while changing how it is expressed. Area and volume require the corresponding powers of a length conversion. This prepares charge ledgers, where multiplying a count by a charge also has a specific meaning.

**Existing exercise anchors:** `source-08`, `source-09`, `source-14`; route `fence`. These are study arithmetic, not clinical treatment instructions.

### MAT-04 — Atomic identity, isotopes and ions: three different changes

**Already available:** Q6 isotope worksheet, the weighted-abundance bench, “One element, a weighted crowd,” and periodic-table particle/isotope reference.

**Content:** Atomic number and proton count; mass number as protons plus neutrons; neutron count; neutral electron count; the meaning of an ion's signed charge; isotope notation; relative abundance and weighted average atomic mass.

**Explanatory connections:** Changing neutrons changes the isotope; changing electrons changes the charge; changing protons changes the element. An element's tabulated atomic weight is not the mass number of every atom of that element. A weighted mean uses the population fractions, not equal votes from every isotope name.

**Existing exercise anchors:** `source-10`: six isotope-table rows plus one weighted-average problem (33 response fields); route `isotopes`; periodic reference “Isotopes & particles.”

### MAT-05 — Electron organization and why the periodic table repeats

**Already available:** Q9 in three retained source versions, a 20-element electron-configuration tutorial, “Three views of one row,” periodicity notes, and periodic-table views for groups/valence and s/p/d/f.

**Content:** Shorthand electron configuration; orbital notation; Lewis symbols for atoms; valence electrons; groups and periods; shells, subshells and orbitals; s/p/d/f capacities and the relationship between those representations. Periodicity material contrasts first ionization energy, electronegativity and other property trends.

**Explanatory connections:** A group number is not always the number of valence electrons, and valence-electron count is not an ion's net charge. Main-group patterns are scoped shortcuts. Transition-metal configurations and exceptions require their own treatment. A general arrow on the table is not a universal rule for every physical property.

**Existing exercise anchors:** `source-05`, `source-11`, `source-16`: the same nine Q9 element rows, each asking for three representations; `source-12`: 20 tutorial rows; `source-15`: 10 authored review prompts, not ten original numbered questions; routes `electrons` and `periodicity`.

**Coverage boundary:** These topics exist in the broader chemistry workshop. The Ion Language course set is not a complete orbital-configuration assessment engine.

### MAT-06 — Charge, subscripts and coefficients: what each number describes

**Already available:** L01, “The charge ledger: three different numbers”; original concept prompts; nitrate-group demonstration; electron-change course set.

**Content:** A subscript counts atoms within a formula; a superscript records net charge; a coefficient counts complete copies. For example, NO₃⁻ contains one N and three O atoms with a total charge of −1. Two nitrate ions contain two N and six O atoms and total charge −2, while each ion remains nitrate.

**Explanatory connections:** A single ion need not be neutral. Neutrality is the target when combining ions into a neutral compound. Positive charge reflects fewer electrons than protons; negative charge reflects more. The electron-change set includes Li, Be, B, C, N, O, F, Ne, K, Ca, Ga, Ge, As, Se, Br and Kr; B/C/Ge reference rows do not receive a universal monatomic charge.

**Existing exercise anchors:** L01; `electron-changes` (16 rows, 39 fields); original PR concept questions; the nitrate-group reference.

### MAT-07 — Ion names, formulas and charges as connected identities

**Already available:** L02, “Name badges: what you can predict and what you must recall”; 64 ion entries; the matching board; `course-ions` with 32 rows and 64 fields.

**Content:** Cations and anions; monatomic and polyatomic ions; cation naming; common monatomic anion endings; selected main-group charge patterns; fixed-charge and variable-charge metals; the need to learn a polyatomic ion's name, formula and charge together.

**Explanatory connections:** The formula field and signed-charge field answer different questions. Recognizing CO₃ does not establish that its charge is known. A suffix does not uniquely determine charge. A periodic-table position can help with selected monatomic ions, but cannot generate an arbitrary polyatomic formula or charge.

**Existing exercise anchors:** L02, `course-ions`, both directions of the ion board, and the complete ion table in the companion index. Core contrasts include calcium/oxide, iron(II)/iron(III), copper(I)/copper(II), and monatomic versus polyatomic anions.

### MAT-08 — Why ammonium is +1; what a whole polyatomic charge means

**Already available:** Ammonium and other polyatomic cards; native field explanations; course ion rows; ammonium-salt examples. This is the most immediate explanatory need.

**Content:** NH₃ versus NH₄⁺; neutral ammonia plus H⁺ gives ammonium; the added proton does not bring an electron. NH₄⁺ contains 11 protons and 10 electrons overall. Its four hydrogens count atoms; they do not determine a charge of four. Its internal bonds are covalent while its net charge belongs to the complete group.

**Related material:** Hydronium, hydroxide, carbonate, hydrogen carbonate, nitrate, sulfate and phosphate. Their formulas and net charges are reference identities used by the worksheet. Electron counting or formal-charge bookkeeping can check a proposed structure; it does not mean every common ion can be predicted from its formula alone without chemical context.

**Explanatory connections:** Covalent bonds inside an ion do not make the ion neutral. A lone pair shared in forming a bond does not cancel the newly added positive charge. Formal charge is bookkeeping within a model, distinct from treating each atom as a free ion.

**Existing exercise anchors:** `course-ions`, ion board, WE-09 ammonium sulfate, and ammonium-containing rows of the naming material. A longer story can connect back to MAT-04–07 rather than introduce another rule to memorize.

### MAT-09 — Oxygen families and the limits of naming patterns

**Already available:** L05, “The oxygen family wall”; six oxygen-family matching rows; an ion-family reference and original comparison prompts.

**Content:** Nitrate/nitrite; sulfate/sulfite; chlorine's hypochlorite → chlorite → chlorate → perchlorate sequence; carbonate/hydrogen carbonate; oxide/peroxide; phosphate/phosphide; chromate/dichromate; permanganate as a separate anchor.

**Explanatory connections:** Within a known family, -ate has more oxygen than -ite. This does not establish a universal oxygen count or charge. The chlorine ladder retains −1 charge as oxygen counts change. The ending -ide does not guarantee a monatomic ion: hydroxide, cyanide and peroxide are counterexamples.

**Existing exercise anchors:** L05, `oxygen-families` (6 rows), WE-14 potassium chlorite, WE-15 sodium peroxide, WE-18 potassium dichromate. The handout's unclear NO⁻ sequence remains excluded from scoring.

### MAT-10 — Building a neutral ionic formula without dismantling ions

**Already available:** L03, “Build the formula without changing the passengers”; the whole-ion builder; `build-formulas` (29 rows, 87 fields); worked examples.

**Content:** Identifying positive and negative ions; whole-ion charges; choosing the smallest positive whole-number ratio that makes total charge zero; writing subscripts; parentheses around repeated polyatomic groups; counting atoms after constructing the formula.

**Explanatory connections:** The charge ledger is count × charge, added across the ions. Criss-cross is a shortcut whose result still needs checking, not the physical reason for the formula. Ca(NO₃)₂ contains two complete nitrates. MgO uses a 1:1 ion ratio. Na₂O₂ must retain peroxide's O₂ group, so reducing it to NaO destroys the intended ion identity.

**Existing example anchors:** WE-01 NaCl; WE-02 CaCl₂; WE-03 MgO; WE-04 Al₂O₃; WE-05 Mg₃N₂; WE-06 Ca(NO₃)₂; WE-07 Al₂(SO₄)₃; WE-08 AlPO₄; WE-09 (NH₄)₂SO₄; WE-13 calcium bicarbonate; WE-15 sodium peroxide.

### MAT-11 — Reading an ionic formula backward and choosing a Roman numeral

**Already available:** L04, “Read backward: let the known ion constrain the unknown”; `compound-names` (29 rows); reverse-direction practice and examples.

**Content:** Recognizing known ions in a formula; assigning their known charges; solving the neutrality constraint for a variable-charge metal; expressing that per-ion charge as a Roman numeral; naming cation before anion. Ambiguous names need additional information.

**Explanatory connections:** In Fe₂O₃, three O²⁻ ions contribute −6; the two Fe ions together contribute +6, so each is +3. “Iron(III)” does not mean three iron atoms. FeCl₂ and FeCl₃ represent different metal charges. A Roman numeral and a molecular prefix have different jobs.

**Existing example anchors:** WE-10 copper(II) nitrate; WE-11 iron(II) phosphate; WE-12 iron(III) sulfate; WE-16 iron(III) oxide; WE-17 copper(I) oxide; `compound-names`; mixed tutorial and Q13.

### MAT-12 — Choosing ionic or molecular covalent naming before naming anything

**Already available:** The shared ionic/molecular comparison reference; course row walkthroughs; the 30-question Nomenclature Tutorial and 21-question Q13.

**Content:** Ionic compounds described by cations and anions; molecular compounds described by atom counts and covalent bonding; metal/nonmetal clues and their limitations; recognition of a polyatomic ion as a whole unit; naming-model selection before calculation.

**Explanatory connections:** Neutrality alone does not distinguish an ionic compound from a neutral molecule. An ammonium salt may be ionic without containing a metal. The bonds inside NH₄⁺ are covalent while NH₄Cl is ionic between NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻. Boron halides in these activities use molecular naming despite boron being a metalloid. Network solids, elemental substances and unknown formulas are outside a simple two-box shortcut.

**Existing exercise anchors:** `nomenclature-names` (20 formula/name/type rows), `nomenclature-formulas` (10 name-to-formula rows), `q13-naming` (21 rows, name/formula plus type). Their exact given formulas and names are in the companion index.

### MAT-13 — Molecular prefixes and formula/name translation

**Already available:** `prefixes` (10 rows); `molecular-names` (CO, CO₂, P₂O₅, NO, NO₂, N₂O); mixed nomenclature activities.

**Content:** Mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, hexa-, hepta-, octa-, nona-, deca-; ordering the element names; the second element's -ide ending; omission of mono- on the first element; conventional vowel handling in names; translating prefixes into atom counts.

**Explanatory connections:** Molecular subscripts are not chosen by balancing a list of isolated ion charges. They are not reduced as though the molecular formula were only a ratio. CO and CO₂, or NO₂ and N₂O, cannot be interchanged because they share element names. A Roman numeral does not serve as a molecular prefix.

**Existing exercise anchors:** `prefixes`, `molecular-names`, tutorial 1–30, Q13. The given P₂O₅ naming exercise does not establish that all phosphorus(V) oxide material consists of discrete P₂O₅ molecules. Full instructor spelling conventions remain to be confirmed.

### MAT-14 — Lewis structures as a bridge from counts to bonding

**Already available:** `lewis` (NH₃, CH₄, H₂O; 9 fields); atomic Lewis-symbol work in Q9/tutorial; the periodic reference.

**Content:** Total valence-electron count; single bonds as shared pairs; central lone pairs; distinguishing a Lewis symbol for one atom from a molecular Lewis structure. The current molecule rows compare valence electrons, bond counts and central lone pairs.

**Explanatory connections:** NH₃, CH₄ and H₂O can be compared using the same electron-accounting model. This supports a later explanation of NH₄⁺ and formal charge. An atom's typical bonding pattern, its valence-electron count and an ion's charge are not interchangeable numbers.

**Coverage boundary:** Freehand structure drawings are self-review, not automatically assessed diagrams. Full resonance, VSEPR, polarity and acid naming are possible later additions, not complete existing worksheet coverage.

### MAT-15 — Mixed naming, error diagnosis and explaining a repaired answer

**Already available:** L06, “Translate both ways, explain a seam, and carry it forward”; all tutorial/Q13 rows; graduated hints; optional written reasoning; a reference card; supported practice and later review forms.

**Content:** Moving from recognition to an explanation; choosing a naming model; identifying which piece of an answer is right; separating identity, charge, ratio, grouping, notation and name errors. Formula/name and charge can be checked independently. A prose explanation can be retained without pretending it has been automatically graded.

**Explanatory connections:** “Formula correct, charge incorrect” is a useful partial success. Seeing a worked answer is learning support, not evidence of unaided recall. A useful repair describes the mistaken connection and the step that changes it. No actual misconception is proven solely by one wrong dropdown choice.

**Existing exercise anchors:** L06; PR-01–72; all 11 course sets; the full reference card; tutorial 1–30 and Q13 1–21. The first three study-plan objectives already connect ion identity, neutral-formula construction and Stock-naming scope to lessons.

### MAT-16 — Laboratory reasoning, safety and polymers

**Already available:** Two safety copies with 17 prompts each; two polymer observation copies with three ball rows each; routes “A rule needs a reason” and “An observation is not yet an explanation.”

**Content:** Relating a safe action to a specific hazard; distinguishing observations from causal explanations; qualitative properties and measured bounce height; comparing formulations; identifying uncontrolled differences; labeling predictions when an experiment has not been performed.

**Explanatory connections:** A safety rule has a reason, and an observed change does not on its own identify its molecular cause. Classroom laboratory procedures and supervision remain the authority; these stories would accompany reasoning practice rather than certify readiness or direct an unsupervised experiment.

**Existing exercise anchors:** `source-01`, `source-02`, `source-03`, `source-13`; routes `safety`, `polymers`. Personal photographs and original observations are not part of this handoff.

## The periodic-table reference available alongside stories

Every element has a profile entry, but this does not imply a complete, experimentally measured CRC-style record for every property. The reviewed reference combines a pinned Lumi_OS element snapshot with attributed public chemistry sources. Units, conditions, unavailable values and predicted values matter.

Existing reference areas include identity and periodic position; neutral electron configuration and electron-related properties; atomic-weight and isotope data; physical-property fields; and source-specific deeper property records. The visible study views are **Groups & valence**, **Isotopes & particles**, **s / p / d / f**, and **Element properties**. A property can support a story when its source, conditions and qualification are retained. An oxidation-state list is not a universal list of free monatomic-ion charges.

This reference is material to consult, not another question bank or a replacement for the course ion list. It is not copied from the CRC Handbook, and no claim of CRC completeness is made.
