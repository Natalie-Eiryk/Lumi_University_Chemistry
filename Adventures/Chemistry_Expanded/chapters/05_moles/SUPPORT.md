# Optional support for the Mole Counting Station

Story ID: luminara.adventure.005

The story stands alone. Choose a reference, picture, worked example, or explanation before, during, or after reading. Nothing here is a required quiz, timer, or demonstration of confidence. A calculator, speech, a drawing, and written calculations are equally legitimate tools.

## Start with the question

A useful receipt says what quantity is wanted and what entities or substance it concerns. “0.5 mol” is incomplete without its entity; “50%” needs a denominator; “10.2” needs a quantity and unit where applicable.

- Number of CO₂ molecules is not the same count as number of oxygen atoms within those molecules.
- Equal amounts of neon atoms and O₂ molecules mean equal numbers of the separately named entities. They need not have equal masses or equal constituent-atom counts.
- An isotope mean describes a stated population or sample. It is not an individual-atom construction instruction.

## Contextual glossary

### Specified entity

The object or composition unit being counted: for example, Ne atoms, O₂ molecules, Ca²⁺ ions, electrons, or CaCl₂ formula units. “Particles” may be too vague when different entities are possible. Formal units alone do not prevent substituting the wrong species. See [Scenes 2–4](STORY.md#ch05-scene-02) and [Scene 17](STORY.md#ch05-scene-17).

### Number of entities, N

A count of the specified entities. N is dimensionless in formal quantity calculus; words such as “O₂ molecules” keep its interpretation clear. A reported count inferred from a measurement may be approximate even though any actual finite collection contains an integer number of its specified members. See [Scene 7](STORY.md#ch05-scene-07).

### Amount of substance, n; mole, mol

Amount of substance is the quantity measured in moles. One mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ specified entities. It is not a universal mass, a container, or a fixed volume under unspecified conditions. A fractional number of moles does not require fractional molecules. See [Scene 2](STORY.md#ch05-scene-02).

### Avogadro constant, N_A

N_A = exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. Its exact defining value links amount and number: N = nN_A, or n = N/N_A. The subscript A is a name label on this constant, not an atom count. See [Scene 2](STORY.md#ch05-scene-02).

### Mass, m

A physical quantity expressed in units such as g, kg, or u. The mass of a sample alone does not identify its chemical composition or determine its number of molecules. See [Scenes 5–7](STORY.md#ch05-scene-05).

### Molar mass, M

Mass divided by amount for a specified substance, commonly expressed in g/mol: M = m/n. Consequently m = nM and n = m/M. It is substance-specific and may depend on the sample's isotopic composition. The story supplies approximate values rather than claiming unlimited precision. See [Scene 5](STORY.md#ch05-scene-05).

### Molecular formula and constituent-atom count

For a specified molecular species, subscripts give the numbers of constituent atoms in each molecule. CO₂ has one C and two O atoms per molecule. Thus 0.5 mol CO₂ molecules contains 0.5 mol C atoms and 1 mol O atoms within those molecules, totaling 1.5 mol constituent atoms. The counting description does not break bonds or claim those atoms are free neutral species. A molecular formula must not be automatically reduced: N₂O₄ and NO₂ describe different molecular compositions. See [Scenes 4](STORY.md#ch05-scene-04), [19](STORY.md#ch05-scene-19), and [20](STORY.md#ch05-scene-20).

### Formula unit

A specified composition unit for formula accounting. In an ordinary ionic solid, it does not imply isolated molecules or uniquely paired ions. The supplied idealized CaCl₂ solid has one Ca²⁺ for two Cl⁻ in its formula ratio. One mole of CaCl₂ formula units represents one mole Ca²⁺ and two moles Cl⁻ in that composition accounting. See [Scene 17](STORY.md#ch05-scene-17).

### Isotope, proton number, neutron number, and mass number

Isotopes of one element have the same proton number and different neutron numbers. Mass number is the integer count of protons plus neutrons in a nucleus; it is not identical to an atom's measured mass expressed in u. A change in isotopic mixture need not change the masses or neutron counts of any individual isotope. See [Scenes 9](STORY.md#ch05-scene-09) and [16](STORY.md#ch05-scene-16).

### Unified atomic mass unit, u; dalton, Da

u and Da name the same unit of mass. The atomic mass constant is one twelfth of the mass of a free neutral carbon-12 atom at rest in its ground state. Real isotope masses generally are not whole-number multiples of u. The 10 u/11 u story model and 24 u/26 u practice model are explicitly invented simplifications, not measurements or claimed isotope data for a real element. See [Scene 16](STORY.md#ch05-scene-16).

### Average atomic mass

The total mass of atoms in a specified collection divided by their number, expressible in u. Equivalently, sum each isotope mass multiplied by that isotope's atom-number fraction. The result need not equal any member's mass. In the artificial 80:20 mixture of 10 u/11 u model atoms, the mean is 10.2 u, while every member is assigned either 10 u or 11 u. See [Scene 10](STORY.md#ch05-scene-10).

### Atom-number fraction and mass fraction

An isotope's atom-number fraction is its number of atoms divided by the total number of atoms. Its mass fraction is its mass contribution divided by the total mass. These denominators differ. Use atom-number fractions to weight isotope masses for average atomic mass. The word “weight” in a weighted mean does not automatically mean a mass fraction. See [Scene 14](STORY.md#ch05-scene-14).

### Relative atomic mass; atomic weight

A dimensionless ratio of average atomic mass to the atomic mass constant. It is related to average atomic mass and molar mass, but their unit labels are not interchangeable. Standard atomic-weight values and intervals are reference information for defined materials and conditions, not a guarantee that every possible sample has an identical isotope mixture. See [Scene 16](STORY.md#ch05-scene-16).

### Sample, population, and pooled mean

The population is the collection a claim concerns. A sample is a selected part used for description or inference. A sample mean describes the sample; treating it as an estimate for a wider population requires attention to selection and uncertainty. To pool batch means into a mean over all atoms, weight each batch mean by its atom count, not merely by the number of batch cards. See [Scenes 13](STORY.md#ch05-scene-13) and [15](STORY.md#ch05-scene-15).

## Optional support A: three relations, different jobs

1. N = nN_A links number of specified entities and amount.
2. m = nM links mass and amount for the specified substance.
3. Constituent count = entity count × constituents per entity, using the supplied composition.

The first relation does not identify the substance. The second needs its appropriate molar mass. The third needs an appropriate formula or composition description. None by itself proves purity, volume, physical state, or measured precision.

## Optional support B: Natalie's dimensional fence posts

Given 8.00 g O₂ and supplied M(O₂) ≈ 32.00 g/mol:

8.00 g O₂ × (1 mol O₂ / 32.00 g O₂) = 0.250 mol O₂ molecules.

Then:

0.250 mol O₂ molecules × (2 mol O atoms / 1 mol O₂ molecules) = 0.500 mol O atoms within those molecules.

For a number of molecules, use 0.250 mol × N_A, giving approximately 1.51 × 10²³ O₂ molecules. For a number of constituent oxygen atoms, use 0.500 mol × N_A, giving approximately 3.01 × 10²³ O atoms within the molecules.

Check four different things:

- Does the relation apply to this substance and entity?
- Do the factors face the direction that cancels the starting units?
- Does the magnitude fit an estimate?
- Does the precision match the supplied data?

Units can catch a reversed factor. They cannot by themselves catch using neon's molar mass for O₂, because both use g/mol. A calculator completing an operation is not independent evidence that the operation was appropriate.

## Optional support C: an average without a new atom

Artificial model: eighty atoms assigned 10 u and twenty assigned 11 u.

Total assigned mass = 80(10 u) + 20(11 u) = 1,020 u.

Mean = 1,020 u / 100 = 10.2 u.

Equivalent weighted form: 0.80(10 u) + 0.20(11 u) = 10.2 u.

Baseline view: start with all one hundred at 10 u, then include 1 u extra for each of twenty heavy members. The extra twenty units across one hundred atoms gives a mean increment of 0.2 u. This spreads the increment in the calculation, not through the actual nuclei.

Checks: fractions sum to one; answer lies between the two masses; answer is closer to the more abundant isotope's mass. The midpoint applies only to equal atom-number fractions in a two-isotope model with those values.

## Optional support D: reverse and pooled means

For two specified artificial masses a and b, with b greater than a and heavy atom fraction f:

mean = a(1 − f) + bf = a + f(b − a).

Therefore f = (mean − a)/(b − a). This recovers a proportion under the stated two-isotope assumptions, not the total number of atoms or an unknown real chemical identity. A result outside 0–1 flags incompatible inputs or model assumptions.

For batches with means μ₁, μ₂ and atom counts N₁, N₂:

pooled mean = (N₁μ₁ + N₂μ₂)/(N₁ + N₂).

The simple mean (μ₁ + μ₂)/2 works when the two batches have equal atom counts, or accidentally in special cases such as equal means. Without relative counts, the pooled mean is generally undetermined.

## Optional support E: exact definitions and approximate samples

The defined Avogadro constant is exact. The story's explicitly exact counting thought experiments can therefore have exact arithmetic results. Measurements, approximate molar masses, purity assumptions, and sampling still limit claims about real specimens. Avoid both extremes: copying all calculator digits as measured certainty, or rounding the defining constant and calling that rounded value the definition.

Rounding tie in Scene 6: the displayed product 2.50 × 20.18 = 50.45 g falls exactly halfway between 50.4 g and 50.5 g at three significant figures. The story explicitly supplies the half-up convention, giving 50.5 g. The ties-to-even convention used by OpenStax gives 50.4 g instead. Both are legitimate reporting conventions; neither is claimed to be the instructor’s rule. Retain the working value and follow the convention specified for an actual task. Significant figures alone do not settle an exact tie or replace a full uncertainty analysis.

Optional advanced note: since the 2019 SI revision, the familiar numerical correspondence between an atomic mass in u and its corresponding molar mass in g/mol is extraordinarily close but not an exact universal identity. This nuance has no material effect at this chapter's introductory precision and is not an additional assessment requirement.

Optional advanced note from Scene 16: free proton and neutron masses are not exactly 1 u, and electrons also contribute to atomic mass. A bound nucleus has less mass than its separated protons and neutrons; the difference corresponds to its binding energy divided by c². A neutral atom additionally includes electrons and their electronic binding contribution. Electrons are not constituents of the nucleus, and atomic mass is not nuclear mass. None of these mass–energy distinctions makes the nucleon count fractional: mass number remains an integer count of protons and neutrons. See source S08 in [SOURCES.md](SOURCES.md). No nuclear calculation is required here.
