# Teacher key for the Mole Counting Station

Story ID: luminara.adventure.005

## Purpose and use

Assess entity specification, amount-versus-mass reasoning, and atom-number-weighted isotope averages. These additions extend beyond the periodic-workbench packet's original data scope. Do not imply that its reference_data.json contains atomic masses or isotope calculations.

Accept diagrams or verbal scaling as well as algebra. References and hints have no penalty. Keep the initial reasoning visible during revision. Questions are optional selections, not a compulsory sequence.

## Question 1 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.005.q01

Number of molecules = 0.25 mol × 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 1.50553519 × 10²³ N₂O₄ molecules. The amount is explicitly exact for this mathematical thought experiment, so the full digit string is defensible; an appropriately identified rounded value is also fine.

Nitrogen atoms: 0.25 mol molecules × 2 = 0.50 mol N atoms within the molecules.

Oxygen atoms: 0.25 mol molecules × 4 = 1.00 mol O atoms within the molecules.

Total atoms: 0.50 + 1.00 = 1.50 mol atoms. Each molecule contains six atoms, giving the equivalent check 0.25 × 6 = 1.50.

Rubric, 4 points: correct molecule conversion with entity label, 1; nitrogen amount, 1; oxygen amount, 1; total and explanation, 1. “Atoms” alone beside 1.50553519 × 10²³ is a substantive entity error. Help by drawing one molecule before changing the exponent.

## Question 2 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.005.q02

The samples have equal amounts of their separately specified entities, so they have equal numbers of neon atoms and O₂ molecules, respectively. Each count is approximately 1.20 × 10²⁴ entities at the precision of 2.00 mol.

Mass of neon = (2.00 mol)(20.18 g/mol) = 40.36 g before rounding, or 40.4 g to three significant figures.

Mass of O₂ = (2.00 mol)(32.00 g/mol) = 64.00 g before rounding, or 64.0 g to three significant figures.

The O₂ sample contains 4.00 mol oxygen atoms within its molecules, because each molecule has two oxygen atoms. The neon sample contains 2.00 mol neon atoms. Equal specified-entity counts do not require equal mass or equal total atom count.

Rubric, 4 points: equal numbers of specified entities with labels, 1; both mass calculations with grams, 1; 4.00 mol O atoms, 1; clear distinction between amount, mass, and entity, 1. Accept either unrounded intermediate values or suitable final rounding if uncertainty is not the learning target. Do not call the approximate mass values exact.

## Question 3 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.005.q03

Average = (0.75)(24 u) + (0.25)(26 u) = 18 u + 6.5 u = 24.5 u.

A 100-atom model gives the same result: total mass 75(24 u) + 25(26 u) = 2450 u; dividing by 100 gives 24.5 u per atom on average. Individual model atoms are assigned 24 u or 26 u; none is assigned 24.5 u. Their neutron counts remain whole numbers. The result lies between 24 and 26 and closer to the more abundant 24 u isotope.

Real isotope masses need not be integers in u. Do not teach that every decimal atomic mass must be an isotope average, or that mass in u is simply an exact proton-plus-neutron count. The artificial values isolate the averaging idea.

Rubric, 4 points: fractions correctly used, 1; average and unit, 1; individual-versus-average distinction, 1; plausibility and whole-neutron explanation, 1. If the learner obtains 25 u, ask whether a 50:50 assumption matches the supplied proportions.

## Question 4 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.005.q04

Let f be the atom-number fraction of the 26 u isotope. The other fraction is 1 − f.

24(1 − f) + 26f = 24.8

24 + 2f = 24.8, so f = 0.8/2 = 0.40.

The sample therefore contains 40% of its atoms as the 26 u isotope and 60% as the 24 u isotope. A non-algebra route observes that 24.8 is 0.8 u above 24, and each heavier atom contributes 2 u above the lighter baseline; 0.8/2 = 0.40.

Adding more 26 u atoms raises the average toward 26 u because the added atoms have mass greater than the current average. The weighted result cannot exceed 26 u in this two-isotope model.

Rubric, 4 points: valid method, 1; fraction 0.40 and percentage 40%, 1; complementary fraction or check of original average, 1; direction of change with reason, 1. A correct guess without reasoning can be followed by one optional explanation prompt.

## Interpreting evidence

The original four questions’ optional 16-point total is a reporting convenience, not a required scoring plan for this expanded set. More useful feedback may separate entity labels, unit conversion, atom counts, average construction, and interpretation. A suggested stopping point is one worked example plus one independently explained transfer of the learner's choice. Do not diagnose confidence or ability from speed, help use, or a single error.

## Precision note

The present mole definition fixes N_A exactly. It does not fix every material's molar mass exactly. Atomic mass in u and molar mass in g/mol are related but different quantities; since the SI revision, their familiar numerical correspondence is extremely accurate rather than an exact universal conversion. This nuance need not become an extra graded demand.

## Question 5 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.005.q05

The entity is missing. One mole need not refer to molecules; it could specify neon atoms, O₂ molecules, Ca²⁺ ions, electrons, or formula units of a supplied ionic substance. A mole is an amount unit, not a universal mass of one gram. To infer mass, identify the substance and use an appropriate molar mass.

Evidence to look for: a distinction between amount and mass, plus two genuinely specified entities. “Particles” without further description does not repair the missing specification when multiple interpretations remain. An appropriate spoken example suffices.

## Question 6 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.005.q06

The proposed expression leaves mol²/g, which is not the requested mass unit. Repair it:

3.00 mol Ne × (20.18 g Ne / 1 mol Ne) = 60.54 g before rounding ≈ 60.5 g at three significant figures.

Three times approximately twenty grams predicts approximately sixty grams, supporting the magnitude. Using 32.00 g/mol for O₂ could still cancel mol but would use the wrong substance-specific factor. Unit cancellation is necessary for the intended dimensional route but not sufficient for chemical correctness.

Feedback focus: if the result is wrong, inspect factor orientation before arithmetic. If the units work but the substance is wrong, name that distinct error rather than repeating a units hint. Accept a properly labeled 60.54 g intermediate if rounding is not being assessed.

## Question 7 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.005.q07

Amount of O₂ molecules:

16.0 g O₂ × (1 mol O₂ / 32.00 g O₂) = 0.500 mol O₂ molecules.

Molecule count:

0.500 mol × 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ ≈ 3.01 × 10²³ O₂ molecules.

Amount of constituent O atoms = 2 × 0.500 mol = 1.00 mol O atoms within the molecules.

Constituent O-atom count ≈ 6.02 × 10²³ O atoms within those molecules.

The factor of two is exact for the specified molecular composition. N_A is exact. The supplied sample mass and approximate molar mass are not thereby exact. The reported results reflect the input precision. Do not require a formal uncertainty calculation; require recognition that exact conversions do not eliminate uncertainty in measured inputs.

Common distinction: multiplying the original grams directly by N_A does not perform a mass-to-count conversion. First obtain amount using the appropriate M, or write both factors in a single labeled chain.

## Question 8 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.005.q08

The composition ratio is 1 Ca²⁺ : 2 Cl⁻.

- Ca²⁺ amount = 0.200 mol.
- Cl⁻ amount = 0.400 mol.
- Total amount of ions in this idealized composition accounting = 0.600 mol.

The ordinary ionic-solid model is extended; “formula units” specifies composition accounting rather than isolated CaCl₂ molecules. The Ca²⁺ superscript describes charge. It does not mean there are two calcium ions per formula unit. One Ca²⁺ plus two Cl⁻ has net charge zero; charge balance and entity counts are related checks with different roles.

Boundary: the problem supplies the material identity and model. It does not ask the learner to derive phase, lattice structure, solubility, or actual synthesis solely from a written formula.

## Question 9 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.005.q09

All values are artificial.

Case A: eighty light and ten heavy atoms; ninety atoms total. Assigned total mass = 80(10 u) + 10(11 u) = 910 u. Mean = 910/90 u = 91/9 u ≈ 10.111 u.

Case B: ninety light and ten heavy atoms; one hundred atoms total. Assigned total mass = 90(10 u) + 10(11 u) = 1,010 u. Mean = 10.1 u.

Both have ten heavy atoms, but the fractions of heavy atoms differ: 10/90 versus 10/100. Removing and replacing change the denominator differently. Neither operation changes an individual model isotope's assigned mass. Accept exact fractions or sensibly rounded decimals with an explanation.

## Question 10 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.005.q10

All isotope values are artificial.

Batch A mean = 10 u. Batch B mean = (15 × 10 u + 15 × 11 u)/30 = 10.5 u.

Merged collection: twenty-five light and fifteen heavy atoms, forty total. Total assigned mass = 25(10 u) + 15(11 u) = 415 u. Pooled mean = 415/40 u = 10.375 u.

Equivalent count-weighted mean of batch means:

(10 × 10 u + 30 × 10.5 u)/40 = 10.375 u.

The unweighted mean of the batch means is 10.25 u. It gives equal influence to two batches that contribute unequal numbers of atoms. With only the two means, their relative atom counts are needed for the pooled mean in general. Actual absolute counts are unnecessary if their ratio or fractions are supplied.

Useful nuance: equal-sized batches permit the unweighted mean; identical batch means also give the same pooled value regardless of sizes. Do not overstate the rule as “averaging averages is always wrong.”

## Question 11 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.005.q11

All isotope masses and totals are artificial.

- Light count = 110 u / (10 u per model atom) = 11 model atoms.
- Heavy count = 110 u / (11 u per model atom) = 10 model atoms.
- Total count = 21 model atoms.
- Heavy atom-number fraction = 10/21 ≈ 0.47619, or approximately 47.619%.
- Total assigned mass = 220 u; mean = 220/21 u ≈ 10.47619 u.

Each kind contributes 110/220 = 0.50 of the total mass. Those are mass fractions. The number-weighted atomic mean instead uses 11/21 and 10/21, yielding the same 220/21 u result. Equal masses require fewer of the heavier model atoms.

Do not describe mass fractions as inherently wrong. They are correct descriptors answering a different question. The error is silently substituting them for number fractions in this mean.

## Question 12 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.005.q12

In the stated artificial two-isotope model, any nonnegative weighted mean with fractions summing to one must lie between 24 u and 26 u, inclusive. 26.4 u lies outside that range.

f = (26.4 − 24)/(26 − 24) = 2.4/2 = 1.20.

That would require 120% heavy atoms and −20% light atoms, which is not a permitted composition. The algebra has exposed incompatible inputs or assumptions; it has not discovered negative atoms.

Possible next checks include the copied mean, the specified isotope masses, whether another isotope/category is present, and whether the label actually refers to another quantity or sample. Two clearly stated checks suffice. The available data do not identify which source entry is wrong. Do not silently change 26.4 to a convenient in-range value.

## Question 13 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.005.q13

Artificial population mean = 0.80(10 u) + 0.20(11 u) = 10.2 u.

Ten-atom sample mean = [9(10 u) + 1(11 u)]/10 = 10.1 u.

The difference does not prove the stipulated population label wrong. A small random sample need not exactly match the population's isotope proportions. Its mean accurately describes its observed composition. Using it to infer the wider population requires attention to sampling and uncertainty.

Deliberately selecting only heavy atoms would produce a mean of 11 u for that selected group, even if the group were large. Increasing size does not automatically repair a biased selection method. No probability calculation or confidence interval is required. Avoid claiming that every larger random sample is necessarily closer to the population mean than every smaller one.

## Question 14 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.005.q14

1. The defining constant is exactly N_A = 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. The value 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ is a rounded working approximation, not the full exact definition.
2. Exact N_A does not make a sample measurement or inference exact. Sample mass, molar-mass approximation, composition, and measurement uncertainty still matter.
3. Relative atomic mass, also called atomic weight, is dimensionless. Individual or average atomic mass can be expressed in u/Da. Molar mass uses a mass-per-amount unit such as g/mol.
4. Decimal atomic mass does not imply fractional neutrons. An average may fall between member values; real individual isotope masses also need not be integers in u. Proton and neutron counts are integers, while mass is a different physical quantity.

Average atomic mass describes a mean mass per atom in a specified population; molar mass relates a material's mass to its amount. Their familiar introductory numerical correspondence does not make their definitions or units interchangeable.

The tiny post-2019 numerical qualification belongs in optional depth, not as an extra scoring demand. A learner who makes the main distinctions accurately should not be marked wrong for omitting it unless it was explicitly the agreed topic.

## Integrative feedback

A useful short review can choose one question from each of three areas: specified entities, dimensional conversion, and isotope averages. There is no requirement to finish all fourteen.

Keep first attempts alongside revisions if the learner wants that record. Ask which check changed the answer. Do not infer speed, confidence, willingness to use hints, or a single misconception as a stable trait. The story's pause is an invitation to inspect the model, not a reward withheld until someone is wrong.
