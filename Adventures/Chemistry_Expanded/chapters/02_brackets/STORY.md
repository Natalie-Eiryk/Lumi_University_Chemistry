## 1. The gas that would not fit the label

In a London laboratory in the imagined autumn of 1904, William Ramsay was trying to put a small glass tube into a much larger wooden box. The tube fitted. Its label did not. Someone had copied the name of a gas onto a strip of paper so generously that the strip covered the neighboring compartment, where a different tube waited in its own curl of packing material.

Ramsay lifted the label again. A name could save an enormous amount of explanation, provided it remained attached to the right thing.

He set the tube down and opened a notebook. The real work behind these imagined preparations had occupied years: separating gases, comparing their properties, and asking what remained when familiar explanations had been tested. The discovery of argon in 1894 had involved both Ramsay and Lord Rayleigh. Neon had followed in 1898 through work with Morris Travers. The small glass objects on the fictional bench represented investigations far larger than their containers.

From inside the empty packing box came the sound of an engine reversing.

Ramsay stopped writing.

A yellow corner appeared between two dividers. Then a mirror unfolded, followed by a windscreen, an entire bonnet, and a bus driver whose hat was decorated with square brackets. The bench did not break. The bus seemed to have negotiated a private agreement with distance.

“Please keep your specimens where they are,” Ms Luminara called through the window. “We provide our own impossible luggage.”

Ramsay stepped back. “Who are you, and why is there a vehicle in my packing case?”

“Ms Luminara. Educational transport. The second question deserves a longer answer than this bench will support.”

Behind her, Jeff tested a seatbelt buckle with considerable attention. Kàra was trying to see how the wheels could occupy the same space as a drawer. Ástríðr held a cup level through the bus's final, gentle tilt. Henry had found a sign saying TEMPORARY DISAGREEMENT WITH SCALE and was checking whether it had an expiry date.

Natalie sat beside an open notebook. She had once taught physics, which gave her many useful questions about the bus and no useful means of stopping it. On the page before her she had written a configuration whose letters seemed manageable separately and strangely forbidding together.

“We're visiting an office that stores long descriptions inside short ones,” Natalie said. “I thought I understood the idea until the short description started feeling more real than what it stood for.”

Ramsay looked from her notebook to his overlapping labels.

Ms Luminara came down the steps carrying a suitcase the size of a teacup. From it she removed an umbrella, six folded maps, and a notice that read ALL PASSENGERS AND LUGGAGE MUST BE ACCOUNTED FOR.

“Everything that goes in comes out?” Ramsay asked.

“That is the claim,” Ms Luminara said. “Today we intend to inspect it.”

She slid the notice into a frame inside the case's lid, then repacked the umbrella and all six maps. The contents slip listed them beneath seven small tick marks.

Ramsay did not immediately board. He asked where the bus would take him, when it would return, and whether anyone proposed to open his gas tubes. Ms Luminara answered each question. The tubes would remain untouched. The journey would visit a fictional representation of modern atomic bookkeeping. He would return to this room at approximately the moment he left. Knowledge from later centuries could be discussed on the bus, but it would not be carried back as an explanation of how his documented discoveries had occurred.

“You are asking me to consider a later account of substances I have already investigated,” Ramsay said.

“And to tell us when a convenient label starts doing more work than its evidence permits,” Ms Luminara replied.

He examined the little suitcase once more, then shut his notebook and left it on the bench.

“I am William Ramsay,” he said as he boarded. He repeated his name slowly when Ástríðr asked. “I come from Glasgow, and I work here in London. The year, unless your instrument has already interfered with it, is 1904. I have been concerned with gases that were present without being recognized as distinct constituents. Before your arrival, my immediate concern was a box.”

“Both concerns qualify you,” Ms Luminara said.

Jackson shifted his bag to make room. “Will he already know the answers?”

“Some of the questions,” Ms Luminara said. “Our modern account of atomic nuclei and electron configurations comes later than his period. We will introduce it openly. Discovering neon did not require knowing the notation in Natalie's notebook.”

Ramsay leaned toward the page without touching it. The string read 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹.

“I recognize no gas from that alone,” he said.

Natalie felt an unexpected relief. She had been preparing to conceal how slowly she read the superscripts. Here was someone who had helped discover elements, and he needed the notation explained.

Henry pointed to the upper numbers. “These count electrons in parts of a configuration. We're learning which parts a bracket can stand for.”

“Then begin there,” Ramsay said. “I can follow an inventory before I know its furniture.”

Ms Luminara handed him a visitor's card. Its front said MODERN INFORMATION WILL BE SUPPLIED. Its back said THIS VEHICLE DOES NOT CLAIM HISTORICAL PRIORITY.

Jeff inspected the card over Ramsay's shoulder. “That seems sensible.”

“Seatbelts everyone,” Ms Luminara said.

The laboratory window lengthened into a strip of gray daylight. The packing box opened into a tunnel lined with labels. One said HANDLE WITH CARE. Another said HANDLE IS AN ANALOGY. The bus emerged under a vast glass roof, where rain ran down the panes and enormous square brackets stood behind counters wearing very small ties.

Above the entrance, gold letters announced THE BRACKET LUGGAGE OFFICE.

Below them, a smaller notice promised SAME CONTENTS. LESS WRITING.

Natalie copied that promise before they went inside. She left a line underneath it. A promise, she had learned, became useful when there was somewhere to write what would count as keeping it.

## 2. Eleven at the counter

The office smelled of paper, warm varnish, and the rain that had followed them through the doors. Wheels rattled under the counters. Somewhere behind a partition, a machine rang a bell after every few seconds of work. The bell had the bright, unquestioning sound of a device that had never been asked to show its calculations.

Their first clerk was a left square bracket. Its partner, a right square bracket, was straightening a pile of claim tickets. Between them stood a long display divided into labeled shelves.

NEUTRAL SODIUM. GROUND STATE.

Beneath that heading appeared Natalie's configuration: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹.

Eleven white tokens rested in small trays. They were unmistakably counters, flat and numbered on the back. Ms Luminara picked one up before anybody could decide it was an electron enlarged to a convenient size.

“This office handles descriptions,” she said. “The tokens count electrons in those descriptions. Real electrons are not white luggage discs, and the shelves are not little cupboards inside an atom.”

Ramsay turned his visitor's card over. “The distinction is welcome.”

A second display named the modern quantities they would need. The nucleus contained eleven protons. The atom had eleven electrons. A proton carried one positive elementary charge; an electron carried one negative elementary charge. Eleven of each made the atom electrically neutral overall. Atomic number counted protons and identified the element as sodium.

Ramsay studied the word nucleus. On the modern glossary Ms Luminara supplied, he underlined the entry and found proton beneath it. He kept the glossary open beside the eleven-counter display.

“And ground state?” he asked.

“The lowest-energy state of the stated atom or ion,” Ms Luminara said. “That heading matters. A configuration is a claim about more than how many electrons exist.”

Henry moved to the first tray. “Two in 1s. Two in 2s. Six in 2p. One in 3s. Two plus two plus six plus one is eleven.”

Natalie followed on paper. She had expected to recognize the whole expression at a glance, the way she recognized a familiar word. Instead, she read it term by term. The slower method gave her somewhere to stand.

Kàra walked the length of the counter. “The two in 2s and the six in 2p belong to shell two, because their first number is two?”

“Yes,” Ms Luminara said. “The leading number labels a shell through its principal quantum number. The letter distinguishes a subshell within that shell. The superscript gives the occupancy of the subshell in this configuration.”

“So the two at the front of 2p⁶ does a different job from the six above it,” Kàra said.

She put two fingers under the front number and then moved them away. She did not want her own gesture to turn into another false count.

Ástríðr noticed that the trays for 2s and 2p had the same shell marker along their lower edge. “Related addresses, different rooms. Except the rooms are already a metaphor.”

“An acknowledged metaphor can still help,” Ms Luminara said. “We'll stop it before it claims to be a floor plan.”

Jeff had found the help drawer. It contained the fully expanded configurations of helium, neon, and argon, printed large enough to read without leaning over the mechanism.

“Do we have to try without these first?” he asked.

“No,” Ms Luminara said. “They are references. Their job is to be consulted.”

Jeff took the neon card. It read 1s² 2s² 2p⁶. He held it below the first three terms of sodium's display. The terms lined up. The final 3s¹ remained beyond the card's edge.

“Ten accounted for here,” Jeff said. “One still outside.”

The right bracket offered him a stamp. Jeff did not take it yet.

“What would stamping do?” he asked.

“Replace that occupied prefix with a shorter label,” the clerk said. Its voice sounded like paper sliding against paper. “No tokens removed.”

Jackson crouched to inspect the slot beneath the display. “Where do the old terms go?”

“Into a reversible fold,” the clerk said.

“Can we open it again?” Jackson asked.

The bracket clerk indicated a brass handle labeled EXPAND. Jackson stood up. An answer that came with a handle was better than an answer that came with a reassuring tone.

Ramsay watched Jeff compare the two strings again. “When a tube arrived with a name attached, I could not treat the name as an observation made afresh. Somebody had done work before the label reached me. Your card is also a claim about what is behind it.”

Ms Luminara nodded toward the original display. “Here we have supplied the configuration and stated the species. That is our reference for this demonstration. The bracket must preserve it.”

The bell sounded behind the partition. A little quicker this time.

Natalie looked back at her notebook, where SAME CONTENTS. LESS WRITING waited above its empty line. She wrote: Expand it and compare.

She did not write trust nobody. The clerk had been helpful, the card was legible, and the reference was exactly what she needed. She wanted a way to make trust inspectable, rather than another rule requiring her to feel suspicious all day.

Jeff put his hand on the stamp. “All right,” he said. “Let's make the small version.”

## 3. The fold that opens again

The brackets approached one another across the counter. Between them, the terms 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ lifted off their display and folded like a map. They folded once along a blank space, twice along another, and finally disappeared into a small square case marked [Ne].

The ten counter windows stayed lit.

Beside the case stood 3s¹ with its one remaining token. The new line read [Ne] 3s¹.

Jeff looked at the case, then at the windows, then at Natalie. “I still want to open it.”

“An excellent use for the handle,” Ms Luminara said.

He pulled. The case unfolded into 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, leaving 3s¹ where it had been. Nothing new was added; nothing disappeared. The expanded line matched the reference exactly.

The clerk folded it again. This time Ástríðr pressed a finger against the little neon symbol.

“Does this mean a neon atom has gone into sodium?” she asked.

Ramsay looked sharply at the case. That possibility plainly concerned him more than the bus emerging from a box.

“No,” Ms Luminara said. “The symbol inside the brackets names the configuration used as our abbreviation. The sodium atom still has its sodium nucleus. We haven't placed a second atom inside it.”

Ástríðr nodded but left her finger there a moment longer. “The picture makes the wrong thought very easy.”

“Then it has to carry the correction nearby,” Henry said.

He asked the clerk for a blank tag and wrote CONFIGURATION REFERENCE. NOT A GAS SAMPLE. The clerk attached it to the case without removing the short notation.

Natalie watched the tag swing. In her notebook she had sometimes drawn circles around inner electrons and written a noble-gas name beside them. She had not meant a separate atom, but the picture had acquired more solidity than she intended. Now she drew the same notation without enclosing a second nucleus.

Kàra tried a different test. “If we replace the prefix with [Ne], do the electrons physically rearrange?”

Ms Luminara touched the fold handle. “Writing an abbreviation changes our description. In this exercise, the atom remains in the same stated state. The office is moving printed information, not driving a physical transition.”

The phrase stayed with Kàra. She had been listening for the click of a real mechanism: fold, lock, change. Sometimes a diagram invited her to invent a force where only a typographical operation had occurred.

Jackson followed the small case to the next counter. A label printer copied [Ne] 3s¹ onto a claim ticket. He took the ticket to a second expansion handle. The same full configuration appeared there.

“It travels,” Jackson said. “The first clerk isn't needed to explain it every time.”

“That is one reason shorthand is useful,” Ms Luminara said. “It preserves a known pattern in a compact form. But the receiving person must know which pattern the bracket means, or be able to look it up.”

Ramsay took the ticket and held it at arm's length. “A name can carry work from one place to another. It can also carry an error efficiently.”

Behind the partition, the machine bell rang three times in quick succession.

The left bracket clerk glanced toward the sound. A ribbon had begun to emerge from a slot in the wall. It bore a succession of sodium labels, each shorter than the one before. The mechanism seemed to be competing with itself.

“Your colleague is enthusiastic,” Ástríðr said.

“New compacting attachment,” the clerk replied. “Its promise is to reduce unnecessary writing.”

Natalie set the verified ticket beside the emerging ribbon. She liked the economy of [Ne] 3s¹. It meant she could look directly at the portion beyond the core without rewriting everything underneath. The compactness was a reward for an understood relationship, not an obstacle she had to vault.

Ramsay asked to see the reference once more. Henry gave it to him. Ramsay read the occupied terms while Henry followed the printed line with a strip of blank paper, exposing one term at a time. At 2p⁶, Ramsay stopped and consulted his glossary before continuing. The others waited.

“Your core case is ten electrons,” he said. “Your additional term is one. The name sodium comes from the stated eleven protons, not from my finding a little sample of neon in the box.”

“That's the distinction,” Henry said.

For a few minutes they simply used it. Jeff folded the line. Kàra expanded it. Ástríðr compared the result with the full reference. Jackson checked whether the printed ticket still led to the same expansion at another counter. No one was hurried to announce mastery.

Rain ran in bright threads down the glass roof. The office, from this angle, was rather beautiful. Long descriptions traveled into smaller cases and arrived whole. Natalie could imagine wanting such a place to work.

Then a new ticket slid out of the compacting attachment.

NEUTRAL SODIUM. GROUND STATE. [He] 3s¹.

The bell rang with extravagant approval.

Natalie reached toward the ticket almost automatically. Its heading was familiar. Its final term was familiar. Its bracket looked official. For a moment, familiarity supplied the missing check.

Then she looked at the neon reference still open under Ramsay's hand.

“Wait,” Natalie said. “What happened to the middle?”

## 4. Eight missing from the label

The ticket was already moving. A small belt carried it toward a roller that would press its notation onto a whole stack of destination cards. Jackson could see the stack descending, one sheet at a time. Once printed, the cards would go into drawers throughout the office.

“Pause it,” Natalie said.

Jeff was nearest the machine, but the first button under his hand said STOP ALL SERVICES. He took half a second to look lower and found HOLD THIS BATCH. He pressed it. The roller stopped above the paper without touching it.

No alarm sounded. A panel opened and displayed PLEASE SPECIFY THE PROBLEM.

Ms Luminara read it with approval. “Unusually good manners for an overconfident appliance.”

Kàra leaned close to the faulty ticket. “The machine kept the last term. It switched the bracket from neon to helium because helium's label is somehow cheaper?”

Both element symbols contained two letters. The machine had not even become shorter in any useful visual sense.

Henry found the rule plate bolted to its side. It read USE THE SMALLEST AVAILABLE CORE. KEEP THE FINAL OCCUPIED TERM.

“That would work for lithium,” Henry said slowly. “It wouldn't preserve all the intervening occupied terms for sodium.”

Natalie took the helium card from the help drawer. [He] expanded to 1s². She set it beside 3s¹. Two electrons represented in the bracket, one outside: three.

The reference for neutral sodium required eleven.

Ástríðr counted the difference on her fingers and then on the written line. “Eight missing from the description. The two in 2s and the six in 2p.”

The machine displayed CONTENTS OPTIMIZED.

“Your contents were not yours to optimize,” Ástríðr told it.

Ramsay put the original and faulty tickets next to each other. “Before we say that eight electrons have been lost, what exactly has changed here?”

Natalie looked up. “The label. We haven't actually ionized a sodium atom.”

The distinction mattered enough to make her cross out the words electrons lost in her first note. She replaced them with electrons omitted from representation. The machine had not converted a physical sodium atom into an eight-times ionized species by editing its paperwork. It had produced an incorrect description under a neutral-sodium heading.

Ms Luminara left the crossed-out words visible. “That is a useful repair. Later we will see an actual electron-count change in a model. We should not confuse it with a missing term on paper.”

Jackson inspected the held stack. “How many labels already went through?”

The machine produced a batch receipt. Twenty-four had been printed before Jeff pressed the local hold. They had not left the office. Six were in the reference drawer cart. Eighteen were assigned to dispatch: seventeen in its waiting rack and the faulty ticket still on the inspection belt.

“Then the error hasn't reached everything yet,” Jackson said. “We need those twenty-four, and we need to stop this rule from making more.”

He gave the receipt to the right bracket clerk, who shut the dispatch shutter for that batch. The rest of the office continued working. People could still collect correct descriptions, ask for help, or wait with their luggage. Jeff relaxed his hand. He had prevented the spread without turning the entire room into an emergency.

Kàra wanted to remove the rule plate at once. Henry asked her to wait.

“If we just replace helium with neon everywhere, what happens to lithium?” he asked.

Kàra let go of the screwdriver the machine had helpfully offered. “We fix sodium by breaking another case.”

Ramsay watched them hold two problems in view: correcting the ticket and correcting the procedure. The first was simple enough. The second needed a rule that said what the abbreviation preserved.

“Can we inspect the original record the machine was given?” he asked.

The clerk brought out a transparent sleeve. Inside, neutral sodium's full configuration remained intact. Its heading still gave eleven protons and eleven electrons. There had been no uncertainty in the input. The error occurred during shortening.

Natalie found that reassuring in a precise way. They did not need to rediscover sodium. They needed to explain a mismatch between two representations.

With the batch held, the clerk disconnected the compacting attachment and invited Kàra to remove its rule plate. She kept the screws in its shallow lid. Henry carried the plate so they could examine its wording away from the stalled rollers.

Jeff carried the faulty case to the expansion handle and opened it. Only 1s² emerged before 3s¹. He then opened the verified [Ne] case. The whole ten-electron prefix unfolded.

“I can distrust the bell and still inspect the box,” Jeff said.

Ms Luminara smiled. “That sentence will travel.”

But Jeff did not smile back immediately. He was looking at the twenty-four-label receipt.

“I nearly pressed the wrong stop button,” he said.

“You looked at what the buttons did,” Jackson replied. “The batch stopped before the next print.”

Ástríðr took the faulty ticket from the belt and pinned it above the held stack. Below it she placed the full reference and the number eight. “We should leave the reason here. Otherwise someone might see a stopped machine and restart it to be helpful.”

The left bracket clerk adjusted its tiny tie. “Would a notice suffice?”

“A notice with the actual failed check,” Henry said.

They wrote it together: This output expands to three electrons. The stated neutral sodium configuration contains eleven. Occupied terms 2s² 2p⁶ were omitted.

The bell tried a tentative ding.

“Less confidence,” Ástríðr suggested, “until the inventory agrees.”

The machine considered this and produced a thoughtful clunk.

## 5. The shelf behind the shelf

The reference drawer cart had already left the main hall. Its route followed a narrow service passage where the public counters had their backs turned toward one another. From here, the polished office looked less effortless. There were hinges, spare handles, ink bottles, and a chair repaired with a piece of wood that did not quite match.

The left bracket clerk led them at a measured pace. Nothing was escaping. The dispatch shutter was closed, the batch was marked, and the original sodium record was safe. Jackson had wanted to run until the clerk showed him the local hold indicator above the passage. It remained steadily on.

“We have time to do this accurately,” Ms Luminara said.

Natalie was grateful for the sentence. Urgency had made her want to race through the next count as proof that the first hesitation had been accidental. She would have made a second error for the sake of appearing less vulnerable to the first.

The cart stood beside a row of shallow drawers. Six faulty cards had been placed in pockets marked SODIUM, alongside older cards that were correct. The clerk had not yet inserted them into the public reference sets.

Ramsay did not pull every card out at once. He asked for a place to put the ones they had checked. Ástríðr cleared a small table beneath a high window. Henry drew three headings on separate sheets: VERIFIED ORIGINAL, FAULTY OUTPUT, NOT YET CHECKED.

“Why the third?” Kàra asked. “Everything unverified can go in the faulty pile until we check.”

Ramsay shook his head. “Then the pile would say more than we know.”

Kàra looked at the correct older card beside the new wrong one. Both had come from the same drawer. She moved the unexamined cards under NOT YET CHECKED.

They worked in pairs. One person read the heading and expanded the bracket. Another compared the result with the supplied full configuration. They were not judging the cards by their age, their printing quality, or the confidence of their stamps. Jeff found a smudged older card that was completely correct. Ástríðr found a crisp new card with exactly the missing terms they expected.

Natalie counted the six faulty cards twice. On her first pass, she laid one aside to wipe moisture from the table and forgot whether it belonged to the completed group. Instead of guessing, she moved that card back to NOT YET CHECKED.

Henry noticed but said nothing until she had finished.

“That was a useful place to put it,” he said.

“I almost made a fourth pile called probably,” Natalie replied.

“Probably can be useful,” Ms Luminara said. “It just needs to be labeled honestly. This particular job lets us check.”

Ramsay sat on the repaired chair. Its short leg gave a mild complaint, then settled. He turned a correct card in his hands.

“In the work that brought us the rare gases, a remainder could be the beginning of a question,” he said. “Here the remainder has been erased by a copying procedure. Those are different situations. You have already supplied the full inventory.”

Natalie looked at the omitted 2s² 2p⁶. “The eight didn't become less important because they were between the beginning and the end.”

Jackson took the six recovered cards and matched their serial marks to the batch receipt. “And we couldn't repair the office by fixing only the card we happened to notice.”

Kàra had begun examining the correct original in another way. She laid a strip of paper over the middle eight electrons. The visible ends looked very much like the faulty output. It was easy to understand how the machine's rule had arrived there. It kept a familiar beginning and a familiar ending and quietly treated the connection as expendable.

She removed the strip.

“I want a repair that checks what the fold covers,” Kàra said. “Otherwise the next mistake could be a different missing section.”

Henry wrote her sentence on the back of the rule plate they had brought along. He added that every term left outside the fold also had to remain.

The rain softened. For a while, the only sounds were paper and the occasional murmur of a count. Nobody turned the six cards into six new lessons. Once the error was established, retrieval was careful ordinary work.

When they returned to the main hall, the right bracket clerk had recovered seventeen cards from the dispatch rack and added the ticket Ástríðr had pinned above the held stack. Jackson laid that group beside the six from the reference cart: eighteen and six, making twenty-four. Every item named by the receipt was accounted for. The faulty originals went into a marked review envelope rather than back into circulation. Its cover listed all twenty-four serial marks.

Natalie asked whether they should destroy them.

“Keep one with the reason,” Ramsay suggested. “A corrected rule is easier to understand when the failure it repairs remains visible.”

Ástríðr chose the first ticket, the one whose bell had sounded so certain. She placed it in the envelope's transparent front pocket, where its serial mark remained visible, and hung the whole envelope above the machine. One faulty original was on view; the other twenty-three stayed behind it. All remained together for the replacement job.

Ms Luminara rested the little suitcase on the counter. “We have recovered the bad outputs,” she said. “We still owe the office a useful way to make good ones.”

Henry turned toward the helium drawer.

“The bracket itself wasn't wrong,” he said. “It was asked to stand for more than it contained.”

## 6. A longer answer that tells the truth

Henry set a helium core case on the workbench. It looked disappointingly ordinary after the trouble it had caused: two square brackets, the letters He, a working expansion handle, and two occupied places in its count window.

“Open,” Henry said.

The case unfolded to 1s².

He placed 2s² beside it, then 2p⁶, then 3s¹. The full line was correct for the supplied neutral sodium ground state. When he folded only 1s² back into the case, the result read [He] 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹.

Ástríðr counted thirteen, stopped, and looked again. “I added the leading two in 2p⁶ as well as its six. That shell number doesn't belong in my sum.”

She touched 2p⁶. “This front two is an address. The six is the count.”

Natalie followed her finger. Together they read only the occupancy numbers: two inside helium, two in 2s, six in 2p, one in 3s. Eleven.

The repair machine accepted the total. It printed a neat message: TOO LONG. INCORRECT.

Kàra let out a short laugh. “It's found another way to be confident.”

The clerk explained that a separate office rule requested the nearest earlier noble-gas core. For sodium, that was neon. The longer helium-core notation did not meet the requested compact format.

“Then say that,” Ástríðr told the machine. “You can't say the electrons are missing when they're all here.”

Ramsay stood between the two labels. One read [He] 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹; the other read [Ne] 3s¹. He opened them in turn. The expanded configurations were identical.

“You have two questions,” he said. “Does the description preserve the stated configuration? And does it use the form your office requested?”

Henry asked for two stamps. The clerk offered CORRECT and INCORRECT. Henry waited.

After a moment, the clerk opened a lower drawer and produced INVENTORY PRESERVED and REQUESTED FORM MET.

“Those will do,” Henry said.

They stamped the longer helium form INVENTORY PRESERVED. They did not stamp REQUESTED FORM MET, because the task specifically asked for the nearest earlier noble gas. The neon form received both stamps. The original faulty [He] 3s¹ received neither.

Natalie watched the three cards settle into a pattern she could explain. Earlier, she had felt caught between two bad options: insist that every shorter method must be equally acceptable, or accept that any nonpreferred answer must be chemically false. The separate stamps let her keep the valid part without denying the instruction.

“I might need to revise a correct description to fit a particular request,” Natalie said. “That doesn't mean the description had lost eight electrons.”

“Exactly,” Ms Luminara said. “Feedback should name the repair it actually asks for.”

Jeff chose the longer helium form and carried it to the distant expansion counter. He had begun to enjoy the act of walking a claim away from its maker. The second handle opened it to all eleven electrons, just as the first had.

When he returned, the machine tried to shorten the label automatically. Jeff held the card above the rollers.

“No,” he said. “First tell me what this job requires.”

The clerk consulted the job slip. It asked for a compact form using the nearest noble gas of lower atomic number. Jeff put the neon form into that job's tray. He left the longer helium form in a second tray labeled VALID EXPANSION EXAMPLE.

Jackson had another concern. “If someone asks only for a full configuration, the machine shouldn't reject the full version because a bracket exists.”

Ms Luminara nodded. “The question sets the representation we need. We must read it before deciding which answer does the job.”

Kàra paced between the trays. “I keep wanting one stamp to mean every kind of success.”

“That would be a very crowded stamp,” Ástríðr said.

The machine printed a prototype roughly the size of a doormat. The clerk quietly pushed it under the counter.

They drafted a replacement rule plate. Start from the supplied configuration. Choose the core specified by the task. Replace exactly the occupied prefix that core represents. Keep every remaining occupied term. Expand and compare afterward.

Kàra reached for the screws, but Jackson put the held-batch receipt beside her hand.

“Have we checked the rest of the machine?” he asked.

The clerk opened a narrow cover. Beyond the folding mechanism, another attachment could propose a continuation, and a separate gate decided whether to approve the result. They had inspected neither.

“We've repaired the fold,” Kàra said. She returned the screws to the lid. “We haven't earned a restart.”

The clerk set the new plate beside the old one. Trial records could travel along the inspection track while the dispatch shutter stayed closed. The twenty-four replacement cards would wait until the whole route had been tested.

Ramsay asked why the office favored the nearest earlier noble gas for these neutral atoms. Henry pointed to the length of the two correct sodium forms. The neon abbreviation removed more repeated writing while preserving the configuration. The earlier condition prevented the convention from collapsing into a self-label at the target.

“That last point needs a demonstration,” Ramsay said.

The left bracket clerk had anticipated them. Three destination cards waited on a trolley: LITHIUM, NEON, SODIUM. Their full ground-state configurations were clipped behind them. A narrow track crossed a painted boundary on the floor.

Ms Luminara took her umbrella out of the tiny suitcase, though the roof did not leak.

“Sometimes a rule looks perfectly clear until it reaches its own edge,” she said. “Let's give this one a proper border inspection.”

Natalie closed her notebook for the short walk. She wanted to carry one distinction without immediately burying it under another: longer could be valid. Preferred could be specific. A correction could say which of those things it meant.

## 7. The border before neon

The painted boundary was not straight. It wandered across the floor, around a potted fern, and through the center of a brass luggage scale. Someone had added a little bridge so a trolley could cross it without interrupting the line.

Henry inspected the scale. “Are we weighing electrons now?”

“No,” Ms Luminara said. “That is an ordinary office scale that has become involved in a graphic design decision.”

Jeff stepped over the bridge anyway. It had a railing, and he appreciated a structure that took its own small risk seriously.

On the near side stood lithium's record: atomic number three, neutral, ground-state configuration 1s² 2s¹. On the far side waited sodium. Neon stood directly beside the boundary, looking less like a place than a question.

The clerk asked Ramsay to select a core for lithium. He found lithium on the modern reference, then moved his finger back to helium. Helium represented 1s², leaving 2s¹ outside. The result, [He] 2s¹, expanded back to the supplied configuration and accounted for three electrons.

At the second handle, Ramsay asked Jeff to read the expansion without looking at his working. Jeff found the same three electrons. Ramsay compared the two records, then put a small pencil tick beside lithium on his own reference.

At neon, Ástríðr took a [Ne] case from the trolley.

“This contains exactly the whole configuration,” she said. “The count is ten. Nothing is missing.”

The clerk looked troubled. “The request says immediately preceding noble-gas core.”

Ástríðr did not put the case back yet. “So the case is meaningful, but it hasn't shown what this request asks me to show.”

Henry read the instruction aloud. Earlier meant lower atomic number, and the target was neon itself. The immediately preceding noble gas was helium. The requested form was [He] 2s² 2p⁶.

Natalie copied that line, then drew a small arrow back to 1s² 2s² 2p⁶. The arrow stood for expansion of notation. She labeled it that way. No physical electron had crossed their painted border.

Kàra stood with one foot on either side of the line. “I had thought the bracket changed when the row got full.”

“Tell us what you meant by row,” Ms Luminara said.

“The written line,” Kàra admitted. “Or the row of the periodic table. I wasn't keeping those separate.”

She stepped back and looked at the actual cards. Neon itself could be described fully, or with a helium core under this teaching convention. Sodium, with one more proton and one more electron in its neutral atom, had a configuration whose first ten electrons matched neon's occupied pattern. The [Ne] abbreviation now had something outside it to carry forward.

“The switch follows a configuration we can expand,” Kàra said. “It doesn't happen because the typography feels crowded.”

Ramsay ran a finger along the painted line without touching any card. “You could move this boundary on the floor and the atoms would remain as they were.”

“Please don't give the office ideas,” Ms Luminara said.

The fern, which had been leaning into the path, withdrew a leaf.

They crossed to sodium. Jeff selected [Ne] 3s¹, checked ten plus one, and opened it. The now-familiar full configuration returned. The demonstration felt easier, but not because he had stopped checking. He knew what the check was for.

Jackson turned the trolley around. “We should try going backward.”

The clerk hesitated. Trolleys apparently preferred forward progress. Jackson reversed it gently anyway. At sodium, the neon core worked for the requested nearest-earlier form. At neon, the same case no longer answered an immediately-preceding-core request. At lithium, the helium core was appropriate, and a neon core would already represent more electrons than the neutral atom possessed.

The reversal caught Natalie. She had been imagining a one-way staircase where the bracket acquired a new value forever. Going backward made the target matter again. The shorthand belonged to the species and the task, not to the learner's latest stop.

Ms Luminara opened the help drawer attached to the trolley. “No need to memorize the crossing by suspense,” she said. “Here is the explanation in one place.”

The card stated the convention directly. For these neutral-atom tasks, use the nearest noble gas with lower atomic number and retain the rest of the actual configuration. For a noble-gas target, an explicit preceding-core request uses the prior noble gas. Other contexts may simply refer to the complete configuration by its own noble-gas label.

Ástríðr set the [Ne] self-label beside neon rather than throwing it into the error bin. She attached a note: TEN-ELECTRON REFERENCE; DOES NOT SATISFY THIS PRECEDING-CORE EXERCISE.

“That is much more specific than wrong,” she said.

The clerk's shoulders, if brackets could be said to possess shoulders, relaxed.

They stood together beside the fern for a moment. Behind them, the held machine was quiet. Ahead, the office extended into further halls. Natalie could feel the temptation to hurry toward the next distinction before this one had become familiar. Instead, she read her own line again.

“The abbreviation follows the inventory,” she said. “The inventory doesn't follow my need for a tidy line.”

Ramsay looked pleased, though he offered no grand speech. He picked up the trolley handle and asked where a visitor might obtain tea.

## 8. Tea beside the observation window

The tea room had no conveyor belt. This was its finest feature.

A long window overlooked the office floor. From there they could see the held batch, the working counters, and a small door with ENERGY on its frosted glass. The door was open, but the room beyond it remained dim. Jeff noticed it and chose a chair where he could keep it in view without being required to enter.

Ms Luminara set the umbrella in a stand. A label on the handle declared that it contained one umbrella. Henry looked into the stand to check whether the joke had acquired another layer.

“It hasn't,” Ms Luminara said. “You may have tea.”

Natalie warmed her hands around a mug. She had not realized how tightly she had been holding her pen. On the first page, the configuration still looked imposing. On the next three, it had become a set of operations she could reverse.

Ramsay sat opposite her. Ástríðr asked what his own investigations had offered instead of the office's conveniently supplied modern ledgers.

He did not pretend they had counted nuclear protons or read electron configurations. In the documented work of his period, separation methods and measurements of properties, including spectra, helped distinguish gases. The modern story they were learning belonged to later developments.

Ms Luminara placed an archive card on the table. It identified an 1898 letter by Ramsay and Morris Travers. Their earlier estimate of neon's density needed reconsideration after E. C. C. Baly identified helium lines in the sample's spectrum. The letter credited his observation and also cautioned that relative spectral-line intensity did not by itself give the amount of helium present.

“That is the kind of distinction I recognize,” Ramsay said in their imagined conversation. “Evidence that a constituent is present is not automatically a measurement of how much is present.”

Kàra looked toward the office's count windows. “Our windows give an exact integer because the exercise has supplied it. A glowing line in a real experiment doesn't come with a ready-made number of electrons printed underneath.”

“Right,” Ms Luminara said. “We are entering a supported model through selected records. The office should not pretend its bookkeeping replaces all the measurements that support atomic science.”

The guest's contribution changed the room. Until then, the references had felt like pieces of an answer key. Now Natalie could imagine them as compact endpoints of much longer scientific work. The point was not to rederive that work before using any notation. It was to remember what kind of help a reference provided.

Jackson had been looking at the notice SAME CONTENTS. LESS WRITING through the window. “A label could be perfectly copied and still attached to the wrong specimen,” he said.

Ramsay nodded. “Yes. Reversible copying protects one relationship. It doesn't settle every other relationship.”

Henry stirred his tea without drinking it. “Then our total check can't also be an identity check unless we know the atom is neutral.”

Natalie considered the statement. For a neutral atom, equal proton and electron counts connected atomic number to the electron total. If the species carried charge, she would need another piece of information. She remembered that rule from an earlier lesson, but she could not yet picture where the office might confuse it.

“We should keep the proton heading,” Natalie said. “Even after the bracket works.”

Kàra pulled her notebook closer. “I like a single clean number. I think I keep trying to make one number carry the whole situation.”

Ástríðr broke a biscuit in half and handed part to her. “You could let two numbers help.”

Kàra smiled. “Radical.”

They did not immediately turn the biscuit into an electron model. Ms Luminara seemed to regard this restraint as an achievement.

For several minutes, conversation drifted to the office itself. Jeff liked the local stop button. Jackson wanted the batch receipt kept beside it. Henry wondered why the brackets needed ties when none of the customers wore them. The left clerk, passing the tea-room door, said ties were useful for pointing to a line without smudging it.

Ramsay reached for the teapot and discovered it was empty. Jeff had been trying to catch the passing clerk's attention for the same reason. They carried it to the serving hatch together. When they returned, Ramsay put the full pot within Natalie's reach and sat down without opening another reference.

Natalie turned to the page where she had crossed out electrons lost. She could still read the first version underneath.

“I used to think a clean notebook meant I'd understood cleanly,” she said. “Today the crossed-out sentence is doing work. It tells me which kind of change we didn't make.”

Ms Luminara did not turn that into an instruction to disclose every past mistake. She only asked whether Natalie wanted a fresh page or room to keep writing below it.

“Below,” Natalie said.

A soft warning light appeared beyond the window. A parcel had reached a gate at the end of the hall. Its electron counter read ten. A mechanical arm had turned toward NEON STORAGE.

The parcel's other plate, clearly visible from the tea room, read ELEVEN PROTONS.

Henry set down his spoon.

“That's the relationship,” he said. “The one the total doesn't settle.”

Ástríðr was already standing, biscuit still in hand. Through the glass, they watched the gate flash WRONG ELEMENT.

Ramsay rose with her. “Then let us ask which observation it has mistaken for a name.”

## 9. The sodium sent toward neon

Ástríðr reached the gate before its arm completed the turn. She did not grab the moving parcel. She pressed HOLD, the same local control Jeff had found at the printer, and waited until the wheels stopped.

The gate continued to flash WRONG ELEMENT. It had a remarkable capacity to repeat a conclusion without adding evidence.

“Ten electrons,” said the gate. “Neon.”

“Eleven protons,” Ástríðr replied, pointing to the second plate. “Please read your whole label.”

The parcel was a demonstration record for a sodium ion. It carried two transparent ledgers, one for the nucleus and one for electrons. The nuclear ledger had eleven positive charge counters. The electron ledger had ten negative charge counters. A history strip showed that the earlier neutral-sodium record had contained eleven electrons.

Natalie's first impulse was to count the smaller column again. The ten was familiar from the neon bracket; the eleven seemed like a contradictory detail that might have been left behind by mistake.

Ramsay asked for the original record. This time Natalie was glad someone else had asked before she completed her thought.

The clerk produced it. Eleven protons and eleven electrons in neutral sodium. The modeled change removed one electron from the atom's electron account. The eleven protons remained unchanged.

“Ordinary ion formation changes the electron count,” Ms Luminara said. “It does not change the number of protons in the nucleus. The atomic number remains eleven, so the element remains sodium.”

Ramsay looked at the two nuclear ledgers. “Using your modern definition, identity remains in that unchanged column.”

“Yes,” Ms Luminara said. She laid the two nuclear ledgers edge to edge so their eleven counters lined up. “That column has not changed.”

Kàra placed a fingertip below each column and read the charges aloud. Eleven positive elementary charges, ten negative elementary charges. The result was one positive elementary charge overall.

“So Na⁺,” Kàra said. “One sodium ion with a net charge of plus one.”

Henry checked the printed superscript. “The plus sign belongs to the whole species. It isn't a count of sodium atoms, and it isn't a subshell occupancy.”

He pointed back to the configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶. Those upper numbers counted electrons in subshells. The superscript in Na⁺ described net electric charge. Similar positions on a page did not give symbols identical jobs.

The gate displayed TEN ELECTRONS CONFIRMED.

“That part is right,” Natalie said. “The inference after it fails.”

She was pleased to find herself saying the distinction without first needing to feel confident about every related question. The ten was useful. It told them the electron count and helped them check the configuration. It did not erase the proton evidence.

Jeff fetched neutral neon's card from the trolley. Ten protons, ten electrons, net charge zero. They placed it beside Na⁺. Both electron configurations read 1s² 2s² 2p⁶. Their proton columns differed.

“Isoelectronic,” Ms Luminara said. “In this atomic comparison, the species have the same number of electrons, and these ground-state configurations match. That does not make them the same element or give them identical properties.”

Ástríðr opened the [Ne] case on the sodium-ion record. It expanded correctly. She closed it again, looking thoughtful.

“This is the most dangerous-looking success we've had,” she said. “The bracket does its job exactly. The gate thinks that means it has answered a different question.”

Jackson followed the gate's track toward neon storage. The receiving cabinet indexed records by proton identity. If the parcel entered under neon, its eleven-proton plate would conflict with the cabinet's ten-proton records. A clerk might then alter the plate to make the paperwork agree, destroying the evidence of the original routing mistake.

“We need the gate to stop renaming things from electron totals,” Jackson said. “And the storage clerk needs to know this parcel's proton plate is correct.”

Ramsay wrote a message for the receiving desk. It named the parcel, the observed discrepancy, and the reason for holding it. He asked Henry to check the modern terminology before he sent it through the office's message tube.

Henry changed one phrase. Ramsay had written same electronic arrangement means equivalent atom. He crossed out equivalent atom himself after Henry pointed to the proton ledgers.

“Matching configuration labels,” Ramsay said, trying again. “Different nuclear charge.”

Natalie watched the guest revise without ceremony. Knowledge from one field of work had helped him ask for original records. It had not made every new phrase safe on first use.

The gate finally offered two separate switches: IDENTIFY ELEMENT FROM PROTON COUNT and CALCULATE NET CHARGE FROM PROTONS AND ELECTRONS. Ms Luminara left both switches unpressed.

“Who wants to try the corrected route?” she asked.

Ástríðr did. She read eleven protons, selected sodium, compared eleven with ten, and selected plus one. The gate replaced WRONG ELEMENT with SODIUM ION, Na⁺. It opened a route toward the ion records, keeping the same configuration card attached.

The parcel rolled a few inches and stopped again.

A third panel lit up beneath it.

ELECTRON DEPARTURE UNMATCHED.

Jackson looked back at the history strip. “We know it isn't in the atom. Where did we put it?”

## 10. The third column

The clerk checked the floor around the gate as though an electron counter might have fallen under a wheel. Jeff found a paperclip. Kàra found a torn corner of an old job slip. Neither item explained the new warning.

Ms Luminara drew a chalk rectangle on the workbench around the atom's two ledgers.

“This is the system boundary in our simplified accounting picture,” she said. “Inside it, we count what belongs to the sodium atom or ion. If an electron leaves this system, where can our record acknowledge it?”

“Outside the rectangle,” Jackson said.

He looked at the bench beyond the chalk. It was empty because the display had never included a place for the surroundings.

Ramsay asked the clerk for another ledger. The clerk produced a particularly handsome book titled MISCELLANEOUS.

“More specific,” Henry said.

The second attempt read SURROUNDINGS.

They opened it beside the sodium record. A departure receipt lay folded inside the cover. One electron had been removed from the atom's account, but the receiving column had been left blank. The display had focused so tightly on the ion that it made the electron seem to vanish.

Kàra held the receipt against the chalk boundary. “The minus sign doesn't get canceled by crossing a line.”

“No,” Ms Luminara said. “The electron retains its negative charge. The remaining atom has one less negative charge and therefore a net positive charge.”

Natalie drew the change in her notebook using three columns. Before: sodium's eleven positive and eleven negative charges; surroundings with no transferred electron counted in this little model. After: sodium's eleven positive and ten negative charges; surroundings with the transferred electron's one negative charge.

The total charge of the modeled atom-plus-transferred-electron system was unchanged. The sodium part had become positive because the system under that particular heading no longer included one electron.

“The positive ion and the negative electron balance if we include both,” Natalie said.

Henry nodded. “And that doesn't mean they are the same object.”

Jackson drew a line beneath the unchanged total. “Does the whole account always have to come to zero?”

“It comes to zero here because we began with a neutral atom and counted no other charges,” Ms Luminara said. “A complete system can begin with a nonzero charge. Conservation means that its total stays the same through the process, provided no charge crosses its outer boundary. It doesn't require that total to be zero.”

The clerk entered the receiving count. ELECTRON DEPARTURE UNMATCHED disappeared. But the machine beside the ledger immediately printed PROCESS EXPLAINED.

Jeff, still holding the paperclip, frowned. “Have we explained how the electron left?”

Ramsay leaned over the receipt. “We have accounted for a transfer. We have not described the apparatus or the energy exchange.”

Ms Luminara replaced the stamp with CHARGE BOOKKEEPING COMPLETE. “Exactly. An actual ionization process needs a physical account. Our diagram specifies an initial species, a final species, and an electron outside the atom. It does not by itself establish how that change happened or whether a proposed real process is energetically possible.”

Kàra looked toward the ENERGY door across the hall. The warning had changed her curiosity. Before, energy had sounded like another difficult topic behind a door. Now it named a missing part of a specific story.

“Could we reverse the arrow just by moving the token back?” she asked.

“For the bookkeeping illustration, we can restore the initial counts,” Ms Luminara said. “That is a reversal of our model operation. In a real physical process, electron capture and the associated energy exchange need their own description. Sliding a counter is not a mechanism.”

They tried the ledger reversal anyway, carefully named. Jeff moved the transferred negative-charge counter back inside the chalk boundary. Sodium's electron count returned to eleven, its net charge to zero, and the surroundings column lost that one transferred electron. Then he moved it outward again, restoring the Na⁺ record.

The physical motion of his hand made the signs feel less paradoxical. Removing something negative left the atom's balance more positive. Nothing needed to want anything. Nobody had to describe sodium as happy, lonely, or in pursuit of noble-gas approval.

Ástríðr had been quiet. Now she returned from the printer carrying the faulty shorthand ticket from the envelope's front pocket. She laid it beside the ion record, leaving its serial mark exposed.

“These looked like the same story when we said losing electrons,” she said. “But over here, the machine left terms off a neutral-sodium description. Over here, we are representing a different electron count for a charged sodium species.”

Natalie traced the two headings. One error required fixing notation. The other account required preserving a physical distinction between neutral atom and ion. If she automatically called every smaller electron total an ion, she would excuse a bad label. If she automatically called every changed count a typo, she would erase ion formation.

“Read what change is actually claimed,” Natalie said.

Ramsay copied that sentence onto the inside of the surroundings ledger. Ástríðr returned the faulty ticket to its pocket above the printer before the sodium-ion parcel moved on.

Jackson asked the clerk to keep the surroundings ledger attached to the demonstration, even when the parcel moved. A detached ledger would be easy to admire and easy to forget.

The clerk supplied a strap. Jeff checked that it held both books without covering either heading. He seemed more adventurous now that the boundary had become explicit. Instead of standing back from the ion model, he volunteered to carry it through the next gate.

“Tell me where the extra column goes,” he said. “Then I'm happy to move it.”

Ms Luminara opened the route. “Beside the system it completes.”

The parcel rolled forward. Its electron count was ten, its proton count eleven, and its name was still sodium. This time, the negative charge outside its boundary had a place in the record too.

## 11. Two numbers above the line

The ion-record hall was quieter than the main office. Its shelves held paired folders: a species record and an account of the change being represented. The clerks had learned, apparently, that a neat endpoint could conceal an untidy journey.

Jeff set the sodium demonstration on a table. Another parcel waited beside it under the name MAGNESIUM. Its nucleus ledger showed twelve protons. Its electron ledger showed ten electrons. The charge field was blank.

“I'd like to do this one without having you tell me the sign first,” Jeff said. “But leave the reference here.”

Ms Luminara placed it beside his hand.

Jeff counted the positive and negative charges. Twelve positive units, ten negative units, leaving two positive units. He wrote Mg²⁺ on the charge card.

The clerk reached for a stamp. Jeff stopped it gently.

“Let me explain what the two means,” he said.

He pointed to the superscript. The 2+ described the net charge on one magnesium ion, measured in units of the elementary charge. It did not mean two magnesium atoms. Twelve protons identified magnesium. Neon had ten protons, so this ten-electron species was not neon.

Henry smiled. “That is the entire job of that two.”

At the neighboring desk, Natalie found a different magnesium card: the neutral ground-state configuration [Ne] 3s². She placed it beside Mg²⁺ and immediately understood why the symbols had once blurred in her mind. Both had a raised two; both concerned electrons; both belonged to magnesium. But the signs and the things being described were different.

“In 3s², the two counts electrons occupying that subshell,” Natalie said. “In Mg²⁺, the two plus tells the species' net charge.”

She copied both, leaving enough space that the superscripts could not crowd their neighbors. Good notation was not merely decorative. If the plus sign disappeared, the second expression would become ambiguous in a plain-text setting.

Kàra asked whether they could write a full electron ledger for the neutral magnesium atom before comparing it with the ion. The supplied neutral record showed twelve electrons, with the configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s². Two electrons beyond the neon core. The ion record had ten.

“The counts differ by two,” Kàra said. “The proton count does not change.”

Ms Luminara agreed. “For this simple bookkeeping comparison, neutral magnesium becoming Mg²⁺ means two fewer electrons associated with the atom. The full physical route and its energy exchanges are further questions.”

Ramsay asked for the surroundings ledger before anyone could forget it. They entered two transferred electrons in the model's receiving account. The ion's +2 and the two electrons' combined −2 preserved the overall charge balance of that represented transfer.

Ástríðr watched the two counters move. “The phrase loses two electrons sounds like an accident,” she said. “As if we ought to find them under the bench.”

“Language brings its own pictures,” Ms Luminara said. “Here loss means a decrease in the electron count of the specified species. The surroundings can gain those electrons.”

The clerk offered a magnifying glass for searching under the bench anyway. Ástríðr accepted it and used it to read the tiny charge sign instead.

Jackson had found another failure waiting in the routing instructions. One line said ALL TEN-ELECTRON RECORDS SHARE A STORAGE LABEL. He put the sodium-ion card, neutral-neon card, and magnesium-ion card below it.

“They can share a comparison folder,” Jackson said. “They can't share an element identity.”

Ramsay proposed keeping the shared electron configuration as an index field rather than the name of the specimen. Henry checked the suggestion. It allowed the office to retrieve isoelectronic examples without erasing their separate proton counts and charges.

For a moment, the three records looked almost irritatingly similar. Ten electrons in each. Different positive charges in the nuclei. Different net charges for the whole species. The class had to resist the convenience of letting the same ten swallow every difference.

Natalie looked from sodium to magnesium. “I think I expected a bracket to finish the identification,” she said. “Now I can see it is one field in a larger description.”

“And a useful field,” Ramsay said. “We need not stop using it because it cannot answer everything.”

Jeff slid his completed Mg²⁺ card into the correct pocket. He had wanted to do one case himself; he had used a reference and asked for time; the case was now complete. Ms Luminara did not give him three harder ones as the price of succeeding.

Instead, she asked whether he wanted to continue carrying the surroundings ledger or leave it with the clerk.

“Leave it where it belongs,” Jeff said. “I don't want the system to work only when I happen to be holding the missing piece.”

Jackson nodded at that. They secured the ledger to the display and checked that its heading could still be read from the front.

Across the hall, the ENERGY door gave a small creak. It had not moved on its own. Kàra had reached it and put a hand on the frame.

“I want to know what our balanced accounts still haven't told us,” she said.

Ms Luminara joined her. “Then let's look under the floorboards. The door will remain open, and anyone can return to the table whenever they have enough.”

## 12. What a balanced ledger cannot buy

The room beyond the ENERGY door contained a platform, a handrail, and a large window onto a dark model space. A notice at eye level read NO PHYSICAL EXPERIMENT IS RUNNING HERE. The displays were representations built to separate questions, not a chamber in which the class would actually strip electrons from atoms.

Jeff read the notice, tested the handrail, and entered. He did not need to prove that caution had disappeared before curiosity could proceed.

On the first panel, neutral sodium's eleven-electron record stood beside Na⁺ and an electron outside the atom. The charge balance was correct. Underneath, an energy field remained blank.

Kàra pointed to it. “If everything balances electrically, why isn't that enough?”

“Charge conservation tells us one constraint,” Ms Luminara said. “Energy accounting supplies another. For an isolated ground-state sodium atom in the gas phase, removing an electron requires energy input. The existence of a filled configuration in the resulting ion does not make that electron depart without cost.”

Natalie felt an old sentence loosen in her memory: sodium wants to get rid of one electron. It had been easy to remember and hard to make precise. It gave the atom an intention, then treated that intention as an energy source.

“So if we say a particular compound forms favorably, we need the other parts of that process too,” Natalie said. “We can't decide from the isolated removal step alone.”

“Yes,” Ms Luminara said. “Interactions with other species and the overall conditions matter. Today we are keeping that wider chemistry outside our calculation. We can still refuse to let a bookkeeping shortcut pretend it has explained the whole process.”

Ramsay examined the blank field rather than filling it with a guess. “Then the honest record says what has been checked and what remains unspecified.”

Henry opened a small drawer beneath the panel. It held modern reference records for neutral neon and singly ionized sodium. Both had ten electrons and the same written ground-state configuration. Their next ionization energies were different.

The class did not need numerical values to see the point of the comparison. The nuclei carried different charges. Matching electron-count and occupancy notation had not erased the interactions that distinguished the species.

“Same number of passengers,” Ástríðr said, “different vehicle?”

“A useful first warning,” Ms Luminara replied. “But do not picture electrons as travelers unaffected by their surroundings. Their states depend on the system, including the nuclear attraction and electron–electron interactions. Even when the occupancy labels match, many physical properties can differ.”

Ástríðr revised her analogy aloud. “The count is shared. The conditions are not all shared.”

Kàra gripped the rail and looked through the dark window. A model appeared: a positive nucleus symbol, a surrounding probability representation, and a line indicating the system boundary. No small balls followed circles. There was nothing she could chase with her eyes.

“I keep wanting a little orbit so I can see where the energy goes,” Kàra said.

Ms Luminara did not substitute a more elaborate cartoon. “We can represent an energy difference without claiming a classical path for the electron. The atomic quantum model has allowed states and probabilities, not miniature planetary tracks. We will look at that distinction more closely later if it helps.”

Jeff stepped back from the window. “I've got enough for now,” he said. “The charge check doesn't pay the energy bill.”

“Keep that,” Ms Luminara said. “You do not need the full quantum theory to use the charge ledger correctly.”

He returned to the table outside, where Jackson was comparing the transport records. Ástríðr went with him. Neither departure was announced as opting out of learning. They carried a bounded conclusion into useful work elsewhere.

Natalie stayed. The window had become still. She realized she was waiting for a visual that would make the whole subject feel obvious at once.

“Could we make a force diagram?” she asked.

“For a carefully chosen classical model, yes,” Ms Luminara said. “But it would not by itself derive the ground-state electron configuration of a multielectron atom. We should say which question the diagram can help us ask.”

Ramsay looked from Natalie to the window. “That resembles the caution we just discussed about spectral lines. A method can reveal something important without yielding every quantity one hopes to read from it.”

Kàra released the rail. “So the floorboards don't hide one simple lever.”

“No,” Ms Luminara said. “Sometimes they reveal why the upstairs handle works only within its stated job.”

They spent a little longer comparing the two ten-electron records. Natalie wrote neither identical nor utterly unrelated. She wrote isoelectronic, then noted that proton number, charge, and energies still mattered. The new word became useful because it preserved a specific similarity without claiming a universal sameness.

When they left, Ramsay paused at the doorway to gather the two ten-electron records. He kept their species headings facing outward. Natalie collected the blank energy worksheet and laid it beside them on the table.

Ms Luminara left the door open. Jeff could see it from his chair, and this time it looked like an available place rather than a demand.

On the table, the surroundings ledger was complete. Beside it lay an empty energy worksheet marked NOT SPECIFIED IN THIS MODEL.

Nobody filled the blank merely to make the page look finished.

## 13. A place to set the pencil down

The office had a room for unclaimed luggage and another for unfinished questions. The second was smaller, with better chairs.

Ms Luminara led them into it before they resumed work on the compacting machine. A sign explained that questions could remain overnight without accumulating a fee. Underneath, someone had written PLEASE TAKE YOUR OWN WORRY HOME ONLY IF YOU STILL NEED IT.

“That last part is ambitious,” Natalie said.

“Office signage occasionally overreaches,” Ms Luminara replied. She turned the sign around so its simpler side faced the room: YOU MAY STOP HERE.

Natalie sat down. Her notebook was open to the charge ledger, but she did not read it immediately. The room had a radiator that clicked as it warmed, and the rain outside had become a steady hush. She could see her own handwriting changing across the day: tight at the beginning, looser where she had found room to revise.

Henry lowered himself into a chair beside her. He had spent much of the morning making distinctions, asking what signs meant, and improving labels. Now he rubbed his eyes.

“I would like the next thing shown to me before I have to name it,” he said.

Ms Luminara nodded. “That is a perfectly good way to begin.”

It mattered to Natalie that Henry said this without having to become a different person. Precision did not require him to be alert every second. She had sometimes confused a habit of careful thought with an obligation to perform it continuously.

Across the room, Ramsay studied the return conditions on his visitor's card. The paper had curled in his pocket. He flattened it beneath an empty saucer.

“Will I remember any of this?” he asked.

“A dreamlike residue,” Ms Luminara said. “Wonder, perhaps. Not a completed set of future results to publish in your own year.”

Ramsay considered that limit. “Then I should make myself useful here while the details remain available.”

“You already have,” Jeff said. “You asked for the original when we were about to trust the gate.”

Ramsay inclined his head. Jeff drew his chair a little closer to the radiator, leaving room for the guest's feet. For a while they watched a drop work its way down the window. At a joining strip it divided; one part continued, and the other clung to the glass.

Kàra raised a finger as though she might explain it. Henry had closed his eyes.

“Later,” she said softly, and settled back.

Natalie let her pencil rest across the notebook. The held batch was safe. Nobody needed an answer from this room for a few minutes.

## 16. Under the office floor

Below the luggage counters, the shelves ended.

The class stood in a gallery where the ceiling showed the underside of the office model: wooden supports, ink pipes, and hinges. Beyond the far rail, those familiar objects dissolved into diagrams of quantum states. Ms Luminara let the contrast remain visible. The room did not pretend that a walk downward had carried them literally inside an atom.

“These are representations of a modern quantum model,” she said. “They help us say what the configuration notation means and where the office furniture misleads.”

Jeff looked upward through a glass panel. He could see the empty 3d tray above, then the display below that bore the same label. “So the tray counts an occupancy. This display concerns states?”

“Yes,” Ms Luminara said. “An orbital is a spatial wavefunction used in this quantum model. From it we obtain a probability distribution for finding an electron in space. It is not a little path that an electron follows around the nucleus.”

Kàra walked beside a diagram whose outline was softly shaded. She had expected the picture to stop being mysterious when she understood that it was a probability representation. Instead, a different difficulty appeared.

“If I shade it darker, am I putting more electrons there?” she asked.

“Not necessarily,” Ms Luminara said. “The legend must tell us what the shading represents. A probability density for one electron and a total electron density are related ideas but not interchangeable labels. Here the display is a qualitative orbital probability representation. It is not a photograph of a cloud made from pieces of electron.”

Kàra read the legend carefully. The act felt familiar after the gate. She had again been trying to infer the whole meaning from one visual feature.

Natalie found the relationship between shells, subshells, and orbitals on a separate panel. The leading number in a term such as 3p labeled the shell. The p identified a subshell type. A p subshell contained three orbitals, and the Pauli principle allowed at most two electrons in an orbital, with opposite spins. Thus a p subshell could hold up to six electrons. An s subshell contained one orbital and could hold two. A d subshell contained five and could hold ten.

“Then the six in 2p⁶ is a total for that subshell,” Natalie said. “It isn't six in one orbital.”

“Correct,” Ms Luminara said.

The panel did not place little turning tops beside the word spin. Henry would have appreciated that. Ms Luminara mentioned that spin was an intrinsic quantum property; the familiar word should not be taken as proof of a tiny classical sphere physically rotating.

Ramsay turned back to the glossary Ms Luminara had given him at the first counter. Beside subshell, he added three orbitals for p, then checked that entry against the panel. The later theory still required unfamiliar words; he was beginning to know where to look when they caught him.

“Why not give us one permanent order?” Jeff asked.

“Because it would overpromise,” Ms Luminara said. “Electrons interact with the nucleus and with one another. The useful introductory filling sequence for our selected neutral first-twenty examples does not become a universal rule for all atoms, ions, excited states, or transition metals.”

“For neutral potassium and calcium in the supplied ground-state records, the occupied configurations include 4s before any 3d occupancy,” she said.

“That is the claim we can carry back without enlarging it,” Ms Luminara replied.

“I don't need every door today,” she said.

Ramsay closed the nearest one gently. “Then let us return with the part that changes the work above.”

## 17. A repair that asks the right question

The compacting machine looked smaller after the gallery. It still had its brass trim, impressive rollers, and bell, but nobody mistook its confidence for a property of atoms.

Jackson placed a clean job slip at the entrance. Before any configuration entered, the clerk would record the stated species, the supplied or verified state, and the requested representation. The machine's first task was to read that information, not to guess it from whichever number looked most convenient.

Ramsay asked to inspect the process in the order a new clerk would encounter it. He stood on the receiving side of the counter rather than beside the controls. From there, several notes the class had written were hidden behind the machine.

“I cannot use an instruction I cannot see,” he said.

Ástríðr moved the important headings higher. The captions she had placed at the front were readable, but the raised inspection tray hid the instructions below them. “We don't have to ban it,” he said. “We need to know what was checked before we use it.”

Total count asked whether all electron occupancies summed appropriately for the stated species. Ground-state matching compared the occupied terms with the verified record when that was the claim being made. Proton identity and net charge were checked where relevant. A final space allowed the clerk to write that information was missing or a case lay outside the office's verified teaching scope.

Kàra read the list and frowned. “It looks like we made exactly the enormous chart Jackson warned us about.”

Jackson shook his head. “It isn't a quiz for every customer. It's a way for the clerk to know which question they're answering.”

The clerk returned 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ and the corresponding count of ten electrons represented by the abbreviation.

Natalie looked at the practical difference. A careful system could be selective. It did not need to bury a learner under every distinction chemistry might someday require.

The machine hesitated, its old neon-routing arm twitching once.

Ástríðr let out a small cheer. “It has learned to ask.”

Ms Luminara corrected gently. “In our fictional repair, we have given it a better rule for this kind of request. We should still inspect how it behaves. A useful question on one example is not proof that every possible case is handled.”

Jeff brought the faulty calcium proposal forward once more. “Will it tell me everything is wrong?”

Ramsay held the blank reverse of his ticket. “You have been quite firm about what I must leave behind.” He looked toward the suitcase containing the modern references.

“I meant what mattered to you here,” Natalie said. “Before the details fade.”

He considered her question. “The need to keep the subject of a check clear. I arrived wondering how a name could stand for a substance. Your office asked how a notation could stand for a configuration. Those are related questions about representation, but they do not use the same evidence.”

Kàra leaned against the window frame. “You didn't need our notation to discover neon.”

“No,” Ramsay said. “And the notation does not reenact the separation and measurement work. It offers a later account of atomic structure.”

On the folder inside the suitcase, Ms Luminara had kept the historical credit visible: Ramsay and Travers, neon, 1898; Ramsay and Rayleigh, argon, 1894. Those investigations already belonged to the world they would return him to. The modern briefing would remain with the class.

Jeff turned his demonstration copy of the faulty shorthand ticket over in his hand. “You helped us because you asked what had actually been established.”

“And you helped me by explaining the notation without treating my unfamiliarity as evidence that I knew nothing,” Ramsay replied.

Henry had been quiet since the final calcium check. Now he said, “I nearly treated precision as getting every word right before anyone could continue. The office would have become unusable.”

Ástríðr answered him gently. “You also found the two stamps. That made the explanation kinder because it made it more accurate.”

Henry nodded. He laid the two stamp impressions side by side on the empty seat, then tucked them into his notebook. Ástríðr reached past him to clear the aisle for Ms Luminara.

Jackson brought the class's copy of the checked-batch receipt onto the bus. The office retained its original. He put the copy in the luggage rack while everyone settled, with their return notes tucked behind it.

“You're taking a record of the repair,” Ramsay said.

“Enough to know which repair,” Jackson replied. “I don't want to remember this as we fixed everything.”

Natalie looked at the receipt's modest claim. The morning had felt large, but its defensible result was specific. A faulty abbreviation had been corrected, an ion misrouting had been repaired, and a count-only ground-state check had gained a needed comparison. The limits made the work easier to trust.

Before departure, Jackson took the receipt and return notes down from the rack and passed them to Ms Luminara. She packed them beside the reference folder and added both to the suitcase's contents slip.

“Seatbelts,” she said, “including our visitor.”

The bus folded the office canopy into a square of gray light. Its engine hummed, and the tunnel of labels returned. As they traveled, the modern reference cards in Ramsay's memory began to lose their sharp edges. He could still follow the conversation on the bus, but the future knowledge would not survive as a transferable scientific manuscript in 1904.

The London laboratory reappeared beyond the windscreen. The packing box sat on the same bench. Ramsay's own notebook remained closed where he had left it. The gas tubes were untouched.

Ms Luminara opened the door. The bus steps rested on a patch of floor that had not existed inside the wooden box a moment earlier.

Ramsay stood and felt in his pocket. He withdrew the visitor's ticket. Its front now read RETURN COMPLETED. The reverse showed only London and the year 1904.

“Your vehicle keeps rather strict accounts for something so extravagant,” he said.

“Extravagance is no excuse for losing a passenger,” Ms Luminara replied.

He stepped down, then turned to ask one more question. The door began to close, noticed him, and reopened apologetically. Ms Luminara waited.

“Will the little helium case still open when I am gone?” Ramsay asked.

Jeff grinned. “We'll check.”

That was enough. Ramsay returned to his bench. As the bus withdrew, the suitcase gave a tiny hiccup. A folded map shot through the open lid, sailed out of the window, and then, apparently remembering the luggage notice, sailed back. Ms Luminara caught it and returned it to the other five before fastening the case.

In the imagined laboratory, the long label still overlapped the adjacent compartment. Ramsay lifted it and moved it to its own tube. The ordinary problem remained ordinary. The documented history beyond that room continued without the bus supplying a missing discovery.

On board, Natalie watched the window become their own day's rain again. She had expected the guest to leave a quotation worthy of framing. Instead, he left an example of useful investigation: asking which observation supported which claim, accepting new help, and refusing to let an attractive description outrun its evidence.

She wrote his name above a short note and labeled the dialogue imagined. The work deserved credit, and the boundary deserved to travel with it.

## 20. The return receipt

The classroom was exactly the size it had been before the journey. Its chairs had no hidden conveyor belts, and the whiteboard offered no immediate access to another century. Jeff looked behind it anyway, then appeared satisfied.

Ms Luminara set the teacup-sized suitcase on the front table. Before opening it, she read the contents receipt aloud: one umbrella, six maps, one folder of modern references, one checked-batch receipt copied for the class, and the group's return notes.

Henry raised a hand. “The original receipt at departure had only the umbrella and maps.”

“Then we need the added items accounted for,” Ms Luminara said.

Jackson compared the office receipt with the suitcase slip. He pointed to the entries Ms Luminara had added before departure: the folder, the class's copy of the receipt, and their return notes. All three additions were accounted for.

Jeff opened the case. The umbrella emerged, followed by the six maps, the reference folder, and the papers. Then a very small tie slipped over the edge of the table.

Everyone looked at it.

Ástríðr remembered the right bracket clerk's bow. “That wasn't ours.”

Ms Luminara picked up the tie. “A final accounting discrepancy.”

The bus, still outside, gave a brief and hopeful honk.

They returned the tie before the first intersection could decide which place it belonged to. This required a short detour through a coat cupboard, a formal receipt from the right bracket clerk, and an assurance from Henry that they were returning an accessory rather than altering an electron configuration.

The clerk was visibly relieved. Without the tie, it had been forced to point at labels with its entire corner.

When the class finally sat down, Ms Luminara placed the three core reference cards on a side table. Jeff laid his demonstration copy of [He] 3s¹ beside them and wrote the original heading above it: NEUTRAL SODIUM. GROUND STATE.

Natalie opened her notebook to the first page. SAME CONTENTS. LESS WRITING. Underneath, her original empty line now held a method: expand it and compare.

The method had become richer during the day. She could compare not only the total but the actual occupied terms. She could ask which species the description concerned and which convention the task requested. She could look for a surroundings account when the model represented an electron leaving a system.

Jeff slid the faulty demonstration copy toward her. Its bracket was still neat. Its final term was still familiar. In the quiet classroom, Natalie could feel why she had nearly accepted it beneath the machine's ringing bell.

She fetched the helium reference, opened [He] to 1s², and wrote 3s¹ alongside it. Three electrons represented. Under the heading for neutral sodium, eight were missing from the written inventory.

“Could I keep helium and repair the rest?” Jeff asked.

Natalie wrote 2s² 2p⁶ between the two terms. The longer form now expanded to the full sodium reference. She put it beside [Ne] 3s¹ rather than crossing it out.

“This one preserves the configuration too,” she said. “Which form did the job ask for?”

Jeff turned the copy over. The clerk had included the nearest-earlier-core instruction. Natalie pointed to the neon form and left both valid expansions visible. She had needed the reference, but she had not needed the bell.

Kàra drew two versions of the sodium configuration. One was fully expanded; the other used [Ne]. She connected them with an arrow labeled change of representation. Beside them, she drew neutral sodium and Na⁺ with an electron in a surroundings column. That arrow had a different label: modeled electron-count change with physical process unspecified.

“I wanted those arrows to mean the same thing at first,” Kàra said. “Now I can see where the confusion started.”

Ástríðr looked at the drawings. “And you can keep both arrows. You don't have to give up the useful picture to correct its job.”

Henry wrote the meanings of three ways a count entered the notation: the raised two in 3s², the raised two plus in Mg²⁺, and the occupancy hidden inside a [He] case. The first counted electrons in a subshell. The second gave net charge on a species in elementary-charge units. The bracket represented a configuration containing two electrons. Their related arithmetic did not make the symbols interchangeable.

Jeff borrowed the helium card and expanded it once, quietly. Then he put it back.

“Still opens,” he said.

Natalie smiled at the callback to Ramsay's last question. The answer did not depend on the guest remaining in the room. That was part of what made the method useful.

Jackson pinned the checked-batch receipt beside the ordinary luggage rack. Henry changed the rack's temporary label from SMALL THINGS to COMPACT DESCRIPTIONS, then added a smaller note beneath it: ALSO COATS, IF NECESSARY.

Ms Luminara sat on the edge of the table rather than standing behind it. “What do you want to carry forward?” she asked.

Natalie did not try to speak for everyone. “That a correct count can be a real success and still not settle a ground-state claim,” she said. “I can keep the successful part of my reasoning while making the next correction.”

Kàra chose the system boundary. “If I stop drawing something, I need to ask whether it left the system or only left the page.”

Ástríðr chose the two stamps: inventory preserved and requested form met. Henry chose the question about which job a symbol was doing. Jackson chose the receiving receipt, because a repair needed to arrive where the error would otherwise be used. Jeff chose the local hold button and the help drawer.

Nobody's answer had to include every topic. The differences made their shared memory more useful.

The room grew quiet. Outside, the rain stopped. Natalie wrote [Ne] 3s¹ one last time, then expanded it underneath. The notation no longer felt like an incantation that had to be remembered whole. It had seams she could open, a count she could defend, and a stated species whose identity did not disappear inside a bracket.

She left the deeper questions on their own page. They were genuine questions, not evidence that the day's understanding was counterfeit. Another journey could return to energies, quantum states, or why a particular chemical process occurred under particular conditions.

On the table, the faulty demonstration copy lay beneath its repaired versions. Natalie placed the helium reference alongside them for whoever wanted to open the comparison next.

Ms Luminara folded the contents receipt and put it into the little suitcase. She checked the table for one last stray item, found only a biscuit crumb, and left that to the ordinary cleaning arrangements of the classroom.

“A shortcut should shorten the journey,” she said, “without losing the passengers.”

She closed the case gently. This time, nothing at all fell out.
