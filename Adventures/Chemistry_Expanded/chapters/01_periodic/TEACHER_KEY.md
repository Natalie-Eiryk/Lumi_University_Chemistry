## Purpose and prerequisites

The central outcome is to distinguish a family clue from a unique identification while keeping element identity tied to proton count. The chapter develops partial versus complete configurations, the role of neutral/ground-state scope, shell/subshell/orbital distinctions, evidence versus interpretation, and visible handling of unresolved or conflicting cases. Whole-number addition suffices for the core puzzle. Quantum and historical extensions require no derivation or memorized historical dates.

## Feedback approach

Use “established,” “partly established,” and “not yet shown” for specific claims rather than a single score. Check whether the response:

- preserves the stated scope;
- gives each symbol its correct role;
- accounts for all candidates and counts;
- distinguishes observation, reference lookup, and inference;
- explains why new evidence changes the result;
- states a relevant model limit without making the explanation unusable.

A correct noun with unsupported certainty needs a reasoning repair. A wrong noun with correct arithmetic may need an occupancy or scope repair rather than repeated addition. Once a learner has repaired the distinction, acknowledge the current answer; do not keep correcting the earlier one.

## luminara.adventure.001.q01

**Worked answer:** The clue is insufficient for a unique identification because the prompt supplies two neutral ground-state first-twenty atoms that fit ns² np⁴: oxygen with 2s² 2p⁴ and sulfur with 3s² 3p⁴. Period 2 would choose O; period 3 would choose S. An independently established atomic number, O = 8 or S = 16, would also distinguish them. A complete electron count would do so under the neutral condition.

**Reasoning check:** Naming one candidate proves only that it fits, not that no alternative fits. Naming a possible added clue without showing how it separates O and S is incomplete but repairable.

**Feedback option:** “Oxygen fits. What excludes sulfur before the extra observation arrives?” If both are already retained, move forward; do not repeat that prompt.

**Sources and scene:** S01, S03, S07; [scene 17](STORY.md#ch01-scene-17).

<a id="q02"></a>
## luminara.adventure.001.q02

**Worked answer:** Seventeen protons fixes chlorine. With eighteen electrons, net charge is 17 − 18 = −1 in units of the positive elementary charge, so the species is Cl⁻, the chloride ion. Argon has eighteen protons, not seventeen. Equal electron counts do not make these species the same element. The original neutral-atom clue does not apply to this charged ion without changing the stated case.

**Reasoning check:** The proton count establishes the element before charge is calculated. Do not identify it as “a negative argon” or silently move it to a new periodic-table element box.

**Model limit:** This ledger does not by itself explain the energetics or likelihood of a real electron-transfer process. It assumes the supplied proton/electron counts are correct. A real transfer must also account for the electron's source or destination; conserving complete-system charge does not require the total to be zero.

**Sources and scene:** S01, S06, S08; [scene 14](STORY.md#ch01-scene-14).

<a id="q03"></a>
## luminara.adventure.001.q03

**Worked answer:** The 5 in 3p⁵ counts five electrons in the 3p subshell. It does not count every electron in the atom. The complete configuration gives 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 5 = 17 electrons. If the atom is neutral, proton count equals electron count, so Z = 17. Without neutrality or other charge information, that electron total alone does not determine atomic number.

**Reasoning check:** Keep the principal label 3 out of the electron sum. Calling 3p one “shell” loses the distinction between the n = 3 shell and its s/p/d subshell categories.

**Sources and scene:** S02, S03, S06; [scene 5](STORY.md#ch01-scene-05).

## luminara.adventure.001.q04

**Possible two-sentence answer:** “Members of a main-group column often share useful chemical patterns related to their outer electronic structures, but F and Cl show that a matching outer pattern does not require identical first ionization energies. A family-address analogy can represent shared features and missing identifying information, but it does not explain electron states, energies, or real chemical interactions.”

**Reasoning check:** Accept other accurate examples and metaphors with stated limits. No personal disclosure is needed. The aim is the distinction between a useful similarity and an absolute identity claim, not agreement with one preferred literary analogy.

**Model limit:** A named-property difference does not establish a universal ranking of reactivity. Avoid adding a new unverified factual comparison solely to make the response more elaborate.

**Sources and scene:** S01–S04, S09; [scene 16](STORY.md#ch01-scene-16).

<a id="q05"></a>
## luminara.adventure.001.q05

**Worked answer:** In 3p⁵, 3 is the principal quantum number; p is the subshell type; ⁵ reports five electrons in that 3p subshell. In Cl⁻, the minus sign reports overall charge −1. “Group 17” names a periodic-table column. “Atomic number 17” means seventeen protons and identifies chlorine.

The shared numeral for chlorine is a coincidence of two different labels. Fluorine is in group 17 but has atomic number 9, so the proposed universal rule fails on a provided counterexample.

**Feedback option:** If a learner identifies the principal number and subshell correctly but calls the superscript five a charge, ask them to name what object the superscript modifies. The same typographic placement can carry different meanings in different expressions.

**Sources and scenes:** S01–S03, S06; [scene 4](STORY.md#ch01-scene-04), [scene 11](STORY.md#ch01-scene-11), [scene 14](STORY.md#ch01-scene-14).

<a id="q06"></a>
## luminara.adventure.001.q06

**Worked answer:** F has 2 + 2 + 5 = 9 electrons in total. Its highest occupied principal level is n = 2, containing 2 + 5 = 7. Its shell-total summary is 2, 7. Cl has 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 5 = 17 electrons in total. Its n = 3 outer level contains 2 + 5 = 7. Its shell-total summary is 2, 8, 7.

The chlorine configuration has five occupied subshell terms but only three distinct occupied principal numbers: 1, 2, and 3. The s and p terms with the same n belong to the same principal shell.

**Model limit:** The shell-total summary suppresses subshell detail. Use it for the comparison it preserves; do not treat it as a universal filling algorithm or a picture of hard concentric walls.

**Sources and scene:** S02–S04; [scene 5](STORY.md#ch01-scene-05).

<a id="q07"></a>
## luminara.adventure.001.q07

**Worked answer:** The existence of an allowed subshell is different from occupancy of that subshell in a particular atom. Neutral ground-state chlorine leaves 3d unoccupied in the configuration model used here; that does not remove the allowed 3d category. The n = 2 shell has 2s and 2p, with capacities 2 + 6 = 8. The n = 3 shell has 3s, 3p, and 3d, with capacities 2 + 6 + 10 = 18.

These are possible capacities, not requirements to occupy every state before moving to a higher principal number. The ground-state arrangement also depends on allowed states, occupancy constraints, and the system's energies.

**Model limit:** No universal transition-metal filling/removal rule has been derived. The capacity calculation also does not imply that chlorine has eighteen electrons in n = 3.

**Sources and scene:** S03–S04; [scene 6](STORY.md#ch01-scene-06).

<a id="q08"></a>
## luminara.adventure.001.q08

**Worked answer:** The dots are separate simulated outcomes from many similarly prepared atoms, so connecting them does not trace one electron's orbit. Each modeled hydrogen atom has one electron; the accumulation of many trials does not put all those electrons inside one atom. The original letter's F-or-Cl uncertainty arises because the identifying evidence is incomplete. It is not a demonstrated superposition of elements and should not be equated with the position probability distribution.

**Reasoning check:** A strong answer distinguishes what is uncertain in each context. It need not claim a specific interpretation of quantum measurement, solve a wave equation, or say that electron counting has become probabilistic in this example.

**Model limit:** The hydrogen 1s illustration is deliberately simpler than a many-electron atom. Chlorine's configuration is not computed exactly from that gallery.

**Source and scene:** S04; [scene 7](STORY.md#ch01-scene-07).

<a id="q09"></a>
## luminara.adventure.001.q09

**Worked answer:** The supplied K and Ca configurations have 19 and 20 electrons respectively and nonzero 4s occupancy while 3d is unoccupied. They contradict the proposed “complete all n = 3 states first” instruction. The principal number labels a shell; it is not a standalone rule ordering all many-electron subshell energies.

Possible shell capacity asks how many electrons the available state categories can accommodate. Ground-state configuration asks for the lowest-energy allowed arrangement for the specified atom, respecting occupancy restrictions and interactions. The observed/referenced first-twenty sequence uses 4s after 3p for K and Ca.

**Model limit:** Do not generalize this into “4s is always below 3d” or a universal rule for removing electrons from transition-metal ions. Those broader cases were not established.

**Sources and scene:** S03–S05; [scene 8](STORY.md#ch01-scene-08).

## luminara.adventure.001.q10

**Worked answer:** The historical contribution is Moseley's use of characteristic X-ray spectra to establish regular relationships associated with elemental atomic number and nuclear charge. The later explanation identifies Z as proton count and uses modern quantum configuration/orbital language. The bus visit, speech, learning interactions, temporal briefing, and dreamlike return are invented.

His expertise helps the class ask for discriminating evidence, compare like measurements, and avoid treating a group label as an atomic number. It cannot supply an unprovided period when both F and Cl satisfy the actual clue. Authority changes neither the data nor the candidate set.

**Historical limits:** Do not attribute modern proton/configuration terminology to the visitor before the explicitly labeled briefing. Do not claim the bus causes his discoveries or that the schematic displays are recovered laboratory records. The narrative's visit is placed in an imagined Manchester moment in October 1913; no exact day or private motive is asserted as historical fact. Scene 10 labels its retrospective summary of the first paper, published in December 1913, before the visitor uses it; the wider 1914 survey is excluded. The paper's eventual contents are not a dated record of what he knew on the imagined October day. Pronunciation is not independently verified.

**Sources and scenes:** H01–H04; [scenes 9–12](STORY.md#ch01-scene-09).

<a id="q11"></a>
## luminara.adventure.001.q11

**Worked answer:** (a) Group 17 does not distinguish: both F and Cl are in it. (b) Period 2 distinguishes and selects F. (c) Atomic number 17 distinguishes and selects Cl. (d) The fictional door's paint brightness has no chemical data meaning and cannot select the atom.

A reference lookup says what is true of a named candidate. It becomes evidence for the unknown's identity only when a relevant observation or given condition is tied to that unknown and compared against the candidates. Knowing that Cl has Z = 17 does not prove that the unknown has Z = 17.

**Reasoning check:** Candidates should be excluded for failure to match evidence, not because one seems less familiar. Any alternative truly distinguishing supplied observation can be discussed, but do not quietly add one that the case never gave.

**Sources and scenes:** S01–S03; [scene 11](STORY.md#ch01-scene-11), [scene 13](STORY.md#ch01-scene-13).

## luminara.adventure.001.q13

**Worked answer:** The original clue was underdetermined because two candidates fit. This record is inconsistent: atomic number 17 means Cl, which is in period 3, so no element satisfies both the atomic-number and period entries together.

Conditionally, if Z = 17 is the reliable entry, the element is Cl and the period should be 3. If period 2 is reliable, the atomic number cannot be 17, but the period alone does not select a unique period-2 element. The useful response is to preserve the record, explain the conflict, check whether entries belong to the same case, and ask the source to verify them.

**Reasoning check:** A definition identifies what a claimed proton count would imply; it does not guarantee that a transcription of that count is correct. Do not silently replace whichever entry seems less fundamental.

**Sources and scene:** S01, S02, S06; [scene 18](STORY.md#ch01-scene-18).

<a id="q14"></a>
## luminara.adventure.001.q14

**Worked answer:** The total is 2 + 3 + 4 = 9, which is the correct total for neutral fluorine. The 2s³ term is invalid because an s subshell has one orbital and a maximum capacity of two electrons. The supplied neutral ground-state reference is 1s² 2s² 2p⁵.

Feedback should acknowledge the correct total and target the overfilled subshell. Repeatedly checking addition cannot fix an occupancy error. More generally, check (1) total electron count, (2) allowed occupancy, and (3) agreement with the stated ground/excited-state requirement separately.

**Model limit:** Passing an electron-total check is not sufficient to validate a configuration. Merely moving an electron to any unfilled category is also not proof that a ground-state arrangement has been obtained.

**Sources and scene:** S02–S04; [scene 18](STORY.md#ch01-scene-18).

<a id="q15"></a>
## luminara.adventure.001.q15

**Worked answer:** F and Cl have different supplied first ionization energies despite belonging to the same group and sharing the ns² np⁵ outer pattern. Therefore, the claim that all group members behave identically is too strong.

First ionization energy is the minimum energy required to separate one electron from an isolated neutral gaseous ground-state atom, leaving the singly positive ion in its ground state. The supplied eV values refer to one atom's ionization. The two values do not establish electron-addition energies, a rate for a named reaction, a universal reactivity ordering, identical behavior in compounds, or the uselessness of group patterns. Any two relevant unsupported extrapolations are sufficient.

**Precision:** Values are approximate, rounded from the cited NIST pages. No extra significant figures or calculation are required. The story does not operate with real F₂/Cl₂ gas samples and does not infer a substance's properties from a fictional door.

**Sources and scene:** S02, S09; [scene 16](STORY.md#ch01-scene-16).

## luminara.adventure.001.q16

**Worked answer:** (a) The original clue plus period 3 uniquely retains Cl within scope: report the result and supporting conditions. (b) The original clue before the period retains F and Cl: preserve both candidates and request distinguishing evidence. (c) Z = 17 with period 2 is conflicting: explain the inconsistency and verify the inputs. Alternatively, 1s² 2s³ 2p⁴ is an invalid configuration: identify the overfilled 2s term and supply the relevant capacity/reference support.

**Possible final sentence:** “Keeping both candidates was warranted before the row arrived; once a reliable period-3 observation excluded F, choosing Cl was warranted.”

**Reasoning check:** Confidence should track the evidence's scope. The goal is neither perpetual doubt nor quick certainty. Accept other chapter examples that correctly distinguish the states and next steps.
