## 1. The bus with a front porch

The bus had grown a front porch overnight.

Natalie noticed the porch before she noticed the slippers. There were two of those, both large enough to shelter a bicycle, tucked around the front wheels. A brass letterbox leaned toward the passenger door. Someone had trained a vine up its post, and every leaf curled into a question mark.

On the destination board, the words SOMEWHERE PRECISE shone through the morning mist.

Natalie stopped with her notebook under her arm. She had brought the notebook because writing a thing down sometimes made enough room to think about it. She had also brought two pencils, because she had once taught physics and knew how much instructional confidence could be defeated by a broken pencil.

Ms Luminara stood on the porch in a coat whose pockets were shaped like envelopes.

Ms Luminara: “Good morning. The slippers are for quiet discoveries. Loud discoveries get boots.”

Natalie: “Does the bus decide which sort we need?”

Ms Luminara: “The bus puts boots on for most things. I've asked it to develop a little range.”

A windshield wiper wagged indignantly.

Jeff had one foot on the lowest step and one on the pavement. He was examining a length of rope that connected the porch rail to absolutely nothing.

Jeff: “Is that safety equipment, or is it a metaphor? I would like to know before I rely on it.”

Ms Luminara unhooked the rope.

Ms Luminara: “Decoration. Excellent question. The real handrail is here, and our imaginary travel comes with a stop button beside every seat.”

Jeff tested the handrail, climbed aboard, and claimed the window seat. His caution had not diminished his interest in the little compass embedded in its armrest. The compass pointed toward the word ASK.

Kàra arrived walking quickly, then slowed to study the vine. One of its question marks had opened almost straight. She nudged it with a fingertip. It curled again.

Kàra: “Are those questions waiting for answers, or answers becoming questions?”

Ms Luminara: “At present they're a plant waiting for water.”

Ástríðr took the watering can from beside the door. She held it over the roots rather than the leaves, then gave the brass letterbox an experimental pat.

Ástríðr: “And what does this want?”

Henry: “It doesn't necessarily want anything.”

Ástríðr: “What is this designed to receive, Henry?”

Henry: “Letters, apparently. Though with this bus I'd check.”

The letterbox sneezed.

Jackson, who had been looking at the destination board, stepped out of the way just as an envelope shot across the porch. It struck the bell, rebounded from the handrail, and landed in Natalie's open notebook. The bell made a surprisingly dignified sound for something that had just been hit by the post.

Across the envelope, in narrow black letters, was an address:

NEUTRAL ATOM. GROUND STATE. FIRST TWENTY ELEMENTS.

Beneath it stood a shorter line:

OUTER-ELECTRON PATTERN: ns² np⁵.

Nothing followed. There was no element symbol, no row number, and no name in the corner.

Natalie read it twice. The symbols were familiar enough to suggest that they ought to be easy. That feeling was less helpful than it sounded.

Ms Luminara took a folded map from her pocket and gave each person a copy before anyone had attempted an answer. Inside was a periodic table. The first twenty boxes had clear names, atomic numbers, and small windows that could be opened to show supplied electron configurations. A legend distinguished the numbers used for rows and columns from the atomic number inside each element's box.

Ms Luminara: “You can use every part of that. The point isn't to remember a map you haven't been given. It is to find out what this address actually tells us.”

Kàra leaned over Natalie's shoulder. Her finger stopped short of the superscript five.

Kàra: “Two and five. Seven outer electrons. Chlorine?”

The destination board clicked. SOMEWHERE PRECISE disappeared. CHLORINE began to form, letter by letter.

Natalie felt the attraction of it herself. Chlorine was a word she could summon quickly. An answer arriving quickly had a way of sounding as though it had already been checked.

Natalie: “Chlorine came to my mind too. I'm not sure whether that's evidence or familiarity.”

Jackson looked from the envelope to the board.

Jackson: “Did we tell it that was the answer?”

Ms Luminara reached through the door and pressed a button marked CONSIDERING. The half-formed destination stopped with its final two letters missing.

Ms Luminara: “Apparently this model of the postal service has an enthusiasm problem.”

Henry had unfolded his map across two seats. He pointed to the repeated letter n.

Henry: “Before we decide, what does this stand for? A number we know, or one we still need?”

Ms Luminara: “A principal-level label left unspecified in the pattern. We'll give it a value when the evidence permits. We aren't entitled to replace it with three just because chlorine feels familiar.”

Ástríðr settled beside the window. She did not try to turn the answer into another answer immediately. She held the envelope up so that everyone could see its blank lower half.

Ástríðr: “There is quite a lot of address missing from this address.”

Ms Luminara: “Then we have a good place to begin. Seatbelts, everyone.”

The bus eased backward. The porch folded itself with the careful movements of someone closing a treasured book. Through the windshield the printed map lifted from Natalie's knees, becoming a landscape of broad terraces and doorways with names above them.

Jeff watched the pavement fall gently away.

Jeff: “Actual atoms?”

Ms Luminara: “A teaching neighborhood. Atoms don't have porches, and electrons won't be driving along these roads. We can ride through a representation, provided we keep track of what it represents.”

The bus slipped between two terraces. Somewhere ahead, two doorbells rang in answer to the same letter.

## 2. A neighborhood that would not become a line

The first thing the neighborhood refused to do was arrange itself like an ordinary street.

Hydrogen stood on the left side of a high terrace. Helium stood far to the right. Between them stretched open air, crossed by a footbridge that appeared whenever someone needed it and disappeared when they didn't. Below, another terrace began with lithium and beryllium, left a large open courtyard, and continued with six more doors.

Jeff unfolded the map again.

Jeff: “The road skips. Are there houses missing?”

Ms Luminara parked beside the courtyard. The engine made a quiet sound like paper being squared into a stack.

Ms Luminara: “The familiar table layout puts certain relationships in useful columns. A blank part of this layout isn't a missing atom or a hole inside an atom. It is space in a chart.”

Henry read the numbers above the columns. They ran from 1 to 18, including the columns passing through the empty courtyard. Then he read the atomic numbers on the second terrace: 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10.

Henry: “The atomic numbers continue without needing a box in every column of every row.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly. Different kinds of numbers are doing different jobs. If we make them all mean a street address, our metaphor will start delivering nonsense.”

The bus lowered a ramp. Kàra stepped onto it and looked along the numbered columns. She could feel an urge to make the whole arrangement click at once. The open spaces troubled that urge. So did helium, sitting high above the right-hand edge as though it had been invited to a different party.

Kàra: “I want the shape to be the explanation.”

Natalie: “Sometimes the shape helps us remember an explanation. That isn't quite the same thing.”

She said it as much to herself as to Kàra. A familiar diagram could conceal a surprising amount of unasked-for certainty.

They walked to the nearest directory. Its heading read FIRST TWENTY NEUTRAL GROUND-STATE ATOMS. Underneath, a small brass flap offered SHOW THE REST OF THE TABLE. Jackson lifted it. The neighborhood continued into the distance, with many more streets than their route had shown.

Jackson: “So first twenty is a boundary on our inquiry, not the size of the world.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. We aren't going to announce that everything outside our day trip has been explained.”

Jackson let the flap fall. It did not lock. They could look again later without pretending they were ready to classify every configuration they might find there.

Ástríðr stood by the door marked F. Its full name, fluorine, appeared below the symbol. The atomic-number plaque read 9. On the next terrace down, directly beneath it in the same column, was chlorine, Cl, with 17 on its plaque.

Each door had a little outer display. Fluorine's said 2s² 2p⁵. Chlorine's said 3s² 3p⁵.

Kàra stopped moving.

Kàra: “Oh.”

Both doorbells rang again. Neither sounded apologetic.

She had expected, perhaps, for the neighborhood to reward the speed of her first recognition. Instead, it had supplied a second place where the same pattern worked. Fluorine's display was not a trick spelling, not an exception, and not a poorly drawn chlorine. It was plainly another candidate.

Henry laid the envelope between the two displays on the directory's folding shelf.

Henry: “The n could be two here or three there. In each case s has two and p has five at that outer level.”

Ástríðr: “Same kind of ending. Different beginning.”

Ms Luminara: “A useful way to notice it. We'll inspect what those parts mean before asking the picture to do more.”

Natalie turned her map so its orientation matched the neighborhood. She traced horizontally along fluorine's terrace, then vertically down to chlorine.

Natalie: “A period is one of these horizontal rows. A group is a vertical column.”

Henry: “Fluorine is in period two. Chlorine is in period three. Both are in group seventeen.”

Jeff pointed back to the envelope.

Jeff: “But it never says a period.”

The distinction seemed small when spoken aloud. Looking at the two doors, Natalie could see how much depended on it. A family resemblance had carried them to the right column. It had not carried them to a unique box.

From the end of the courtyard came the sound of wheels. A narrow delivery trolley rounded the corner. Its tray held a parcel shaped exactly like the envelope, enlarged enough to require two straps. On its front, a polished plate read SINGLE DESTINATION SERVICE.

The trolley paused at the directory. A little arm scanned the envelope. Another arm lifted a rubber stamp.

Kàra watched the stamp swing toward a pad labeled CHLORINE.

Kàra: “Wait.”

The arm continued.

Kàra stepped forward, not close enough to put her hand beneath it, but close enough to press the clearly marked hold switch. The trolley stopped with the stamp raised.

Kàra: “Chlorine still fits. But fluorine hasn't stopped fitting.”

Ms Luminara joined her beside the switch.

Ms Luminara: “That is the repair we need. You haven't exchanged one guess for another. You've noticed that the evidence permits both.”

Behind the trolley, a printer began to whirr. It produced a slip containing a single sentence: PLEASE SELECT ONE.

Jackson took the slip, read it, and looked at the two doors.

Jackson: “Its form has room for one answer. The chemistry has room for two.”

The machine printed the same sentence again, slightly larger.

Ástríðr caught the paper before it fell into the courtyard.

Ástríðr: “I'm not convinced that font size is the missing evidence.”

## 3. The stamp that traveled faster than its reasons

The trolley's service hatch was beside the hold switch. It bore a label that said OPEN FOR EXPLANATIONS, which Jeff regarded as a promising feature in any machine.

He opened it.

Inside was no mysterious engine. A strip of paper ran across three brass rollers. One roller read MATCH. The second read CHOOSE. The third read REPORT. Between the second and third was the stamp, already inked for chlorine.

Jeff: “It found a match, chose it, and was going to report that as a completed identification.”

Henry bent to read a smaller card tucked beneath the second roller.

Henry: “Here. ‘When more than one match exists, choose the most recently used destination.’”

Kàra let out a short breath. She had done something very close to that in her head.

Kàra: “The trolley has my bad shortcut in brass.”

Ms Luminara: “It has a shortcut that works for choosing a favorite parking place. It doesn't establish which atom a clue identifies.”

There was relief in the distinction. Kàra's first thought had not been evidence of a defective mind. It had been a possible answer promoted beyond what the evidence supported. The machine had done the same promotion more efficiently, which was useful only if one admired fast mistakes.

Jackson followed the paper strip toward the back of the trolley. There a stack of completed labels waited beneath a transparent cover. Each label had a destination, a confident green check mark, and no space for the original clue.

Jackson: “What happens when one of these arrives?”

A small recording played from the service hatch.

Trolley: “Receiving stations use the confirmed destination to prepare subsequent directions.”

Jackson: “And can they see why it was confirmed?”

Trolley: “The destination is confirmed.”

Henry: “That isn't an answer to his question.”

The trolley considered the distinction, or gave a convincing imitation of considering it. A small light blinked beside the words REASONS NOT FORWARDED.

Natalie felt the story of the mistake lengthen. Before this, she had pictured a parcel at the wrong door. Now she pictured another machine accepting that door as a fact and building a route from it. The earliest unsupported choice would become harder to see with every tidy label applied afterward.

Natalie: “We need the original clue to travel with the result.”

Jackson: “And if there isn't one result yet, that needs to travel too.”

They were not delivering anything dangerous. Ms Luminara made that clear when Jeff checked the straps. The parcel was a teaching packet for this fictional neighborhood, and the day's consequence was misdirected information, not a chemical spill. That was consequence enough. Nobody needed an invented emergency to justify reading an address accurately.

Jeff unhooked the stamp from its arm and placed it on a cloth in the service hatch. The trolley emitted a wounded little ping.

Jeff: “I'm not destroying it. We're stopping it from claiming something it hasn't established.”

Ástríðr looked around for a second tray. There wasn't one. The trolley had been built to look successful only when it sent something away.

Ástríðr: “It needs a place to wait that doesn't look like failure.”

Ms Luminara opened one of her envelope-shaped pockets and drew out a folding shelf. She attached it to the side of the trolley. Henry checked that its label described the current state rather than merely saying NOT DONE.

They settled on TWO CANDIDATES REMAIN.

Fluorine and chlorine were both printed beneath it, with their matching outer patterns. Natalie copied the scope from the original envelope above the names: neutral, ground state, first twenty. She left a blank line for the missing observation.

The trolley scanned the revised shelf.

Trolley: “Delivery incomplete.”

Jackson: “Identification incomplete. Delivery can wait. Those aren't the same operation.”

For a moment nothing happened. Then the first roller turned backward, withdrawing the premature label before it reached the outgoing stack. A new strip emerged with two names on it.

Kàra watched the names remain visible beside one another. She had been braced for a correction in which chlorine was crossed out and fluorine written above it. This was more demanding in one way and kinder in another. She had to tolerate an unresolved pair. She did not have to pretend that the part of her reasoning that worked had been worthless.

Ms Luminara: “We can leave that there while we investigate. If you would rather read the explanation first, it's on the map. If you want to compare the windows yourself, we can do that. Nobody has to be puzzled on schedule.”

Natalie sat on the edge of the courtyard bench. Its stone was warm. A paper leaf skittered across her shoe and caught beneath the trolley wheel.

Natalie: “What else would we have to know to select one?”

Henry: “The row would do it.”

Jeff: “Or the full electron count, if the neutral condition stays true.”

Ms Luminara: “Both are promising. We'll check why each works.”

Above the bench was a noticeboard listing incoming post. One line referred to their envelope. Another, carrying the same dispatch mark, was marked OBSERVATION SLIP: NEXT COLLECTION AT NOON.

Jackson pointed it out. Beneath the listing were a copy-request button and a small clock. Ms Luminara checked both before pressing the button. A receipt dropped into the tray: COPY REQUESTED; DELIVERY TO BUS LETTERBOX. The clock had not yet reached eleven.

Kàra: “So the rest exists, but it hasn't reached us.”

Ms Luminara: “According to this record. We can wait here or use the workshop while the post makes its round. The request doesn't depend on finishing a lesson.”

Natalie looked again at the raised five. She could follow the argument about two doors while still feeling unsure of what each mark meant.

Natalie: “I'd like to understand the address we already have. But I want that receipt where we can see it.”

Jackson clipped it above the waiting shelf. The class had a next step and a way to notice when waiting was no longer useful.

The bus unfolded its porch again beside the bench, offering shade. Ástríðr moved the two candidate cards into it so neither would blow away.

Ástríðr: “A waiting room for possible answers.”

Henry: “With conditions attached.”

Ms Luminara: “Our most comfortable kind.”

## 4. A number with a small territory

On the porch table, the envelope seemed less intimidating than it had on the destination board. It was paper again, rather than a command about where the bus ought to go.

Natalie opened her notebook to a clean page. She began to write ELECTRON CONFIGURATION, stopped after the first word, and watched her pencil point leave a tiny dent in the paper.

Ástríðr sat opposite her, folding one corner of a spare card into a little stand.

Ástríðr: “Would it help to make the symbols larger?”

Natalie: “I think so. I can recognize the whole thing without being sure what each piece is allowed to tell me.”

She had known students who could reproduce a formula and still not know what its symbols referred to. She had sometimes mistaken her own familiarity with the formula for a guarantee that she would explain it well. That memory was not a charge against every lesson she had taught. It was an uncomfortable, useful reason to slow down now.

Ms Luminara brought a tray of wooden type. There was a large 3, a lowercase p, and a smaller raised 5.

Ms Luminara: “Then let's let each piece introduce itself.”

Henry set the three pieces together: 3p⁵.

Henry: “The three is the principal quantum number, the principal shell label. The p tells us a subshell type. The five counts electrons in that particular subshell.”

Jeff picked up the raised five and moved it over the whole row of type.

Jeff: “So this isn't the total for the atom.”

Henry: “Correct.”

Jeff: “And it isn't five atoms.”

Ms Luminara: “Correct again. Its territory is smaller than either of those.”

She set a narrow bracket beneath 3p⁵. Above it she placed a card: FIVE ELECTRONS IN 3p. No color was needed to show the connection.

Kàra was still standing, moving a few steps between the table and the railing. When she stopped, she took the large 3 away and replaced it with n.

Kàra: “Then in np⁵ the number of electrons in p is given, but the principal-level number isn't.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. In our address, ns² and np⁵ refer to the same unspecified outer principal level.”

Natalie wrote that in her own words, leaving the technical phrase beside it. The shared n had looked like two separate little obstacles. Now it was doing a single useful job: declaring a pattern without selecting the row.

Ástríðr took the spare card and made two stands. On one she wrote n = 2, on the other n = 3. The same wooden s² p⁵ pieces could be placed behind either.

Ástríðr: “Like an address with the street name but no building number.”

Henry opened his mouth, then paused.

Ástríðr: “Yes, I know. Not actual streets inside atoms.”

Henry: “I was going to say it works for incompleteness. We should keep that part.”

Ástríðr looked delighted.

Ms Luminara put an empty wastebasket under the table. On its side, in dignified script, was RETIRED PARTS OF METAPHORS.

Ms Luminara: “We aren't obliged to throw away the whole comparison because one part stops helping.”

Ástríðr dropped an imaginary brick into the basket. Henry contributed an imaginary streetlamp. The basket gave a small, satisfied rustle.

The original pattern lay between them. Two plus five made seven. That arithmetic was sound. What had gone wrong was not the sum; it was the leap from seven outer electrons to one named atom.

Natalie drew a line beneath the two superscripts and wrote 7. She nearly put TOTAL beside it, then stopped.

Natalie: “Outer-level total, for this supplied pattern. If I write only total, I erase the distinction we need.”

Ms Luminara: “Useful repair.”

She did not demand a longer speech. Natalie found she appreciated that. Sometimes a correction took only a few words, and forcing it to become a performance made it harder to see whether the words had actually helped.

Jeff had unfolded the rest of the fluorine card. He kept the earlier terms covered with his hand and showed only the ending.

Jeff: “This is like a receipt with its first lines folded under. I can add what's visible correctly and still not have the entire amount.”

Jackson: “Then the machine needs to know whether the incoming field is an outer pattern or a complete configuration. Otherwise even correct addition won't save it.”

Henry wrote OUTER PATTERN on the envelope's copy and COMPLETE CONFIGURATION on a fresh sheet. They were not different spellings for the same thing.

A quiet bell sounded from the incoming-post board. Kàra was halfway to the rail before she saw the smaller notice: REQUEST ACCEPTED FOR NOON COLLECTION. No observation had arrived yet.

She sat on the porch step. Ms Luminara handed her a cup of water, and Kàra took a long drink before looking at the clock again. There was no reason to spend the whole interval watching its minute hand.

Ms Luminara: “We have the important part of our next move. We can open both full displays, count what they contain, and compare those counts with the outer seven.”

Natalie closed the notebook for a moment. On the page, her imperfect lettering had left more space than she usually allowed herself. There was room for a second candidate, room for a condition, room for a question that did not have to be answered by the end of the line.

She took the fluorine card from Jeff.

Natalie: “Let's unfold the rest of the receipt.”

The porch steps lowered toward the two waiting doors.

## 5. What the outer window hid

Fluorine's door was made of pale wood, with a small handle beneath its configuration window. Chlorine's was darker and slightly taller. Jeff inspected both, then looked back at Ms Luminara.

Jeff: “Before I use the handles, does taller mean something chemical?”

Ms Luminara: “Not in this display. The carpenter was feeling expressive. We use the printed data, not the carpenter's mood.”

Ástríðr laughed and sat on the low wall between the doors. The wall moved politely to keep both windows within reach.

Jeff pressed EXPAND beneath fluorine's outer pattern.

The little window unfolded into three panels:

1s² 2s² 2p⁵.

He read each panel aloud, putting his finger beneath the superscript rather than the leading numeral. Two, two, five. His first addition was so quick that Natalie asked him to leave the tokens on the ledge while she followed it.

Jeff: “Two plus two is four. Four plus five is nine.”

Natalie: “Nine electrons in the complete neutral atom. Seven in the n = 2 outer level. The other two are in 1s.”

She had not needed another explanation of addition. She had needed the sum to remain visible long enough to connect it to the labels. Jeff left the tokens where they were.

Kàra pressed the same button beneath chlorine. Five panels unfolded:

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵.

The display was longer but not less readable. Each subshell name stayed attached to its occupancy. Henry placed a loose frame around the last two panels, leaving the earlier three outside it.

Henry: “Here is the outer n = 3 part. Two plus five still makes seven.”

Jackson put a second frame around everything.

Jackson: “And here is the complete account. Two plus two plus six plus two plus five.”

Ástríðr counted with him, arranging the results along the ledge: 2, 4, 10, 12, 17.

The final seventeen sat beneath chlorine's plaque, also seventeen. It looked satisfyingly symmetrical. Natalie resisted writing an equals sign between the two before she had said what they counted.

Natalie: “The configuration sum counts electrons. The atomic-number plaque counts protons. They match here because the atom is neutral.”

Ms Luminara: “Good. Keep that little because. It holds the bridge up.”

Jeff stepped back until he could see both complete displays. He had been imagining the outer pattern as most of an address, needing only some final decorative flourish. The difference between nine and seventeen made it feel like a very different kind of incompleteness. The same seven outer electrons could accompany different numbers of inner electrons.

Jeff: “The outer window wasn't lying. It just wasn't showing the whole inventory.”

Ástríðr: “And if we accuse it of lying, we might stop using the part it shows us well.”

Henry: “As long as it says outer. If it says complete, then leaving out the inner electrons is a problem.”

They let that distinction stand between them for a while. The bells above the doors no longer seemed to be contradicting one another. Each had answered the question the letter actually asked. The trouble lay in expecting that question to have one answer.

Kàra ran a finger along the empty space in ns² np⁵ where a particular principal number would go. Her first seven had survived the inspection. Chlorine had survived as a candidate. What had not survived was the word only, which she had never spoken but had almost allowed the trolley to print.

Kàra: “I hadn't said ‘only chlorine.’ But I was reaching for the only-chlorine button.”

Jackson: “That matters. The output made the stronger claim whether or not we meant to make it.”

Natalie wrote only in the margin and circled it. Then she drew an arrow back to the original envelope. The word had not come from there.

A second flap on each door offered a shell summary. Fluorine's read 2, 7. Chlorine's read 2, 8, 7. The shorter summaries made the matching outer sevens easier to see, but they hid the division between s and p.

Jeff: “Another useful window, another bit left out.”

Ms Luminara: “Precisely. We choose a representation for a purpose. It is worth asking what information it preserves.”

Henry pointed to the middle eight in chlorine's shell summary.

Henry: “That is 2s² plus 2p⁶. One shell containing two different subshells.”

Ástríðr looked at the five panels and the three shell totals.

Ástríðr: “Then five terms doesn't mean five shells.”

Natalie: “I can see myself making that mistake if the spaces between terms looked like separate floors.”

The bus, which had been waiting nearby, immediately began to grow five storeys. Ms Luminara slapped the side panel.

Ms Luminara: “Don't encourage the architecture.”

The top two storeys folded away with a disappointed creak. A new door appeared beneath the bus instead. Its sign read SUBSHELL WORKSHOP. Under that, a smaller sign read NO ACTUAL ROOMS INSIDE ATOMS.

Jeff peered down the steps.

Jeff: “You anticipated Henry.”

Ms Luminara: “A wise vehicle does.”

She collected the candidate cards without merging them. The full configurations had made the distinction clearer, but neither configuration had been supplied as the identity of the addressed atom. They were reference records for the candidates. Looking up two possible answers was not the same as measuring which answer belonged to the letter.

Natalie added that to her notebook before following the others. She wanted the difference to survive the descent.

## 6. Rooms inside rooms and the point where rooms fail

The workshop under the bus was wider than the bus itself. Nobody commented immediately. They were becoming selective about which impossibilities required explanation.

Across the floor lay three large frames labeled n = 1, n = 2, and n = 3. Within them were smaller labeled frames. The first contained 1s. The second contained 2s and 2p. The third contained 3s, 3p, and 3d.

Kàra stopped at 3d.

Kàra: “That wasn't on chlorine's display.”

Ms Luminara: “It has zero occupancy in the neutral ground-state chlorine configuration we used. A category of allowed states can exist without being occupied in a particular atom.”

Henry crouched beside the frame. A removable card said ALLOWED SUBSHELL; OCCUPANCY NOT IMPLIED.

Henry: “So the directory of possible rooms isn't a list of rooms currently full.”

Ástríðr: “That's a useful distinction even in an ordinary house.”

She put her empty cup inside the 3d frame, then immediately took it out.

Ástríðr: “No. That looks like an electron being added. I'll keep my cup.”

Ms Luminara led them around the frames rather than through them. The room model was meant to show categories nested within categories. It was not a cross-section of a solid atom with walls separating little particles.

A principal shell grouped states with the same principal quantum number n. Within it, subshells such as s, p, and d distinguished further kinds of states. The familiar configuration notation named occupied subshells and counted electrons assigned to them in the model.

Natalie tried drawing that hierarchy. Her first sketch put an orbital beside a shell as though they were comparable containers. She crossed out the line herself when she saw Henry opening another flap inside the 2p frame.

Under it were three small outlined spaces.

Henry: “Three orbitals in a p subshell.”

Under 2s was one.

Jeff: “So s and p aren't two different words for one orbit each.”

Ms Luminara: “Right. An s subshell has one orbital; a p subshell has three. In this orbital description, each orbital can accommodate at most two electrons, with the required opposite spin labels.”

She showed two markers with distinct printed arrows, then turned the display so the arrows could be seen without any color coding.

Ms Luminara: “Those spin labels describe a quantum property. They don't mean a tiny ball literally spinning clockwise or anticlockwise in this room.”

Jeff looked at the bus, as though preventing it from adding a carousel.

Jeff: “Let's not give it ideas.”

The bus made a very innocent sound overhead.

Kàra placed two markers in the 2s outline. There was no third permitted position in that orbital diagram. In the 2p part, six markers could be represented across three orbitals. Five could therefore fit in p without filling its maximum occupancy of six.

Kàra: “That is why s² and p⁵ are different kinds of counts. The capacity depends on the subshell structure, not on how big I draw the superscript.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. The notation cannot make an allowed state just by writing down a number.”

Natalie looked at 3d again. Five orbital outlines lay beneath that flap, enough for a maximum of ten electrons in the subshell. She did not need to memorize them to understand why a shell could contain more than s and p. She wrote that reason rather than copying every box.

Natalie: “Then saying ‘a shell holds eight’ without a qualification would be dangerous here.”

Henry: “The second shell can account for eight in 2s and 2p. But the third shell also has 3d states.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly. We will use the actual configurations for our first-twenty examples. We won't turn one familiar shell total into a universal capacity.”

The workshop lights dimmed over the d frame, leaving it legible but taking it out of the center of the display. It was a boundary marker, not a demand that they learn every later configuration that day.

Ástríðr sat cross-legged beside Natalie and held the notebook steady while Natalie repaired the hierarchy. Shell, subshell, orbital. She kept the drawing simple enough to read. Beside it she wrote that these were categories in a model, not literal apartment blocks.

Ástríðr: “My incomplete street address could explain why we needed another clue. It couldn't have told us how these categories fit inside one another.”

Natalie: “The folded receipt couldn't either. It helped me count what was missing, but it doesn't explain permitted occupancies.”

Jeff: “But it got us to ask what was included. That's still useful.”

The three of them looked at the empty frame before adding another thought. Their metaphors had earned a place in the journey without becoming its physics.

Above their heads, the bus extended a chute from the RETIRED PARTS OF METAPHORS basket. A tiny cardboard wall dropped through it. Ms Luminara caught the wall and laid it across the frames.

Ms Luminara: “Here is the part we retire. There is no hard shell surface enclosing electrons like sweets in a tin. Orbitals describe electron states and spatial probability patterns. They aren't miniature roads.”

Kàra looked toward a frosted door at the end of the workshop. Soft light moved behind it, without any visible path.

Kàra took Natalie's notebook, with permission, and held its nested boxes toward the frosted door.

Kàra: “I understand the labels. I'm still seeing walls. Could we look at what we use instead of roads and rooms before I make this picture permanent?”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. Bring the sketch. We can compare it with a small, carefully labeled representation.”

Jeff was already at the door. He read the control panel, selected STATIONARY VIEW, and looked back at the others.

Jeff: “We can go in without having the room spin us.”

Ms Luminara: “A brave and excellent choice.”

## 7. Weather without little tracks

Beyond the frosted door, there was no floor in sight.

There was a railing, a bench, and a clear platform that held their weight. Around it, points of light appeared briefly and vanished. After a while a faint, roughly spherical cloud became visible, denser in some regions than others. The effect was quiet enough that Kàra's first impulse was to lean forward rather than hold on.

A sign floated at eye level:

SIMULATED POSITION RESULTS FOR MANY SIMILARLY PREPARED HYDROGEN ATOMS IN THE 1s STATE.

Below it, another line read: ONE ELECTRON PER ATOM; THIS DISPLAY ACCUMULATES MANY TRIALS.

Jeff read both lines aloud.

Jeff: “So the points aren't all the electrons in one hydrogen atom.”

Ms Luminara: “Right. One electron belongs to each of the model's repeated preparations. The accumulated dots help us represent a probability distribution. They are not a photograph of a crowded atom.”

Natalie watched a dot appear far from a cluster she had begun to expect. It did not travel there along a visible curve. The next dot appeared elsewhere. The display refused to connect them.

Natalie put two dots on the transparent sheet supplied beside the bench and joined them. A third flash appeared behind the line, far off to one side. She started to extend her drawing.

Kàra: “Like watching a tracker refresh and imagining the path between readings.”

Henry leaned forward to read the counter beneath the display. Each flash advanced it by one, and each number carried the label NEW PREPARATION.

Henry: “But these aren't successive readings of the same atom.”

Natalie held her connected dots beside the counter. She had drawn a route between two different trials.

Ms Luminara: “That's the important catch. These are outcomes from different similarly prepared systems. We aren't following one electron around its orbit.”

Natalie rubbed away the line with the corner of the cloth. The dots remained.

The bus offered an overlay with a familiar circular track. Jeff looked at its label: CLASSICAL PLANETARY PICTURE. He left it off.

Jeff: “We already know what mistake it would encourage. I'd rather watch this long enough to get used to it.”

They sat on the bench. Ástríðr held her cup between her hands, and Henry leaned back against the railing. Without a voice continuously telling them what to notice, the scene had time to become strange.

The glow did not mark a hard outer wall. Some regions had much higher probability density than others, but the cloud did not behave like a solid shell that a little ball could bounce against. A movable boundary could enclose a chosen proportion of the represented probability; moving that boundary changed the chosen volume, not the fundamental nature of the state.

Henry: “Then if somebody draws an orbital surface, I should ask what the surface represents. It isn't the skin of the electron.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. Often it encloses a specified fraction of the probability. The drawing convention needs a legend.”

Kàra watched the unconnected flashes. She was used to using motion as an explanation. If something changed, she wanted to know what pushed it, where it went, what track it followed. Those were powerful questions in many of the settings she knew. Here, insisting on the last of them too early could smuggle in a picture that the model did not supply.

Kàra: “I don't like giving up the route.”

Ms Luminara: “You don't have to enjoy it immediately. We can still make precise predictions. They aren't all predictions of a tiny planet's route.”

Natalie drew a cluster of dots, then stopped before shading the page into an ornamental sphere.

Natalie: “Does this mean the atom's electron count is uncertain too?”

It was a question she might once have edited out before speaking. The word probability seemed to threaten the exact bookkeeping they had just completed.

Ms Luminara shook her head.

Ms Luminara: “Not in the way you mean. In the specified hydrogen atom here, we have one electron. The spatial probability model doesn't turn that into half an electron or an unknown inventory. A configuration count and a probability distribution answer different questions.”

Henry looked from the display to the original candidate cards in Natalie's notebook.

Henry: “And not knowing whether our letter means fluorine or chlorine is another kind of uncertainty. That comes from incomplete identifying information.”

Ástríðr: “The letter hasn't put one atom halfway between the two elements.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly. Our uncertainty about an address is not a demonstration of quantum superposition.”

Kàra laughed, softly this time.

Kàra: “There goes a sentence I might have tried to make sound clever.”

She reached beneath the bench and mimed dropping a scrap into the retired-metaphor chute. Nobody laughed at her uncertainty. Henry smiled at the precision of the disposal.

The display changed to a labeled schematic of s and p orbital shapes, clearly marked as simplified orbital representations. The p set had different orientations. Ms Luminara did not claim these colored-looking lobes were sacks that electrons filled with substance. The shapes and their associated probabilities belonged to the model of states.

Natalie studied the small label that distinguished the hydrogen demonstration from the many-electron configurations on their cards.

Natalie: “We used a simpler system to see what an orbital isn't. We haven't calculated chlorine from this picture.”

Ms Luminara: “An important limit. Many-electron atoms involve electron-electron interactions as well as attraction to the nucleus. The configuration language is useful, but this little gallery isn't an exact solution for every atom.”

Jeff stood and walked to the far end of the platform, where the cloud thinned into darkness. He was not trying to catch a point of light. He was comparing the view with the diagram he had carried in his head when he boarded.

Jeff: “The picture I grew up with was easier to draw.”

Ástríðr: “Do you miss it?”

Jeff considered the question.

Jeff: “A little. But I like knowing which part it couldn't do.”

When they left, Natalie folded the transparent sheet around the workshop sketch. There were still boxes on one and unconnected dots on the other. Neither page could do the other's job. Kàra carried the cloth back to its hook and took one last look at the counter before closing the door.

## 8. The staircase that was not a filling rule

Back in the workshop, the frames had rearranged themselves into a staircase. Each step bore a principal number. The bus seemed determined to be architecturally helpful.

At the third step, Jeff found a large sign: FILL THIS FLOOR BEFORE PROCEEDING UPWARD.

He looked at the empty 3d frame, then at the next step, where 4s had appeared.

Jeff: “Does it mean fill every category with a three before using a four?”

Henry looked from the sign to the empty 3d frame.

Henry: “That follows the floor numbers. I'd expect the next electrons there.”

He placed two loose markers beside 3d, keeping them outside the frame until they checked. Ms Luminara opened the reference flap below the sign. The two cards inside could be read immediately; nobody had to defend the prediction first.

Jeff chose potassium and Henry chose calcium. Beyond the side window, the corresponding element boxes lit up: atomic numbers 19 and 20. Their supplied neutral ground-state configurations ended in 4s¹ and 4s². Their 3d subshells were unoccupied in those configurations.

Henry left his markers where they were while he checked the calcium card again.

Henry: “My floor rule predicts the wrong place. The extra capacity is there, but that doesn't say it's occupied next.”

He took down the sign. Its hooks left two pale marks on the wood.

Kàra pulled the third-shell frame closer. It had room for s, p, and d categories. She had just learned that the possible capacity of the third shell was larger than eight. Now she could see why learning capacity was not equivalent to learning the order of ground-state occupancies.

Kàra: “Allowed room and lowest-energy arrangement are different questions.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. ‘Ground state’ concerns the lowest-energy state of the specified atom. Simply counting possible places on a numbered floor doesn't determine it.”

Natalie opened to the word ground in her notebook. She had left a little square beside it, intending to fill in a definition when she had more than an image of something at the bottom.

Natalie: “Lowest energy for that system. Not physically closest to the ground, and not a statement that every electron sits in the lowest orbital.”

Ms Luminara: “Correct. The allowed quantum states and occupancy constraints matter. So do the interactions. You can't place every electron in one favored state just by calling it low.”

Ástríðr touched the two-position s orbital outline. She remembered the markers they had placed before entering the gallery. That restriction was no longer a decorative rule pasted onto a set of rooms. It was part of why the simple instruction to pile everything at the bottom would fail.

Ástríðr: “Could we look under the floorboards, just enough to know what makes the energy matter?”

Ms Luminara's eyes brightened.

Ms Luminara: “At last, a request the bus can take literally.”

The staircase opened sideways. Beneath it lay a broad model panel showing a positive nucleus and a surrounding electronic state. Ms Luminara named the system carefully: an isolated atom being described at an introductory level. The positively charged nucleus attracted electrons. Electrons also interacted with one another. The allowed states and total energy could not be replaced by a story about electrons wanting a tidy shell.

Henry: “So if I say ‘the electron wants to get comfortable,’ I have replaced a physical account with a character motive.”

Ms Luminara: “As a clearly labeled joke, it might be harmless. As the reason, it is missing the mechanism.”

Jeff pointed to a little lever marked SUPPLY ENERGY. Ms Luminara explained that an atom could be excited into a higher-energy state when it absorbed appropriate energy. Its proton count did not thereby change. The exact allowed transitions and energies were not being calculated by this wooden lever.

Jeff waited until she had finished, then moved it. The panel changed state labels and raised an energy indicator. No electron smiled. No atom changed its name. A record beneath the panel changed from GROUND STATE to EXCITED STATE.

Jackson: “Then our original address excludes that case. The ground-state condition isn't an extra adjective we can ignore.”

Natalie: “It tells us which configuration records we are comparing.”

They watched the indicator return in a schematic relaxation demonstration, with emitted energy accounted for on the panel. Ms Luminara warned against interpreting the animation as a promise about every possible route or lifetime. It illustrated why energy must go somewhere; it did not supply a complete account of spectroscopy.

Kàra sat on the lowest step and read the first-twenty sequence on the reference card. The used subshells ran through 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, and then 4s for potassium and calcium. She did not turn the list into a universal law for every neutral atom and ion in the distant part of the map.

Kàra: “I wanted one beautiful staircase that would do all of it.”

Henry: “We have a useful route for these cases, and warnings about where the staircase stops working.”

Ástríðr: “That's still more than we had on the porch.”

Natalie wrote two questions beneath the ground-state definition. What arrangements are allowed? Which allowed arrangement has the lowest energy for this system? She could feel the difference between them now. One was not an inconvenient qualification appended to the other. Together they prevented the model from saying that a shell was full merely because the next principal level was occupied.

Ms Luminara let the panel close. The bus attempted to restore its original FILL THIS FLOOR sign. Jeff caught it halfway up.

Jeff: “Retirement basket?”

Ms Luminara: “With honors. It served us long enough to show us why it must leave.”

They carried the sign back to the porch. The clock was approaching noon, and the incoming-post board still showed their copy request. Kàra checked its dispatch mark against the envelope before leaving it clipped to the shelf.

Natalie turned the calcium card over. A narrow source note ran along its lower edge.

Natalie: “We were right to check the card instead of obeying the staircase. But what sits behind a reference card? How did people check that the element numbers meant something?”

Ms Luminara pulled a cord beside the porch. The bus unfolded an upper reading room above the courtyard, with an archive shelf visible through its broad window. Jeff took the trolley by its handle, and they brought the still-unresolved parcel with them up the shallow ramp.

## 9. The man behind a measured number

By the time they reached the reading room, the courtyard had lost most of its morning shade. The room smelled of dry paper and newly sharpened pencils. Jeff parked the trolley beside a chair near the window. Kàra chose the window ledge. Ms Luminara chose the only chair with a lever beneath it, which Natalie suspected was not a coincidence.

On the wall hung two periodic tables. One was the familiar modern chart. The other belonged to an older arrangement, with gaps and a different visual order. Natalie stood between them, turning the fluorine and chlorine cards over in her hands.

Natalie: “We've been using atomic number as though its meaning arrived printed on the universe. Someone had to establish what the sequence corresponded to.”

Henry: “Otherwise seventeen could just be an administrative label.”

Jackson: “And if the administration were wrong, every label downstream would still agree with it.”

Ms Luminara rested her hand on the lever.

Ms Luminara: “There is an investigator I would like you to meet. Not because he can guess our missing row. Because he worked on what a number in the ordering of elements could mean physically.”

The reading-room window fogged. Its lower edge became a laboratory bench, and the bench became the sill of a much older window. A date appeared beside the frame: MANCHESTER, OCTOBER 1913. The visit unfolding beyond it was an imagined encounter, not a recovered event in the historical record.

A young man was comparing marks on a photographic record with a sheet of figures. Nearby stood an X-ray spectrometer. The apparatus was still. Through the glass of the bus's observation boundary, the class could inspect it without entering an operating experiment or treating a century-old arrangement as modern safety guidance.

The young man moved the sheet closer to the light. His pencil hovered above one figure, went back to the record, then returned to the paper.

Jeff watched him repeat the comparison.

Jeff: “He's checking the line instead of making the line fit.”

A bus door appeared where a cupboard door had been. The young man looked up. He did not scream or declare the event impossible. He looked at the new hinges, the porch light, and the destination board, in that order.

Moseley: “I don't believe that was here a moment ago.”

Ms Luminara: “An entirely sound observation. I'm Ms Luminara. May we show you a question about the elements? We can return you to this moment before your work is disturbed.”

He approached far enough to see that the doorway did not contain the back of the cupboard. Instead it contained six learners, a stack of maps, and a bus wearing slippers.

Moseley: “Henry Gwyn Jeffreys Moseley. Harry will do. I work here in Manchester, in Rutherford's laboratory. At present I'm studying characteristic X-ray spectra and what their frequencies tell us about the atoms producing them.”

He wrote his name on a spare card, leaving enough space between the words for Natalie to copy it without hurrying. She checked the spelling against his card before turning to the apparatus.

Henry, the classmate, held up a hand.

Henry: “I'm Henry too. We should probably keep your surname on the labels.”

Moseley glanced at the speaker labels that had materialized above the seats.

Moseley: “Your vehicle has anticipated the difficulty.”

Ms Luminara: “It learns from painful conversational experience.”

The guest left his working record on the bench. He stepped aboard carrying only a pencil and a question. He wanted to know why their chart placed such confidence in the numbered sequence. Ms Luminara told him the visit was outside ordinary time, that some of the class's language belonged to later science, and that she would label those later ideas when they used them.

He looked at the modern map, then stopped at the configuration window and pointed to its unfamiliar marks. Ms Luminara placed a blank briefing card beside it. They would return to those marks after examining the evidence he had brought.

Moseley returned their attention to the problem he knew. Characteristic X-rays from different targets could be investigated through their spectra. The work of William Henry Bragg and William Lawrence Bragg on crystals and X-ray analysis had opened an experimental route. Moseley had also worked with Charles Galton Darwin. Rutherford's nuclear atom and Bohr's new ideas were part of the scientific landscape in which he was trying to make sense of the results.

Moseley: “The interesting thing is not that I can write consecutive integers beside a list. I can do that to the contents of a cupboard. The interesting thing is whether measurements support a regular relation to those integers.”

Kàra leaned forward. She liked the difference immediately, then realized she had been about to accept the atomic-number plaques because the plaques were so well made.

Kàra: “What would let you distinguish a useful numbering from a neat arrangement?”

Moseley tapped the pencil against his palm.

Moseley: “Bring the measurements with you. Let an arrangement fail if the measurements require it.”

The bus's destination board tried to print A NEAT ARRANGEMENT. Jackson switched it to MEASUREMENTS FIRST.

At the far end of the reading room, the wall unfolded into an enlarged, clearly labeled reconstruction of the spectrometer's evidence. There would be no attempt to conduct an X-ray experiment on the bus. They would examine what the method measured, and what it did not.

Moseley looked at the display, then back toward his laboratory.

Moseley: “If this extraordinary conveyance can enlarge a record without altering it, I should like to see where your question goes.”

Ms Luminara: “Seatbelts for the furniture, everyone. Our guest has agreed to investigate.”

## 10. Lines a ruler could not wish into place

The enlarged photographic record was less spectacular than Jeff had expected. No rainbow poured across it. There were narrow lines, some stronger than others, and enough marks that the eye wanted a guide.

He liked it better after a moment. A grand rainbow might have persuaded him that he understood something merely because he enjoyed looking at it.

Moseley stood beside the record and let the class inspect it. The display described the historical method in broad terms: sufficiently energetic electrons struck a target; characteristic X-rays were emitted; diffraction by a crystal related the observed geometry to wavelength. Frequency could then be determined from wavelength. The display was an explanation of recorded evidence, not instructions for assembling or operating apparatus.

Henry pointed to a line.

Henry: “That mark is evidence of radiation with a particular wavelength, once the instrument's geometry and calibration are accounted for. It isn't a photograph of an electron's path.”

Moseley: “Yes. And the geometry and checks matter. A sharp-looking mark does not remove the obligation to know what was measured.”

Kàra moved the transparent ruler provided by the model. She could line it up with a mark, but she could not drag the mark along with it. When she pressed too hard, the ruler squeaked.

Ms Luminara: “The ruler is protesting your interpretation.”

Kàra eased her grip.

Kàra: “I was hoping the pattern would show itself more politely.”

Before they moved to the next panel, Ms Luminara unfolded a label: RETROSPECTIVE TEACHING SCHEMATIC, BASED ON THE FIRST PAPER PUBLISHED IN DECEMBER 1913.

Ms Luminara: “This summarizes the investigation reported later this year. It does not certify what you had completed on the particular October day we have imagined, and it leaves out the wider survey published in 1914.”

Moseley read the label before helping them examine the schematic. They would use the supplied retrospective information openly, just as they would label the later atomic theory. No invented numerical values were assigned to the historical plates. The schematic showed corresponding characteristic lines for a limited series of elements, distinguishing the reported relationship from the simplified teaching drawing. They were comparing like lines across elements, not arbitrarily choosing whichever mark made a straight trend.

Ástríðr noticed two strong marks in one displayed record. She put the ruler beneath the brighter one.

Ástríðr: “I'd choose this. It's the clearest to see.”

Henry placed his ruler at the line the schematic identified as corresponding to the selected series. It did not coincide with hers.

Henry: “This label sends me here.”

Ástríðr: “Yours may be the right comparison. But if the line is difficult to read, surely that matters too?”

Moseley drew the teaching key between them. It distinguished selecting a corresponding line from judging how well that line could be measured.

Moseley: “Both questions matter. Choosing the most conspicuous mark does not establish that it belongs to the same series. Nor does a good label remove difficulty in measuring it. There can also be contributions from impurities; the target's name cannot account for every mark.”

Ástríðr moved her ruler, but kept a small question beside the less conspicuous line. She had changed which comparison she proposed without dropping her concern about how clearly it could be read.

Jackson crossed to the trolley beside Jeff's chair and opened its service hatch. He pointed at the old rule.

Jackson: “Choose the most familiar result. Choose the strongest-looking line. They sound different, but both can discard the information that tells us what the result means.”

Moseley read the rule and asked to see the original envelope. Jackson gave it to him without offering the class's answer first.

The guest set it aside until they finished examining the spectra. He did not use a scientific reputation as a substitute for looking at the evidence.

On another panel, the schematic plotted the square root of corresponding X-ray frequencies against the consecutive integers used for elemental order. The relation was approximately linear for the selected series and range. The axis labels stayed visible. Natalie traced one axis with her pencil without touching the paper.

Natalie: “The vertical spacing isn't frequency itself. It's the square root of frequency.”

Moseley: “An important distinction. If you change what an axis represents, you change what a straight line means.”

Jeff had been ready to describe the graph as “everything rises evenly.” He revised the sentence before speaking.

Jeff: “A transformed measurement varies regularly with the elemental number in the cases shown. That is more specific.”

Ms Luminara nodded. No one asked him to derive the relation to be allowed to understand its significance. The point was that the number acquired experimental support as more than a list position. The relation connected characteristic radiation with an ordering tied to nuclear charge, while the theoretical account was still developing.

The modern display named the nuclear-charge proposal associated with Antonius van den Broek alongside Rutherford's nuclear model. Moseley did not become the sole inventor of every idea needed to interpret his measurements. Henry read the names, then drew a small arrow from the apparatus to the earlier work that made the measurement possible.

Henry: “A measured result can be someone's contribution without the entire explanation having started with that person.”

Moseley: “That is a useful habit in giving credit.”

They sat around the table beneath the display. The pause felt different with their guest there. They were not merely receiving a historical accomplishment polished by hindsight. Their fictional Moseley had shown them a problem in which the measurements were available before the final modern account of all atomic structure.

Natalie: “I used to picture the table and the atom as though someone had inspected the inside and then drawn the chart. Here, measurements of something atoms emit help test what the ordering means.”

Kàra: “And a theory can explain a pattern well in one respect without being the last theory we'll need.”

She thought of the orbit tracks they had refused in the probability gallery. Moseley had mentioned early atomic models that used language about rings and orbits. It would be easy to smile at what later science changed and miss what the earlier work had made visible.

Ms Luminara waited until they had finished.

Ms Luminara: “We can preserve a successful measurement and revise a model used to interpret it. A later account doesn't make every earlier observation disappear.”

Before they closed the display, Moseley asked them to cover the element names on the schematic. Henry slid a blank sheet over the labels. The lines and axis values remained.

Moseley: “What disappeared?”

Jeff: “The names we were using to interpret the records. The marks themselves did not.”

Natalie pointed out that the remaining display still depended on the work of measurement. It was not raw nature separated from every instrument and decision. But hiding a name did not move a spectral line, and writing a more convenient name beside it would not improve a poor fit.

Jackson tried the opposite arrangement. He covered the measurement panel and left the list of names and integers visible. Now the ordering looked beautifully certain and almost entirely unsupported to a new reader.

Jackson: “This is what the trolley forwards. A clean result with its route to the evidence removed.”

Ástríðr lifted the sheet again. She did it carefully, as though uncovering something that deserved not to be hurried.

Ástríðr: “We need both. Not every receiving station needs all the apparatus details at once, but it needs a way back to them.”

Moseley left the two sheets beside the display. Their simple alternation had made a distinction visible that a more elaborate animation might have hidden. Natalie sketched the covered and uncovered versions in her notebook, labeling the drawing as their own comparison rather than a page from the guest's laboratory.

Moseley picked up their envelope at last. He read the scope, then the outer pattern, then the blank space beneath it.

Moseley: “Now I should like your promised briefing. These configuration symbols belong to a language I have not been taught.”

Natalie opened her notebook to the porch page. It was the first time that day her unfinished notes looked like something she could share without pretending they were complete.

## 11. Two names on the same bench

Ms Luminara placed a card between Moseley and the configuration displays. Its heading read LATER SCIENTIFIC BRIEFING. She read the first part aloud before inviting the class to help with the rest.

In the modern account being used for their adventure, an element's atomic number was its proton count. A neutral atom had an equal number of electrons. Electron configurations described occupancies of quantum states using a language developed beyond the models available in 1913. Their recent probability gallery had illustrated why those states should not be treated as little planetary paths.

Moseley listened, asked which parts were modern descriptions, and kept the briefing card beside him. He did not claim to have brought proton-count terminology or later quantum orbitals aboard from his own laboratory.

Moseley: “Then I will use this as information your teacher has supplied, rather than claim it as something I have already established.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly the arrangement.”

Henry: “For fluorine, n is two in the outer pattern. For chlorine, n is three. The supplied records say 2s² 2p⁵ and 3s² 3p⁵.”

Moseley: “Nine electrons in one account, seventeen in the other. And neutrality connects those totals to the corresponding nuclear charges in your modern account.”

Moseley touched the edge of the second sheet.

Moseley: “Did this come from the sender too?”

Natalie: “No. We unfolded it at chlorine's door.”

Kàra: “You can't tell from that either.”

Moseley: “Not if those are the conditions and those are the two matching records.”

Ástríðr: “The bus could have brought a whole conference, and we'd still have two doors.”

## 13. The row earns its place

For a moment, PERIOD 3 seemed almost too small an arrival for everything they had done while waiting.

Natalie watched Kàra make the comparison. The action was slower than pressing a button, but not laborious. They were no longer trying to show every thought that had ever crossed their minds. They were showing the short chain that justified this answer.

Ms Luminara: “That one may outlive us all.”

Navigation: “This address is now sufficiently specified.”

## 14. The plaque that stayed seventeen

Chlorine's door opened after the bell. The parcel slid gently inside, and a receipt emerged: DELIVERED, with the conditions printed beneath. A smaller line invited them to collect the teaching gift in the hall.

Jeff was about to follow when a side window slid upward. It offered a comparison display with seventeen positive marks on one side and seventeen negative marks on the other.

Jeff: “Could we look at this before we go in?”

Ms Luminara brought the bench closer. The completed receipt stayed clipped above it.

Jeff checked the label before drawing a conclusion. MODEL CHARGE LEDGER. The marks represented proton and electron counts, not seventeen little people on opposing teams.

Jeff: “Neutral chlorine: seventeen protons, seventeen electrons.”

A second display appeared beside it, still bearing the chlorine name and the atomic-number plaque 17. This time it had eighteen electron marks.

Ástríðr looked at the two displays.

Ástríðr: “One extra negative charge. Chloride?”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. A chlorine atom that has gained one electron is represented as the chloride ion, Cl⁻. Its nucleus still contains seventeen protons.”

Henry traced the minus sign with his finger without touching the screen.

Henry: “The superscript here reports net charge. It isn't doing the same job as the occupancy superscript in 3p⁵.”

Natalie felt the usefulness of that distinction immediately. Raised symbols were not a category with one meaning. Their role depended on the expression they belonged to.

Jackson looked down the terrace toward argon's door.

Jackson: “Eighteen electrons. That's the count on argon's card. Does the comparison now belong there?”

Ástríðr kept a finger beneath the seventeen proton marks.

Ástríðr: “We changed the electron side. Would moving it change this side too?”

Jackson looked back at the ledger. His hand was already on the trolley handle. He let go.

Jeff went to argon's door and asked for its reference card. It supplied eighteen protons and eighteen electrons for the neutral atom. He brought the card back to the bench, where they could compare the two accounts without moving their completed delivery.

On the bench, neutral argon and chloride now sat beside one another. Both had eighteen electrons. Their proton counts differed.

Kàra: “Same electron count. Different nuclei. Different elements.”

Jackson: “The eighteen matched, but the proton counts don't. I was about to change the destination for the wrong reason.”

Natalie checked the original letter rather than relying on the sentence she remembered.

Natalie: “Neutral. That was the bridge from electrons to protons in our delivery. It isn't true of this new card.”

They left the cards on the bench while they looked at the minus sign again. The identity plaque stayed seventeen. The charge ledger changed. That separation was the whole point of the display.

Ástríðr: “It hasn't moved into argon's house just because the electron account looks similar.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly. We sometimes compare species with the same number of electrons. That does not make them the same element, the same size, or identical in their behavior.”

Henry asked whether they should call the two cards isoelectronic. Ms Luminara supplied the term, then let it remain an optional label beside the plain-language explanation. Nobody had to remember it to understand the distinction.

Jeff returned to the display controls. One button said CHANGE ELECTRON COUNT. Another, behind a transparent cover, said CHANGE PROTON COUNT.

Jeff: “Those aren't two ways of doing the same operation.”

Ms Luminara: “No. Changing the number of protons changes the element. Ordinary ion formation changes the electron count without changing that nuclear identity. The covered button is only a comparison control in the model; it is not a chemical recipe for turning one element into another.”

Jeff left it covered. He did not need to press every button to learn what it meant.

Kàra counted the ledger signs again, using seventeen minus eighteen for the net charge in units of the elementary positive charge. The result was negative one. There was no need to imagine that chlorine had become an “angry atom” or that the extra electron had fulfilled a wish.

Kàra: “The sign is bookkeeping about a real electrical imbalance. It's not the atom's mood.”

Ms Luminara: “Precisely. And if we ask why a particular electron-transfer process occurs, we need its physical circumstances and energetics. The ledger tells us what charge the stated transfer produces.”

Jeff looked at the extra electron mark.

Jeff: “The comparison hasn't shown where that electron came from.”

Ms Luminara: “Right. In an actual transfer it comes from elsewhere in the system. Include that source in the account, and total charge is conserved. Conserved does not have to mean zero.”

Jeff left the two displays labeled as inventories. They had compared states without witnessing a process that produced one from the other.

Natalie had almost written “chlorine wants one electron” in her notebook because she had heard similar sentences so often. She looked at the words before they were finished, then replaced them with the actual claim: gaining one electron, with proton count unchanged, gives Cl⁻.

She was not being asked to solve every energetic question about chloride formation. She was being asked not to mistake an accounting result for a motive or a universal prediction.

Jackson pointed back to the original letter.

Jackson: “And our accepted destination still refers to neutral chlorine. We shouldn't quietly replace it with the ion because the next display is interesting.”

Ástríðr folded the chloride card separately and marked it COMPARISON CASE. The main parcel's paperwork remained neutral. That small act prevented their extension from rewriting the problem they had just solved.

Natalie: “I think I was letting every new piece of information rename the whole thing.”

Henry: “Now we can say which property changed.”

Jeff: “And which didn't.”

Ms Luminara closed the comparison window, leaving its label visible for anyone who wanted to return. Jackson checked that the chloride card had gone into the comparison folder. Then he took the completed receipt and led them through the already-open door.

The trolley gave a satisfied ping. Its parcel had stayed at the address the evidence supported.

## 15. A disappointingly useful treasure

Inside the delivered envelope was a postcard.

## 16. The souvenir that promised too much

The shortest route back to the workshop passed the corner of group 17. There a souvenir stall sold postcards, bookmarks, and an alarming number of slogans.

Jeff: “We have just met the everything problem.”

She opened the two first-ionization-energy reference panels. The panels defined the comparison before showing approximate values: the minimum energy required to remove one electron from an isolated gaseous neutral atom in its ground state, leaving the singly positive ion in its ground state and a separated electron.

For fluorine the supplied value was about 17.4 eV. For chlorine it was about 13.0 eV. The values were deliberately rounded; eV meant electronvolts, an energy unit, with each value referring to one atom's ionization. The display was a comparison, not a request to memorize spectroscopic precision.

Kàra: “Same outer occupancy pattern doesn't mean the same energy cost for removing an electron.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly. Here is one named property, under stated conditions, for which they differ.”

Jeff: “This is electron removal. It doesn't tell us the energy change for adding an electron, and it isn't every possible reaction of the element.”

Ms Luminara: “Thank you. We must not replace a slogan with another slogan wearing a unit.”

The source panels remained references rather than an experiment the class had conducted. No gaseous fluorine or chlorine sample appeared at the stall. The imaginary doors were classified atom records, not bottles of substances whose behavior they could infer from paint color or decorative size.

Natalie wrote the two approximate values next to the property name. She deliberately left space after them. A list of numbers without definitions would soon become another set of impressive-looking plaques.

In a many-electron atom, electron-nucleus attraction and electron-electron interactions affected the energies. It was not enough to count outer electrons and declare every consequence identical.

Henry: “Can we explain the comparison simply by saying chlorine's outer electron is farther away?”

Ms Luminara: “That can point toward part of an introductory trend, but treat it carefully. Orbitals are distributions, and shielding and nuclear charge matter too. We don't get an exact ionization energy by measuring the height of our fictional door.”

Ástríðr tapped the taller door illustration on her map.

Ástríðr: “The carpenter escapes responsibility again.”

Natalie: “The repeated outer pattern was useful enough to narrow our candidates. It wasn't detailed enough to identify one element or predict identical properties.”

Jackson: “Then downstream instructions should say what property is being predicted, instead of sending ‘same group’ as though it were a complete specification.”

Kàra: “And when I hear ‘more reactive,’ I should ask in which reaction and conditions, rather than imagine one universal reactivity number.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. A useful question for another day as well as this one.”

## 17. The next letter refuses to be chlorine

Jeff folded his arms and waited another moment. Nothing automatically advanced. The machine did not treat silence as permission to choose.

Jeff: “That is already a meaningful improvement.”

Ástríðr: “It has learned to wait without pretending nothing has happened.”

Ms Luminara: “We have shown that this test behaves as intended. Keep the wording that modest.”

Jackson nodded. One successful new case was evidence about the repair, not a certificate that every possible future input would be understood.

Natalie took the role of a receiving station. She accepted the machine's waiting slip and tried to reconstruct the problem without looking at their table. It gave her the case mark, scope, outer pattern, two candidates, and missing observation. She could see why the answer was not yet unique.

Natalie: “I can continue from this. I don't have to guess what your uncertainty means.”

Kàra supplied a second authored observation slip marked PERIOD 3 for the same test case. She put it into the new-observation slot rather than replacing the original envelope.

Sulfur remained. Oxygen was retained in the record with the reason for its exclusion. The machine did not print chlorine anywhere.

Ástríðr gave the trolley a small bow.

Ástríðr: “A lovely performance of not reusing the previous noun.”

The trolley pinged, then printed a note in very small type: TEST CASE COMPLETED UNDER SUPPLIED CONDITIONS.

Henry smiled.

Henry: “It is developing a readable personality.”

Jeff was not finished. He took another copy of the original fluorine-chlorine test and added a different distinguishing observation: ATOMIC NUMBER 9. The neutral and ground-state conditions remained. The candidate check selected fluorine.

Jeff: “We need to show that two candidates doesn't always end with choosing the lower door. Otherwise our new procedure could still be a disguised route to chlorine or sulfur.”

Kàra laughed. She had been so relieved by the period-three solution that she had nearly let period three become the expected ending of every incomplete letter.

Kàra: “Thank you. I would have made a comfortable little habit out of that.”

They gathered around the output shelf. Oxygen, sulfur, fluorine, and chlorine now appeared in different roles across the test records. No element was always the correct answer, and no unresolved case was treated as a failed person.

Jackson traced the sequence with his finger: preserve conditions, find all candidates in the declared scope, inspect what new evidence excludes, report the conclusion with its reasons. He did not ask the trolley to print the sequence as a triumphant slogan. He made it the working instructions inside the hatch.

Natalie wrote down what had changed in their model of a successful repair. It was not enough that the machine had finally delivered one parcel. It had to handle a changed case without importing the old answer, and it had to show a useful unresolved state when the information still allowed more than one result.

Henry had been writing another test while they watched the output. He showed them its case mark, keeping the contents folded.

Henry: “We've checked a choice between candidates. What should it do if the supplied entries can't both be right?”

Jackson cleared the result tray and invited him to try it.

<a id="ch01-scene-18"></a>
## 18. The letter with nowhere to go

Henry laid the envelope flat so nobody could accidentally read only the first line.

NEUTRAL GROUND-STATE ATOM. FIRST TWENTY ELEMENTS.

ATOMIC NUMBER 17.

PERIOD 2.

Jeff looked at the two last lines, then at the map.

Jeff: “Those don't go together.”

The trolley agreed in its least helpful voice.

Trolley: “No destination found. Please guess again.”

Ástríðr winced.

Ástríðr: “It has learned a new way to be unhelpful.”

Ms Luminara placed a hand on the input shelf. This was an authored test case, she reminded them, deliberately containing conflicting information. There was no hidden correct candidate that they could earn by trying harder.

Natalie read the atomic number first. Seventeen protons fixed chlorine. Chlorine's modern periodic-table position was period three. The period-two condition could not be true of that same element in their stated classification.

Natalie: “This differs from the first envelope. The first allowed two candidates. This one allows none if we insist that all the supplied claims hold together.”

Henry: “We should flag a conflict, not call it insufficient in exactly the same sense.”

Jackson added a third shelf beside the waiting shelf and the completed shelf. It was labeled CHECK CONFLICTING INPUTS. Beneath it he left space to state the conflict in ordinary language.

Kàra looked uneasy. She had enjoyed the clean narrowing process. It was tempting to choose atomic number as the more fundamental fact, declare chlorine, and cross out the period. That would identify which statements were inconsistent, but it would not establish which entry in a real record had been copied wrongly.

Kàra: “If atomic number defines the element, shouldn't we just use seventeen?”

Ms Luminara: “We can say what seventeen protons would imply. But if these are purported observations of one unknown case, we must still check which record is reliable. Perhaps the period was copied wrongly. Perhaps the atomic number was. The definition doesn't certify the transcription.”

Kàra sat on the stool beside the input shelf. That distinction took a moment. A powerful definition did not make every written claim of its value trustworthy.

Jeff brought the dispatch marks into view.

Jeff: “We can check whether the two slips even belong to the same case before we change either one.”

Jackson: “And we should preserve what was received so a correction can be traced.”

Ástríðr wrote a message for the fictional sender: “Atomic number 17 identifies chlorine, which is in period 3. The period-2 entry conflicts with that. Please verify the two entries.” She read it to the others before clipping it to the test envelope.

There was no accusation in it. There was also no pretending that the conflict had vanished because the group could invent a plausible explanation.

Henry pressed the trolley's report button again. It printed the specific conflict and stopped. That was a better stopping point than an endless request to guess.

Natalie took a blank test card. She wanted to know whether the repaired mechanism could identify a problem smaller than the whole answer. Using the reference beside her, she deliberately moved one occupancy count while keeping the total unchanged. She marked the card AUTHORED ERROR TEST and purported to describe a neutral ground-state fluorine atom with this complete configuration:

1s² 2s³ 2p⁴.

Jeff summed the superscripts. Two plus three plus four made nine. That total matched the number of electrons expected for neutral fluorine.

Jeff: “The total passes.”

Henry pointed to 2s³.

Henry: “The occupancy doesn't. An s subshell has one orbital and a maximum of two electrons. Three in 2s isn't allowed in this description.”

Natalie turned to her workshop sketch. The question she had written there returned: what arrangements are allowed? A correct electron budget did not guarantee that the electrons had been assigned to valid states.

Natalie: “And even an allowed total and allowed occupancies would not automatically prove the stated ground-state arrangement. We would still compare with the appropriate reference or model.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. That separates three checks without asking one to do the others' work.”

Kàra took the card back to the configuration window and compared it with the verified neutral fluorine reference, 1s² 2s² 2p⁵. She did not alter the false card silently. She marked the specific overfilled subshell and placed the corrected reference beside it.

Ástríðr: “If we told someone only ‘your answer is wrong,’ they might keep adding the total and find nine every time.”

Jeff: “Then they'd think they were missing an arithmetic mistake that wasn't there.”

Henry: “We should acknowledge the total and repair the occupancy.”

Jeff put his correct sum beside Natalie's deliberately altered configuration. He read the revised feedback with his finger covering the arithmetic. It still told him where to look. He uncovered the nine and nodded; nothing in the message required him to mistrust the part he had checked.

The trolley now had three visible outcomes. A unique supported match could be reported. Several surviving candidates could be retained pending distinguishing evidence. Conflicting inputs or an invalid configuration could be flagged with the reason for concern.

Jackson inspected the shelves. There were more of them than the original design had allowed. The trolley looked less sleek. It looked more useful.

Ms Luminara: “Any objections to a vehicle that admits when its input needs checking?”

Jeff: “Only from the souvenir stall.”

The trolley made a small, almost human-sounding laugh. Henry checked the speaker label before deciding that the metaphor could stay.

<a id="ch01-scene-19"></a>
## 19. The part of the map they had not walked

They packed the workshop before leaving.

It would have been easy for the bus to fold everything into its pockets while they watched. Instead, Ms Luminara asked which displays belonged together so the next visitor could find the explanations without reconstructing the entire day.

Natalie stacked the full fluorine and chlorine configurations beside the outer-pattern card. Henry placed the shell, subshell, and orbital diagram underneath them, with its model limit visible. Ástríðr put the retired architecture signs in the basket but kept the partial-information postcard. Jeff returned the measurement gallery controls to stationary view. Jackson left the tested sorting instructions inside the service hatch and the limitations beside the controls.

Kàra carried the larger periodic table to the courtyard directory. She opened the flap that showed the rest of the chart.

The first twenty elements occupied only a small part of it. Beyond calcium, the table continued through regions whose configurations they had not investigated. The empty-looking courtyard in the earlier rows had not been a promise that all later structure would be simple.

Kàra: “I want to look. I don't want looking to become a claim that we covered it.”

Ms Luminara: “Then look.”

They stood beneath the open map. The bus kept its engine off.

Henry identified the s and p regions that had dominated their route, then the d and f regions beyond its detailed scope. Ms Luminara explained that the simple outermost-shell valence count they had used belonged to their neutral main-group examples. Transition-metal configurations and chemical participation would require more care than copying that rule everywhere.

Natalie left a blank page after the first-twenty reference rather than trying to squeeze the distant boxes into today's account.

Natalie: “If I can't apply the same shortcut there, that doesn't make today's reasoning useless. It tells me which conditions I need to bring with it.”

Jeff: “Like a tool that works on the bolts it was made for.”

Ástríðr: “Provided we don't start insisting every bolt must fit the tool.”

At the top-right corner, helium's box caught Natalie's eye again. It had been there at the beginning, but she could now ask a more specific question about it.

Natalie: “Group eighteen, but 1s². It doesn't have an s² p⁶ outer configuration.”

Ms Luminara: “Correct. The first principal shell has only the 1s subshell, whose maximum occupancy is two. Helium belongs with the noble gases, but the familiar eight-electron outer pattern of neon and argon isn't a universal requirement for every member of that group.”

Henry pointed to the absent 1p category on the workshop diagram.

Henry: “We shouldn't invent 1p just to make the visual pattern look uniform.”

Kàra considered the helium box for a while. The exception to the simplest written pattern did not look like a flaw in the table. It looked like a reason to understand the structure rather than merely repeat the shape of a sentence.

Kàra: “So even a good pattern needs its actual domain. And there can be a deeper similarity than identical notation.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. For today's halogen clue, fluorine and chlorine fit the stated ns² np⁵ pattern directly. That success does not turn every group into the same kind of formula-matching exercise.”

They closed the broad map halfway, leaving the first-twenty route visible and the extension easy to reopen. The unvisited elements were not locked away as advanced territory for more worthy travelers. They simply had not been the question of the day.

Jeff checked the return route. The shortest way passed the probability gallery, where the lights still formed no little tracks. He suggested one last look from the doorway rather than restarting the display. Natalie agreed. She wanted to see whether the old planetary picture would spring back as soon as they left the room.

It did, a little. The image of a ball on a ring was easy to summon. She did not scold herself for it. She set beside it the model limit they had learned: a configuration is not a map of classical paths, and an orbital probability picture is not a bag of tiny visible particles.

Ástríðr watched Natalie close the notebook.

Ástríðr: “Does understanding something stop the first picture from appearing?”

Natalie: “Apparently not always. But it gives me something to do when it appears.”

Kàra nodded. She could still feel the pull of chlorine as the familiar first answer. Now that feeling came with the memory of another door answering the bell.

They went back to the porch and checked that the postcard, repaired sign, and two original candidate cards were aboard. The guest's modern briefing remained in the historical-fiction folder, separated from the record of his actual work.

Natalie tucked the chloride and argon comparison behind their neutral-atom case. On the folder's edge she wrote a question for later: If the electron counts match, what changes because the nuclei differ? She left it there without turning back toward the workshop.

Jackson took the last look at the trolley. Its shelves were empty now, ready for later cases. It did not display a grand certificate claiming to understand all chemistry. It displayed its current instructions and the particular tests the class had tried.

Jackson: “That is enough to hand over honestly.”

Jackson handed Ms Luminara the disconnected stamp, wrapped in its cloth. She pocketed it and shut the service hatch without covering its label.

Ms Luminara: “Then we can leave.”

The porch rail folded. The big map settled into Natalie's lap. The neighborhood began to shrink, but its legends remained readable until she chose to fold it herself.

<a id="ch01-scene-20"></a>
## 20. Somewhere sufficiently specified

The journey home was quiet enough to hear the pencils rolling in the tray above the windshield.

The bus no longer needed to announce every turn. Its destination board read HOME, with a small note beneath it: ROUTE CONFIRMED. Jeff checked his window compass and found that it still pointed toward ASK. He decided not to demand that every instrument in the bus do the same job.

Ástríðr leaned against the seat back and drew another porch around the postcard. The first had been narrow; this one had room for both candidate cards. She clipped fluorine on the left and chlorine on the right, preserving the record of the first incomplete address even though the delivered letter had gone to only one of them.

Henry watched her.

Henry: “Should the period slip be with them?”

Ástríðr: “Yes. On a hinge. Then we can see the problem before and after the new evidence.”

She folded a small paper tab so PERIOD 3 could cover or reveal the original two-candidate state. The device was simple enough that it did not need the bus's help. Natalie thought it was the best piece of apparatus they had made all day.

Kàra stood in the aisle while the bus waited at a red light shaped like a soft electron cloud. She held the overhead rail and watched her reflection in the dark window.

Kàra: “My first answer felt like falling into a familiar well. Once chlorine was there, it was harder to keep fluorine visible.”

She glanced at Henry before continuing.

Kàra: “That's a comparison about my attention. I haven't discovered quantum mechanics inside my hesitation.”

Henry smiled.

Henry: “The label is appreciated.”

Ms Luminara: “A metaphor returning with its passport in order. How refreshing.”

Kàra sat down beside Jeff. Her foot still tapped against the seat rail. On the hinged postcard, she lifted the period slip, checked that both names showed, and lowered it again. This time she did not need anyone to tell her which state each version represented.

Jeff unfolded the map one more time.

Jeff: “I thought the adventurous part would be going into an atom. Instead I ended up opening the service hatch and asking the old apparatus what it had actually measured.”

Ástríðr: “Was that disappointing?”

Jeff: “No. The cloud gallery was beautiful. But I liked finding the shortcut in the rollers.”

Jackson, across the aisle, was reading the instructions he had copied from the hatch. He had left room for future corrections. He did not write UNIVERSAL beside them.

Jackson: “If the next person can see why a result is unresolved, they can do something useful. The old machine made them start with a confident answer and work backward to discover the missing reason.”

Natalie opened the notebook at its first page. Her early pencil marks were still there: the almost-total beside seven, the circled only, the two seventeen labels pointing to different meanings. She could have recopied the day into a clean summary and discarded the rest. Instead she kept the repairs visible.

Between the pages was the transparent gallery sheet. The erased connecting line left a faint smear; the two dots remained clear. She put it back beside the nested-box sketch.

Natalie: “I thought I was coming to remember chlorine's address.”

Ms Luminara: “And did you?”

Natalie looked at the map rather than proving she could answer from memory.

Natalie: “Period three. Group seventeen. Atomic number seventeen. For the neutral ground-state atom, 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵.”

She closed the map halfway.

Natalie: “But I also know why the first clue wasn't enough, and why the electron total needs the neutral condition before I use it to infer proton count. That feels more portable.”

Ms Luminara: “It is. The table will still be here when you need it. We are learning how to use a map, not auditioning to become one.”

The bus turned into the familiar parking space. Its slippers had acquired question-mark-shaped mud. The porch unfolded with a faint creak, and ordinary daylight fell across the postcard mounted beside the controls.

No new envelope shot out to demand that they begin the next adventure immediately. The letterbox held its flap politely closed. Somewhere there would be more questions about brackets, ions, compounds, names, and counts. They could wait until there was time to ask them well.

Henry checked that the completed-delivery folder stayed with the postcard. Jackson checked that the waiting shelf was genuinely empty rather than hiding an abandoned parcel. Jeff held the door while Ástríðr collected the cups. Kàra watered the question-mark vine, which had drooped a little in their absence.

Natalie was last down the steps. She paused on the porch, looking at the destination board. SOMEWHERE PRECISE had sounded like a boast that morning. Now the smaller phrase underneath HOME pleased her more.

Sufficiently specified.

She could stand there without pretending to know everything. She could also leave when she knew enough for the next step.

Ms Luminara took the retired stamp from her pocket and set it inside the letterbox, still wrapped so it would not mark the next envelope by accident.

Ms Luminara: “Good work today.”

Natalie glanced once more at the little hinge beside the controls. Both versions of the address would be there when she came back.

She tucked the notebook under her arm. As she walked away, the vine behind her stirred, and one of its curled question marks opened slightly toward the light.
