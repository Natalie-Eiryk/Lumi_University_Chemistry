## The whole puzzle in a few steps

The initial clue says **neutral atom, ground state, first twenty elements**, with the outer configuration pattern **ns² np⁵**. Within that scope, fluorine and chlorine both fit. Fluorine has outer configuration 2s² 2p⁵; chlorine has 3s² 3p⁵. The unspecified n is why the pattern does not select a row.

A later observation, **period 3**, excludes fluorine and retains chlorine. Chlorine's full configuration is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵, containing 17 electrons. Because this case is neutral, it has 17 protons. Atomic number 17 identifies chlorine. This conclusion answers the stated identification question; it does not establish every property of chlorine or of an unspecified sample.

## Contextual glossary

### Atomic number and element identity

**Atomic number, Z:** the number of protons in an atom's nucleus. It identifies the element. F has Z = 9; Cl has Z = 17; Ar has Z = 18. A periodic-table group number is a different label. Chlorine happens to have both atomic number 17 and group number 17; fluorine, in the same group with Z = 9, exposes why those labels cannot be substituted.

**Proton:** positively charged nuclear particle. The story uses proton count to identify the element. It does not model the nucleus's internal dynamics or teach nuclear reactions.

**Electron:** negatively charged particle. Changing the electron count while retaining the nucleus changes charge without changing which element is present. Electrons are not little bus passengers following fixed planetary tracks.

**Atomic number versus atomic mass:** atomic number counts protons. It is not the mass of an atom, a mass number, or an average atomic weight. This chapter does not calculate isotope mixtures or molar masses.

See [scene 11](STORY.md#ch01-scene-11) and [scene 14](STORY.md#ch01-scene-14).

### Neutrality, ions, and charge

**Neutral atom:** total positive and negative charge balance; its proton and electron counts are equal. Neutrality is the needed condition when using an atom's total electron count to infer its proton count.

**Ion:** an atom or group of atoms with net electrical charge. This chapter's worked ion comparison is the monatomic chloride ion. Polyatomic ions are outside its detailed treatment.

**Charge bookkeeping:** for these proton/electron ledgers, net charge in units of the positive elementary charge is proton count minus electron count. Seventeen protons and eighteen electrons give 17 − 18 = −1, so Cl⁻. Eighteen protons and eighteen electrons give zero, so neutral Ar. Both have eighteen electrons, but they are different elements.

**Isoelectronic:** having the same number of electrons in the comparison being made. Cl⁻ and Ar are the chapter's example. Equal electron counts do not mean equal proton counts, equal sizes, or identical properties.

**Electron transfer versus its cause:** the charge ledger tells us the charge produced by a stated gain or loss. An actual transfer also requires an electron source or destination in the surroundings. Total charge is conserved when the complete system is accounted for; it need not total zero. The two story ledgers compare inventories, not a witnessed transfer. Whether a real transfer occurs depends on the system, interactions, energetics, and conditions. “The atom wants a full shell” is not a physical explanation.

See [scene 14](STORY.md#ch01-scene-14).

### Period, group, and the declared scope

**Period:** a horizontal row of the periodic table. In the neutral ground-state first-twenty examples used here, the period corresponds to the highest occupied principal level. This relationship should not be silently applied to every ion or excited state.

**Group:** a numbered column, using groups 1–18. Similar outer structures help explain recurring main-group chemical patterns. They do not imply that every group member has identical quantitative properties or reacts identically in every situation.

**Main-group scope:** the s- and p-block examples on this route, with explicit treatment of helium's 1s² configuration as a group-18 exception to the familiar ns² np⁶ pattern. The simple valence convention used here is not exported unchanged to transition metals.

**First twenty:** hydrogen through calcium. This limits the candidate set; it is not a claim that the periodic table ends at calcium. Within this scope the original ns² np⁵ clue has F and Cl as candidates. Expanding the scope without saying so would change the problem.

See [scene 2](STORY.md#ch01-scene-02), [scene 16](STORY.md#ch01-scene-16), and [scene 19](STORY.md#ch01-scene-19).

### Configuration notation and its levels

**Principal shell:** states sharing a principal quantum number n. Shells are not hard physical walls. In many-electron atoms, sharing a principal number does not make all subshell energies identical.

**Subshell:** a subdivision identified by n and a subshell type, such as 2s, 2p, or 3d. Configuration superscripts give occupancy in that named subshell.

**Orbital:** a quantum-state description used in the orbital model, associated with a spatial probability distribution. An orbital is not an orbit or a container with a material wall. An s subshell has one orbital, a p subshell three, and a d subshell five. Each orbital accommodates at most two electrons, with opposite spin labels. Those spin labels are not a claim that the electron is a classical spinning ball.

**Occupancy versus capacity:** 3p⁵ says five electrons occupy 3p; the maximum capacity of a p subshell is six. An unoccupied 3d category is not absent from the allowed shell structure. The third shell can accommodate 18 electrons across 3s, 3p, and 3d, but it does not follow that it must reach 18 before any 4s occupancy appears in ground-state atoms.

**Ground state:** the lowest-energy state of the specified atom. The chapter uses reference configurations for neutral ground-state atoms. Allowed occupancy and correct total electron count are necessary checks but are not, by themselves, proof of the ground-state arrangement.

**Excited state:** a higher-energy state of the same specified atom. Appropriate energy input can excite an atom without changing its proton count. The workshop lever is a schematic energy-accounting illustration, not a prediction of all transitions, rates, or selection rules.

**Valence electrons:** in the neutral main-group examples here, electrons in the outermost occupied principal level. Fluorine's 2s² 2p⁵ and chlorine's 3s² 3p⁵ each contain seven under that convention. More advanced contexts require checking which valence convention is intended.

See [scene 4](STORY.md#ch01-scene-04), [scene 5](STORY.md#ch01-scene-05), [scene 6](STORY.md#ch01-scene-06), and [scene 8](STORY.md#ch01-scene-08).

### One expression, three different jobs

In **3p⁵**:

- **3** labels the principal shell
- **p** identifies a subshell type
- **⁵** counts electrons occupying that 3p subshell

In **Cl⁻**, the superscript minus sign indicates the species' net charge. It does not count electrons in one subshell. Position alone does not give every superscript the same meaning.

In **ns² np⁵**, the same unspecified n applies to both outer subshells. The superscripts sum to seven outer-level electrons; they do not say the atom has seven electrons in total.

A shell summary such as chlorine's **2, 8, 7** compresses the full configuration into per-shell totals. It omits the s/p division. Five terms in chlorine's full configuration do not mean five occupied principal shells.

### Probability, evidence, and a model's limits

**Probability density:** describes the relative likelihood per unit volume of a position outcome in the model. The gallery accumulates simulated outcomes from many similarly prepared one-electron hydrogen atoms. It does not display many electrons inside a single hydrogen atom or trace one electron's path.

**Probability boundary:** a drawn orbital surface may enclose a chosen fraction of the probability. It is not a physical edge where an electron is enclosed by a shell wall.

**Incomplete identifying evidence:** uncertainty about whether the letter means F or Cl comes from missing case information. It is not evidence of a quantum superposition of elements. That epistemic uncertainty also differs from the position probabilities in the hydrogen illustration.

**Reference versus observation:** looking up both candidate configurations establishes what would be true of each candidate. It does not establish which candidate belongs to the unknown case. The period observation is useful because it is supplied for that case and distinguishes the candidates.

**Underdetermined case:** more than one candidate survives all stated conditions. **Conflicting case:** no candidate can satisfy all the supplied conditions together. Both deserve an explicit report, but the next steps differ: seek distinguishing evidence for the former; verify inconsistent inputs for the latter.

## Optional deeper supports

### Why eight is not a universal shell capacity

The second principal shell contains 2s and 2p: capacities 2 + 6 = 8. The third shell also includes 3d: capacities 2 + 6 + 10 = 18. Nevertheless, the supplied neutral ground states of potassium and calcium end in 4s¹ and 4s² while 3d is unoccupied. Allowed states, occupancy capacity, and lowest-energy arrangement are different questions. Use the verified first-twenty configurations rather than “finish every numbered floor” as a universal rule. The chapter does not teach transition-metal filling/removal rules.

### Why a same-group counterexample is useful

F and Cl share the outer ns² np⁵ pattern but have different first ionization energies: roughly 17.4 eV and 13.0 eV respectively, using the cited NIST data rounded for this comparison. First ionization energy is the minimum energy needed to separate one electron from an isolated gaseous neutral ground-state atom, leaving its singly positive ion in the ground state. An electronvolt (eV) is an energy unit; these values refer to one atom's ionization, not an energy per mole. It is not electron affinity, a universal reaction rate, or an overall “reactivity score.” One named-property difference is enough to defeat “all group members behave identically”; it does not defeat useful group patterns.

### What the historical visit contributes

Moseley's historical X-ray measurements helped give atomic number a physical basis associated with nuclear charge. Characteristic spectral evidence is different from merely assigning sequential labels. The visit uses a simplified schematic, not invented numerical laboratory data. Before the October visitor reads it, scene 10 explicitly labels the display as a retrospective summary of the investigation published in December 1913, excluding the wider 1914 survey. Modern proton/configuration language is separately briefed in scene 11. His dialogue, visit, and return are invented. Pronunciation has not been independently verified; no phonetic guide is offered. ### The shortest help ladder

1. Read the scope: atom or ion, neutral or charged, ground or excited state, first twenty or a broader set?
2. Read what the configuration gives: full electron account or only an outer pattern?
3. Keep every candidate that satisfies the stated evidence. Do not use familiarity as an exclusion rule.
4. Ask what additional observation would distinguish the remaining candidates.
5. Check a proposed answer's count, allowed occupancy, and stated state separately.
6. Report the result with the conditions and evidence that support it.
