## The formula and English bridge

### Supplied simple ionic compound formula to name

1. Identify the known ions. Use a reference when necessary.
2. Read each whole-ion count in the formula. Keep internal polyatomic-ion subscripts intact.
3. Use overall neutrality to find a missing metal-ion charge in the stated simple model.
4. Name the cation, including a Roman numeral when the chosen conventional variable-charge name needs one; then name the anion.
5. Check that translating the name back gives the same ion identities and conventional ratio.

Example: Fe₂O₃ contains three O²⁻ entries for every two iron entries in the composition ratio. The oxides total −6. The two equal-charge iron ions total +6, or +3 each. The name is iron(III) oxide. The 2 counts iron; III expresses its oxidation state. [Scene 4](STORY.md#ch04-scene-04)

### Supplied simple ionic compound name to formula

Retrieve the charged ion identities from the name and reference. Find the smallest neutral whole-ion ratio. Write the cation first. Use parentheses when more than one copy of a polyatomic ion is present. Do not turn a Roman numeral directly into a subscript.

Example: calcium nitrate uses Ca²⁺ and NO₃⁻. One calcium balances two nitrate ions, so Ca(NO₃)₂. It contains Ca 1, N 2, O 6 per formula unit's composition. [Scene 8](STORY.md#ch04-scene-08)

### Supplied binary molecular formula to name and back

The formula specifies the atoms per molecule. In this introductory naming convention, the first element keeps its name and normally omits mono- for a single atom. The second uses a count prefix and an -ide form. Keep the molecular counts; do not reduce them unless asked separately for an empirical formula.

Example: N₂O₄ is dinitrogen tetroxide. The name supplies two nitrogen atoms and four oxygen atoms per molecule. NO₂ is its empirical formula, but replacing the requested molecular formula with that ratio loses information. [Scenes 14–15](STORY.md#ch04-scene-14)

The two routes above are the chapter's introductory convention. Broader IUPAC compositional nomenclature also uses count prefixes for ionic substances: magnesium dichloride can describe MgCl₂ without asserting that it consists of molecules. A prefix is not, by itself, structural evidence. [Scenes 6](STORY.md#ch04-scene-06) and [17](STORY.md#ch04-scene-17); [nomenclature source](SOURCES.md#s09-iupac-compositional-nomenclature).

## Contextual glossary

**Chemical symbol.** An element identifier, such as C for carbon, Cl for chlorine, or Cu for copper. Capitalization and the following lowercase letter are part of the symbol. Cl is not C followed by a numeral one. [Scene 2](STORY.md#ch04-scene-02)

**Subscript.** A lowered number giving the count associated with the immediately preceding atom symbol, or repeating an entire parenthesized group. In Ca(NO₃)₂, the internal 3 belongs to nitrate and the outside 2 repeats nitrate. An omitted subscript means one in the relevant formula description. [Scene 8](STORY.md#ch04-scene-08)

**Coefficient.** A number placed before a species formula; it counts or scales whole specified entities in the present example. In 2 CO₂, there are two molecules, each with one carbon and two oxygens. The total inventory does not become a new single-molecule formula. [Scene 16](STORY.md#ch04-scene-16)

**Net charge.** The sum of all signed charges of the written species: add positive contributions and negative contributions with their signs. A superscript such as 2− reports charge rather than an atom count. In the simple proton/electron ledger, charge in units of the elementary charge is proton count minus electron count. [Scenes 2](STORY.md#ch04-scene-02) and [11](STORY.md#ch04-scene-11)

**Cation and anion.** A cation has positive net charge; an anion has negative net charge. Either may be monatomic or polyatomic. NH₄⁺ is a polyatomic cation despite containing no metal. [Scene 7](STORY.md#ch04-scene-07)

**Monatomic ion.** One atom carrying net charge, such as Cl⁻ or O²⁻. O²⁻ has one oxygen atom, not two. [Scene 12](STORY.md#ch04-scene-12)

**Polyatomic ion.** A covalently bonded group with net charge, such as NH₄⁺, NO₃⁻, or SO₄²⁻. Its charge applies to the group; internal atom counts are part of its identity. Moving or multiplying the whole ion in a formula does not mean the atoms physically travel as toy luggage. [Scenes 7–8](STORY.md#ch04-scene-07)

**Ionic compound and ionic lattice.** In the introductory cases here, cations and anions occur in a neutral overall ratio within an extended structure. A formula unit records composition. It is not automatically a little isolated molecule enclosed by a counting box. The schematic models do not supply measured crystal geometries. [Scene 3](STORY.md#ch04-scene-03)

**Formula unit.** A unit of composition for counting a substance whose conventional formula gives the relevant ratio. One formula unit's composition for (NH₄)₂CO₃ includes N 2, H 8, C 1, O 3. That inventory does not assert independent molecular packets in the solid. [Scenes 7](STORY.md#ch04-scene-07) and [18](STORY.md#ch04-scene-18)

**Neutrality check.** Sum the charges of the chosen numbers of whole ions. A neutral compound formula must total zero in this model. A selected collection of ions, such as 2 Fe³⁺ with total charge +6, need not be neutral. Neutrality alone does not prove that a named substance exists, is stable, is soluble, or can be safely made. [Scenes 4](STORY.md#ch04-scene-04), [11](STORY.md#ch04-scene-11), and [16](STORY.md#ch04-scene-16)

**Charge per ion versus total charge.** In Fe₂O₃'s supplied equal-charge model, two irons contribute +6 total but each contributes +3. The Roman numeral is III, not VI or the iron count II. [Scene 4](STORY.md#ch04-scene-04)

**Oxidation state and Roman numeral.** Stock-style names use a Roman numeral to state an element's oxidation state. In the simple monatomic-ion examples, that equals the ion charge. More broadly it is a formal electron-bookkeeping assignment, not always a literal local physical charge or a claim of free ions. [Scenes 3–5](STORY.md#ch04-scene-03) and [17](STORY.md#ch04-scene-17)

**Fixed common charge.** A naming convention treats selected familiar cations as having a known common charge, so ordinary names such as magnesium chloride omit an unnecessary numeral. This introductory label is not a claim that the element has no other chemistry under any conditions. Explicit magnesium(II) states the correct oxidation state here, even if the conventional classroom answer is shorter. [Scene 6](STORY.md#ch04-scene-06)

**Variable-charge metal name.** A name such as copper(I) chloride or copper(II) chloride distinguishes the metal oxidation state needed for the supplied compound. The numeral does not count copper atoms. [Scene 5](STORY.md#ch04-scene-05)

**-ide.** A familiar ending for monatomic-anion names, such as chloride, but not a proof that every named species is monatomic. Hydroxide OH⁻ and cyanide CN⁻ are polyatomic. In molecular names, the second element also takes an -ide form without asserting the presence of a separate anion. [Scenes 10](STORY.md#ch04-scene-10) and [14](STORY.md#ch04-scene-14)

**-ate and -ite.** Within the known pairs used here, sulfate has one more O than sulfite, and nitrate has one more O than nitrite. The ending alone does not establish every ion's starting oxygen count or charge. [Scenes 9–10](STORY.md#ch04-scene-09)

**Hydride.** H⁻ in the supplied ionic hydrides NaH and LiH. It has one proton and two electrons in the simple ion ledger. Do not confuse it with neutral H₂ or infer that hydrogen is always negative. [Scene 11](STORY.md#ch04-scene-11)

**Oxide, peroxide, oxygen.** O²⁻ is monatomic oxide; O₂²⁻ is the whole peroxide ion; O₂ is the specified neutral oxygen molecule. Lithium peroxide uses two Li⁺ for one O₂²⁻ and retains Li₂O₂. Hydrogen peroxide H₂O₂ is supplied separately as molecular; its name does not turn the molecule into a display of free ions. [Scenes 12](STORY.md#ch04-scene-12) and [15](STORY.md#ch04-scene-15)

**Molecule and molecular formula.** The specified discrete molecular entity and a formula giving its atom counts. A molecular formula alone need not give all connectivity, geometry, or properties. Preserving N₂O₄ does not claim that a symbol model fully describes its chemistry. [Scenes 14–15](STORY.md#ch04-scene-14)

**Empirical formula.** The simplest whole-number atom ratio. N₂O₄ corresponds to NO₂; H₂O₂ corresponds to HO. This ratio is a different description from atom counts per molecule. NO₂ is also a molecular formula for a different species, nitrogen dioxide; context identifies its role. [Scene 15](STORY.md#ch04-scene-15)

**Binary molecular count prefix.** Mono 1, di 2, tri 3, tetra 4, penta 5, hexa 6 in the examples here. Carbon monoxide and dinitrogen tetroxide use familiar conventional contraction; phosphorus triiodide retains repeated i. IUPAC systematic compositional naming retains the a in dinitrogen tetraoxide; the familiar tetroxide spelling used here carries the same counts. Follow the stated convention without treating that difference as a different composition. [Scene 14](STORY.md#ch04-scene-14)

**Heuristic.** A useful clue or shortcut whose scope must be checked. Metal/nonmetal composition often guides introductory classification, but ammonium salts and molecular metal oxides defeat an absolute rule. A metalloid label likewise does not identify the bonding in every compound that element forms. [Scenes 7](STORY.md#ch04-scene-07) and [17](STORY.md#ch04-scene-17)

## Optional learning supports

- Draw an arrow from name to charged ion formula and back. Include both internal atom counts and whole-ion charge on the card.
- Separate “I recognized it,” “I retrieved it without looking,” and “I reasoned correctly once given the identity.” These are different useful observations, not a rank order of people.
- Keep an old answer beside its revision if that helps locate the repaired part. A total charge in the wrong field can be true arithmetic attached to the wrong quantity.
- Try one return translation: does the name lead back to the supplied formula and category? Stop after one useful check if that is enough.
- Say which reference information is missing before guessing. An incomplete name such as iron chloride may need clarification even when the learner's reasoning is sound.
- Use readable plain text if subscripts are unavailable: Ca(NO3)2 and Mg^2+ can be unambiguous. “Fe2+” may need clarification about the intended position and role of 2; do not silently reinterpret different species as equivalent.
