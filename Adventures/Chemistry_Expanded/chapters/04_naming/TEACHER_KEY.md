# Teacher key for the Compound Naming Station

Story ID: luminara.adventure.004

## Purpose and use

Assess the separation of compound category, composition, and oxidation state. A correct answer with a shaky explanation invites one chosen transfer attempt, not an endless adaptive loop. Hints, reference use, and pausing have no penalty. Preserve the original response and record revisions separately. These are human-readable rubrics, not claims of automated semantic grading.

## Question 1 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q01

CuCl: one chloride contributes −1, so one copper contributes +1. Name: copper(I) chloride.

CuCl₂: two chlorides contribute −2, so one copper contributes +2. Name: copper(II) chloride.

The Roman numeral specifies copper's oxidation state, matching the ion charge in these examples. The subscript after Cl counts chlorides in the formula-unit ratio. Neither formula has a displayed subscript after Cu, so each ratio contains one copper.

Rubric, 4 points: both names correct, 1 point each; correct charge accounting, 1; correctly distinguishes oxidation state from subscript count, 1. If “copper two chloride” is spoken, ask what two means before deciding whether the reasoning is wrong.

First helpful prompt: “Keep the copper count and copper charge in separate spaces. What charge do all the chlorides contribute?”

## Question 2 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q02

Two NH₄⁺ ions give +2; one CO₃²⁻ gives −2. The neutral formula is (NH₄)₂CO₃ and the name is ammonium carbonate. The outside 2 multiplies every atom inside the ammonium group. Composition per formula unit: N 2, H 8, C 1, O 3, totaling 14 atoms. This is a counting description of the ratio, not a claim of separate molecular units.

It contains oppositely charged polyatomic ions despite consisting only of nonmetal elements. The bonds within those ions are covalent; the compound is ionic overall.

Rubric, 4 points: intact-ion formula and neutrality, 1; conventional name, 1; ionic-versus-internal-covalent explanation, 1; all atom counts, 1. An expanded atom list can earn the counting point but cannot replace the requested ion-preserving formula. Do not award a wrong formula merely because its total atom count matches.

First helpful prompt: “If you add a second ammonium ion, which atoms come with it?”

## Question 3 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q03

MgCl₂: magnesium chloride. The supplied Mg²⁺ and two Cl⁻ balance charge. In this introductory convention magnesium's fixed common ion charge makes a Roman numeral unnecessary; explicit magnesium(II) still expresses the correct charge. Do not classify it as chemically nonsensical.

PCl₃: phosphorus trichloride. Each molecule contains one phosphorus and three chlorine atoms.

SF₆: sulfur hexafluoride. Each molecule contains one sulfur and six fluorine atoms.

A molecular prefix di is not used before chloride in the requested ionic name magnesium chloride. Tri and hexa in the molecular names specify atoms per molecule, not charge. Broader IUPAC compositional nomenclature also permits count prefixes for ionic substances; magnesium dichloride does not, by itself, claim molecular structure. The requested convention determines the target form.

Rubric, 4 points: three correct names, 1 point each; explicit category/number distinction, 1. Handle spelling separately from composition. A correct count with a spelling error is not evidence that the learner confused charge and count.

First helpful prompt: “The problem has already supplied the category. Which naming system goes with that category?”

## Question 4 Worked answer

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q04

H₂O₂ states that each molecule contains two hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms. HO describes its simplest whole-number atom ratio, so it is the empirical formula in this context. It does not preserve the molecule's atom count. The requested molecular formula must remain H₂O₂.

N₂O₄ has two nitrogen and four oxygen atoms per molecule; NO₂ has one nitrogen and two oxygen atoms per molecule. Their simplest ratios match, but their molecular compositions differ. No conclusion about chemical equilibrium or reaction conditions is needed or licensed by reducing the notation.

Rubric, 4 points: original molecular count, 1; correct meaning of empirical formula, 1; rejects replacement for the stated task, 1; correct N₂O₄/NO₂ distinction, 1.

First helpful prompt: “Are you describing a ratio or the contents of one molecule?”

## Question 5 Four oxygen and chlorine tags

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q05

- O₂: one neutral molecule containing two oxygen atoms; the lowered 2 is an atom count within the molecule.
- O²⁻: one monatomic oxide ion containing one oxygen atom, with net charge −2. Its raised 2− is charge, not a two-atom count.
- O₂²⁻: one peroxide ion containing two oxygen atoms with net charge −2 on the whole group. It is not two separate oxide ions; two oxide ions would have total charge −4.
- 2 Cl⁻: two monatomic chloride ions. Each contains one chlorine atom and carries −1. Combined: two chlorine atoms and charge −2. The leading coefficient counts whole chloride ions.

Useful evidence: the learner distinguishes species identity, atoms per species, number of species, and combined charge. No actual reaction or sample preparation follows from juxtaposing these symbols. See [Scenes 2](STORY.md#ch04-scene-02), [12](STORY.md#ch04-scene-12), and [16](STORY.md#ch04-scene-16).

## Question 6 A Roman numeral from a ledger

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q06

For supplied Fe₂O₃, three O²⁻ contribute −6. The two iron ions contribute +6 total, so each is +3 in the equal-charge simple ionic model: 2q + 3(−2) = 0, q = +3, with q measured in elementary-charge units. Name: iron(III) oxide. The subscript 2 counts iron in the formula-unit ratio; III records the iron oxidation state, matching ion charge here.

The different name iron(II) oxide supplies Fe²⁺. With O²⁻, a 1:1 neutral ratio gives FeO. Thus replacing III by II changes the named composition; it is not an optional spelling choice. Do not generalize the equal-charge assumption to every iron oxide or mixed-valence compound. See [Scene 4](STORY.md#ch04-scene-04).

## Question 7 Two ways a name supplies charge information

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q07

Magnesium chloride: MgCl₂. Copper(II) chloride: CuCl₂. Each supplied +2 cation needs two Cl⁻. In the introductory conventional name, magnesium's common fixed +2 charge is implicit. Copper's variable-charge naming supplies the chosen oxidation state with II. Same charge arithmetic does not require identical wording conventions.

“Magnesium dichloride” is a valid broader compositional description, but the requested introductory ionic naming convention uses magnesium chloride. Count prefixes are not restricted to molecular substances in all nomenclature systems. “Magnesium(II)” explicitly states the correct oxidation state here and is not chemically impossible, though unnecessary in the conventional introductory answer. Distinguish requested style, valid broader nomenclature, and actual chemical errors. See [Scenes 5–6](STORY.md#ch04-scene-05).

## Question 8 Repair the elevator output

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q08

One Ca²⁺ and two NO₃⁻ give +2 − 2 = 0. Conventional formula: Ca(NO₃)₂, calcium nitrate. The outside 2 repeats all of NO₃. Per formula unit's composition: Ca 1, N 2, O 6.

CaNO₆ retained only one nitrogen while doubling oxygen. A second nitrate includes its nitrogen; correct O count alone cannot establish correct formula or ion preservation. An ungrouped atom inventory may describe totals, but the requested conventional formula should preserve the recognizable nitrate units. Formula units are composition bookkeeping for an extended ionic substance, not evidence of isolated Ca(NO₃)₂ molecules. See [Scene 8](STORY.md#ch04-scene-08).

## Question 9 What the suffix can and cannot do

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q09

Within each supplied pair, the -ate ion has one more oxygen than its corresponding -ite ion, and the pair shares a charge: sulfate/sulfite are −2; nitrate/nitrite are −1. The comparison does not establish a universal starting oxygen number or a universal charge. Nitrate has three O while sulfate has four; their charges differ.

CaSO₃ is calcium sulfite and CaSO₄ is calcium sulfate. In each, Ca²⁺ and the specified −2 anion balance 1:1. Balancing establishes the ratio; the supplied ion identity establishes whether the anion is sulfite or sulfate. A mnemonic may help retrieve an already learned pair without deriving all unknown ions. See [Scenes 9–10](STORY.md#ch04-scene-09).

## Question 10 An ending is not an atom counter

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q10

A repaired claim: familiar monatomic anions commonly use an element stem plus -ide, but the ending does not guarantee that every ion is monatomic. Chloride is monatomic; hydroxide and cyanide are polyatomic established names.

Mg²⁺ requires two OH⁻, giving Mg(OH)₂, magnesium hydroxide. Total O 2 and H 2 per formula unit's composition. O₂H would specify a different internal atom count, not two OH groups, and no supported ion identity or charge follows from inventing that string. Preserve the given hydroxide ion and multiply the whole group. See [Scene 10](STORY.md#ch04-scene-10).

## Question 11 The negative hydrogen card

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q11

Na⁺ and H⁻ balance 1:1: NaH, sodium hydride. H⁻ retains one proton and has two electrons, so charge in units of e is 1 − 2 = −1. Relative to neutral H, this ledger contains one additional electron without changing element identity. The comparison does not establish a formation mechanism or its energetics.

H₂ is the supplied neutral diatomic hydrogen molecule containing two H atoms. H⁻ is a monatomic negative ion. The subscript and charge sign denote different quantities. The verified hydride identity directly defeats “hydrogen must always be positive.” This does not mean hydrogen is always negative or that every metal–hydrogen material obeys one simple model. No synthesis method, reaction condition, or handling guidance is implied. See [Scene 11](STORY.md#ch04-scene-11).

## Question 12 Reduce the right things

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q12

Case A: two Li⁺ balance one O₂²⁻, so Li₂O₂, lithium peroxide. The whole-ion counts 2:1 are already in lowest terms. Its internal O₂ subscript is part of the supplied peroxide ion. Dividing the visible atomic subscripts would yield LiO, the simplest Li:O atom ratio, but would not preserve the requested conventional peroxide composition. Do not treat the two oxygens as two O²⁻ ions.

Case B: two Mg²⁺ and two O²⁻ can be represented in their simplest neutral whole-ion ratio as one of each, MgO, magnesium oxide. The reduction removes a common factor from the numbers of intact monatomic ions. Each ion's identity and charge remain unchanged.

The rule is not “never reduce ionic formulas” or “always reduce every subscript.” Preserve the verified whole ions while finding the conventional simplest neutral ratio. Different atom counts may serve other expressly requested descriptions. See [Scene 12](STORY.md#ch04-scene-12).

## Question 13 Two molecules do not become one bigger molecule

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q13

Each CO₂ molecule contains C 1 and O 2. Two such molecules contain total C 2 and O 4. The leading 2 counts molecules; the subscript 2 counts oxygen atoms per molecule. The name of each compound remains carbon dioxide.

Replacing 2 CO₂ by a single purported molecular formula C₂O₄ would not preserve the statement that there are two separate CO₂ molecules. An atom inventory does not establish connectivity, molecular identity, or existence of a larger molecule. The task does not ask whether any other species with such an inventory exists. See [Scene 16](STORY.md#ch04-scene-16).

## Question 14 The honest edge of a naming rule

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q14

Ammonium sulfate defeats the “no metal must mean molecular” half. OsO₄ defeats the “metal must mean ionic” half. Element categories can be useful introductory clues; the supplied or verified compound identity takes precedence over an absolute shortcut.

A checker must not reject the recognized name osmium tetroxide solely because osmium is a metal. Osmium(VIII) oxide is also recognized. A particular task may request a naming convention or an instructor-preferred form, which should be stated or clarified rather than inferred from the worksheet's existence. Assigning oxygen −2 in neutral OsO₄ gives q + 4(−2) = 0 and formal osmium oxidation state +8. VIII is not a claim of a free Os⁸⁺ ion inside the molecule. This boundary example is not a lesson in all transition-metal compounds or a request to handle osmium tetroxide. See [Scene 17](STORY.md#ch04-scene-17).

## Interpreting evidence without overclaiming

The original optional 16-point scheme for q01–q04 is retained as a local formative convenience, not a mastery diagnosis. New questions have no compulsory point scheme. Prefer feedback on the demonstrated relationship: category selection, species identity, charge per ion versus total, whole-ion preservation, molecular atom counts, or naming convention.

A spelling slip such as a contraction error should not automatically erase sound composition reasoning. Distinguish a genuine typo from the IUPAC systematic dinitrogen tetraoxide and the familiar dinitrogen tetroxide used here; both preserve N₂O₄'s composition. A correct final name with an explanation based on a copied subscript warrants a chosen discriminating example, not an unlimited loop of testing. Accept unambiguous plain-text chemical notation, speech, or labeled sketches when those preserve the intended quantities; ask about ambiguous notation rather than silently normalizing it.

Reference use, a full worked explanation, stopping, and later revision are legitimate. Recognition of a card, retrieval of its identity without support, and reasoning from the supplied identity are different observations. A correct response does not prove transfer to every naming family, and these keys do not implement or certify automated semantic grading.
