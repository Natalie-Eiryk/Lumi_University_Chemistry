## 1 A destination called two

The bus's destination sign said TWO.

It had said TWO at the last six junctions. At the seventh, the letters grew larger. A little brass bell rang, as though this improvement deserved recognition.

Ms Luminara: “The navigation system has become very confident about one thing.”

Jeff leaned into the aisle to see whether the windshield offered anything more useful. Beyond it, two roads divided into four, then disappeared beneath a broad glass roof. Signals clicked through strings of chemical symbols. Every few seconds a voice announced a number, paused, and announced the same number more firmly.

Jeff: “Does it know which two?”

Henry: “It hasn't even specified two what.”

Natalie looked down at the page on her lap. Before boarding, she had written FeCl₂ on one side and left an empty space on the other. The formula felt like something she could examine. The English name felt like a word she ought to remember. Between them she had drawn an arrow that presently went nowhere.

She had taught physics. She knew the uneasy sensation of understanding a relationship while being unable to retrieve the word everyone else used for it. She also knew how easily a teacher could misread a blank space as an absence of thought. None of that had filled this particular blank.

Natalie: “I can see that there are different jobs for the numbers. I still can't make the words arrive in the right order.”

Ms Luminara glanced at the notebook without taking it.

Ms Luminara: “Then we shall visit the place that claims to do exactly that.”

Kàra: “Does its sign inspire confidence?”

Ms Luminara: “Not even slightly. Seatbelts, everyone.”

The bus folded its mirrors, lifted its wheels, and slid sideways between two enormous departure boards. Natalie caught her pencil before it escaped. Ástríðr caught the notebook. Jeff, who had chosen the front window specifically to see the landing, kept his face toward it despite tightening both hands around the seatbelt.

They settled beside a platform where formula cards traveled along silver rails. The cards were taller than the bus. FeCl₂ approached a gate marked IRON TWO. CO₂ followed close behind, one corner of its paper sleeve caught beneath the iron card's retaining clip. A mechanical finger pointed at the little 2 on each card and sent both toward the same tunnel.

For a moment everything seemed to work. The gate opened, the rail hummed, and a green lamp flashed READY. Then two different destination locks tried to accept the same command. A padded barrier lowered between them. The rail stopped with a disappointed chime.

A smaller board lit up: SUCCESSFULLY IDENTIFIED TWO.

Jackson: “It thinks its job is finished.”

Ms Luminara parked. Her dress, which had carried a pattern of ordinary railway tickets, now displayed parentheses and little raised signs. She examined a ticket near her sleeve and turned it right way up.

Ms Luminara: “The station has mistaken reading a mark for understanding a message.”

Jeff: “Are we the replacement machinery?”

Ms Luminara: “Only if you volunteer. Nothing here can hurt you, and the teaching displays can wait. The help drawer opens before anybody answers.”

Jeff opened it at once. It contained ion cards, a prefix list, blank labels, and a cushion of improbable softness. Beneath those lay a large lever marked PAUSE, accompanied by instructions explaining that it could also be operated by speaking, pointing, or asking somebody else.

Jeff: “I approve of the equipment.”

Kàra remained seated while the others stood. Her knee was bouncing against the underside of the folding table. Outside, the rails multiplied behind layers of glass. She could almost feel her thoughts taking every branch at once.

Ms Luminara offered her the station map rather than the driving controls.

Ms Luminara: “Would seeing the routes before we move help?”

Kàra: “Yes. I would like fewer destinations in my head.”

They unfolded the map across two seats. It showed a broad central platform, an ionic district, a molecular tunnel, a reference library, and a service bridge whose label had peeled away. At the center stood a brass machine called the UNIVERSAL NUMBER INTERPRETER. Its operating instructions occupied one line: FIND A NUMBER AND SAY IT.

Natalie laughed, then stopped because the instruction resembled her own instinct more closely than she liked. She had looked at a subscript and hoped it would tell her what name to say. She simply expected herself to do the process with more dignity than a brass machine.

Natalie: “I was looking for a direct arrow.”

Henry: “From which information to which information?”

She tapped the blank between formula and name. She had not written that part.

Ástríðr took a blank label from the drawer and put it beside the arrow: WHAT DOES EACH PART MEAN?

Ástríðr: “A bridge needs somewhere to stand in the middle.”

The station bell rang again. Farther along the platform, more cards joined the queue. Jackson counted them, then stopped counting and checked where they were supposed to go. Their destinations were not dangerous reactors or hospitals waiting for real supplies. They were teaching displays: a lattice gallery, a molecule theater, a set of ion cabinets. But a wrong label would still make the next display tell the wrong story.

Jackson: “If we merely open the barrier, the wrong information travels farther.”

Ms Luminara: “Then our first useful action is leaving it closed.”

Jeff carried the cushion down the bus steps. Natalie brought the notebook with its incomplete arrow. Kàra took the map after marking the bus's parking place with a star. The platform had become quiet enough to hear their footsteps, and the large, confident TWO remained above them, waiting to be asked what it meant.

## 2 Behind the brass panel

The interpreter's maintenance compartment opened when Henry pressed a button labeled QUESTIONS ABOUT THIS MACHINE. Inside were two routes, IONIC COMPOUNDS and BINARY MOLECULAR COMPOUNDS. Their wires had been twisted together with a ribbon that said SIMPLIFIED FOR CONVENIENCE.

Henry did not pull the ribbon off immediately. He asked Jackson to photograph the arrangement with the station's record camera. If they repaired it without preserving the fault, they might never discover which other signs had inherited it.

Jackson: “There could be old outputs that still look official.”

A paper roll emerged from the side of the machine. It listed FeCl₂ → IRON TWO, CO₂ → CARBON TWO, and Fe₂O₃ → IRON TWO OXIDE. The last entry had a proud stamp reading FIRST NUMBER FOUND.

Ástríðr: “Consistent. That is not always a compliment.”

Jeff found a second roll in a narrow drawer. This one held the complete incoming messages, including descriptions such as supplied simple ionic compound and specified molecular formula. The interpreter had discarded those phrases before reading the numbers.

Jeff: “The information wasn't missing. It threw it away.”

He laid the complete record beneath its shortened copy. Their edges matched, but the shortened copy stopped just where the useful sentence began. Jackson followed a thin return line on the wiring plan until it disappeared behind a riveted plate. Beside it, a tiny lamp marked REPRINT glowed through a small window in the brass.

Jackson: “There's another way to get a label after the main printer. I can't see where this line ends yet.”

Henry marked it on the photograph. They could test the route when they had a sound name to send back through it. For now, Jeff clipped the intact records together so they could not accidentally work from the shortened copies.

Natalie knelt beside him. Each card had entered with a context line. The machine's neatness had made its work worse. It had separated the visible formula from the kind of thing the formula represented, then treated the surviving marks as sufficient.

Natalie: “I do that with vocabulary sometimes. I keep the name and lose the sentence that told me when to use it.”

Ms Luminara: “And then the word looks as though it has betrayed you. Very inconsiderate of a word.”

She set three demonstration tiles on the floor: Cl₂, Cl⁻, and 2 Cl⁻. They were large enough to read from the bench. She stated that this was a notation comparison, not a chemical reaction.

Ms Luminara: “These are different messages. Chlorine as the specified diatomic molecule has two chlorine atoms. Chloride is the monatomic negative ion. The coefficient in the last tile says we are counting two chloride ions.”

Before following the numbers, Henry checked the letters. Cl identified chlorine; its lowercase l was not a numeral one, and C alone identified carbon.

Henry placed a finger below the low 2, then beside the raised minus sign, without moving either.

Henry: “The location matters before the arithmetic starts.”

Ástríðr tried the interpreter on all three tiles. It gave a number for the first and third, then complained that the middle message contained no useful information. The minus sign did not fit its favorite question.

Kàra stood back from the open panel. She had expected a spectacular failure: sparks, broken gears, a wheel that had flown off. Instead, the machinery was doing its assigned task very well. The task itself was too small.

Kàra: “It's like measuring every road by length and calling that a direction.”

Ms Luminara: “A useful comparison. What is its limit?”

Kàra: “Road length and direction are physical quantities. Here we're separating several meanings carried by notation. The machine is the thing doing the muddling.”

She sounded impatient with the qualification, then realized she did not resent it. Naming the limit kept the picture from becoming another bad shortcut. She sketched two separate boxes on the maintenance glass: COUNTS and CHARGES.

Natalie added a third: KIND OF THING REPRESENTED.

Jackson asked whether a fourth should record what the station was being asked to do. Naming a known compound was different from proving the identity of an unknown sample. Finding an empirical formula was different from preserving a molecular formula. The same incoming symbols might support different tasks, provided the task was actually stated.

They added REQUEST.

Under the four boxes, Kàra unrolled the machine's long instruction strip. FIND A NUMBER AND SAY IT was only its headline. Farther down, little fallback rules guessed a category, guessed an ion, or shortened a formula whenever the missing context was needed. The faults on the departures board began to look less like unrelated accidents.

Kàra: “It keeps guessing back the information it threw away.”

Jackson: “Then we'll follow one complete record through each route. Fixing the entrance won't tell us what the next room still ignores.”

He pinned the strip beside the map. Henry selected INSPECT, and the two towering formula cards shrank to fit the work tray. The CO₂ sleeve was now free of the iron card, though its corner remained bent. They left it at the molecular entrance with its original message attached. The first two departures would stay in sight until both could travel for reasons they could explain.

The panel looked less elegant. The original single wire had been satisfying in a way that four labeled boxes were not. Natalie felt a small reluctance about abandoning it. There was comfort in believing chemistry contained one hidden trick that would make all the names fall into place.

Ms Luminara sat on the edge of the platform rather than standing over their work.

Ms Luminara: “We can make a dependable route without pretending that every route is the same.”

Jeff: “And we can keep the drawers.”

Ms Luminara: “The drawers are staying.”

They agreed to test the ionic route first, using the original FeCl₂ card and its supplied identity. Henry copied the machine's proposed name beside the card instead of erasing it. Ástríðr put a removable tag on it reading PREDICTION. Jackson disconnected only the shared output wire, leaving the rest of the station paused. Kàra marked the first turning on her map, then folded the instruction strip inside it.

Natalie turned past her original blank answer and began a fresh page in her notebook. Formula on the left. English on the right. In the middle she left enough space for the reasoning, even though that meant turning the page sideways.

The bus's doors opened. From the ionic tunnel came a low mechanical humming, like a hundred suspended strings being sounded together. Jeff chose the window again. He wanted to see the system the formula described, because a pair of written twos had already proved insufficient company.

Ms Luminara took the steering wheel.

Ms Luminara: “Next stop, a place where one formula is much smaller than what it describes.”

## 3 The iron platform with no little trios

The tunnel opened into a vast repeating display. Iron and chloride markers extended above and below the bus, bright against a dark background. The representation simplified the solid to emphasize composition and charge bookkeeping. A small legend said that the positions and sizes were schematic, not a measured crystal structure at a uniform scale.

Natalie had expected groups of three: one iron, two chlorides, then a little space before the next set. Instead, the pattern continued past the windows. The bus passed between markers that belonged to the surrounding arrangement rather than to neatly fenced individual packages.

Henry: “There isn't a separate FeCl₂ molecule waiting at each stop.”

Ms Luminara: “Right. We are representing an ionic solid. The formula records its composition ratio. A formula unit is a way to count that composition, not evidence of a tiny isolated trio.”

Jeff pressed his palm against the safe display window. Several markers lit up together. The bus had no physical contact with real ions, but the view allowed him to choose a region and inspect its record. One iron entry appeared beside two chloride entries.

Jeff: “The record groups them for counting. The solid doesn't have to be divided along those lines.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly.”

The station's wrong label floated through the display: IRON TWO. It cast a shadow across the formula but gave no account of where its two came from.

Natalie opened the reference drawer. The chloride card read Cl⁻, chloride ion, net charge −1. She noticed that the card gave the name and charge together. She had sometimes tried to memorize them as separate facts, then wondered why one arrived without the other.

Natalie: “If I know chloride is minus one, the two chlorides contribute minus two altogether.”

Henry placed two counters marked −1 on the console. Across from them sat one empty iron space.

Henry: “The compound's overall formula is neutral. One iron entry must contribute plus two in this simple ionic bookkeeping.”

Ástríðr took the +2 ion card from the drawer and put it in the space. The two sides of the charge ledger summed to zero. A bell rang once, quietly, without opening the next gate.

Ástríðr: “Iron and chloride have settled their accounts.”

Ms Luminara: “Our accounting is balanced. The ions haven't negotiated anything. The actual material's structure and stability involve physical interactions and energy. Today we're checking whether our description agrees with the supplied identities.”

Ástríðr nodded and changed her label from AGREEMENT to NEUTRAL TOTAL. She liked the first phrase, but she did not need to keep it as an explanation of matter.

Ms Luminara printed the conventional name on a fresh card: iron(II) chloride. Under it she printed the spoken version, iron two chloride, and pointed to the parentheses surrounding the Roman numeral.

Ms Luminara: “II specifies iron's oxidation state, plus two. For this supplied simple ionic case, it matches the monatomic iron ion's charge. It is not a count of two iron atoms.”

Natalie wrote the sentence in her own order. Chloride name from ion vocabulary. Chloride charge from the reference. Chloride count from the formula. Iron charge inferred from neutrality. Roman numeral records that iron state.

The arrow across her notebook finally crossed the gap. It had become several short arrows, which was less graceful and far more useful.

Kàra: “The subscript two and the Roman two happen to have equal numerical values here. That makes the wrong shortcut look good.”

Jackson leaned forward. He had been waiting for precisely that problem.

Jackson: “If we test only FeCl₂, the broken machine can pass.”

They compared two possible explanations for the correct name. One read the subscript after chlorine and copied it. The other counted chloride charge and found the charge required per iron. Both produced II here. A successful output alone did not identify which mechanism had produced it.

Natalie had already begun to close her notebook. The right name had given her the relief she wanted, and she disliked having that relief called provisional.

Natalie: “What would make the two methods give different answers?”

She kept a finger between the pages. If they found such a card, she would have to open the notebook again.

Henry: “One where the metal subscript and the metal oxidation state aren't the same.”

Kàra pointed back toward the preserved paper roll. Fe₂O₃ waited on the central platform. Its first number was two, its second was three, and the interpreter had already chosen which one it liked.

Before leaving, Jeff asked the bus to dim its motion effects. He wanted a still view of the lattice. Ms Luminara parked on a transparent observation shelf. No test appeared. No timer began. The markers continued beyond the edges of the window while the class sat with the difference between a formula they could hold and a structure they could not divide into the little packages they had first imagined.

Natalie drew a small rectangle around one counting selection, then made the edges dotted. She wrote counting choice beside it. She did not draw bonds she had not inspected or claim the model supplied a full crystal geometry.

Jeff: “I thought the picture would explain the name directly.”

Ms Luminara: “It explains what sort of thing we are naming. That's part of the bridge.”

Jeff kept looking until the continuous pattern became easier to see than the nonexistent trios. Then he asked to move on. The bus reversed gently from the shelf, carrying a correct name whose justification now mattered more than the fact that two happened to equal two.

## 4 The six that belonged to nobody alone

Fe₂O₃ occupied the next platform like a large unopened envelope. The proposed label, IRON TWO OXIDE, hung beside it. Kàra asked that they leave the label visible while they tested it.

Kàra: “If we erase it now, I'll remember that it was wrong but not why it seemed reasonable.”

Ms Luminara: “An excellent reason to leave it.”

The bus entered a second schematic lattice display. Its counting window showed two iron entries and three oxide entries. Ástríðr found the supplied oxide card, O²⁻, and placed three copies on a magnetic board. They stuck with a satisfying thump.

Ástríðr: “Three minus twos. A very grumpy six.”

Henry: “Negative six in total.”

Ástríðr: “My grumpy six accepts the correction.”

Jeff smiled, then looked at the two empty iron spaces. He put a +6 card across them both. The board's total became zero. Its name printer, however, offered iron(VI) oxide.

He frowned before anyone spoke.

Jeff: “I gave the whole positive side a label. It treated the label as one iron's charge.”

Henry slid a divider between the iron spaces without moving the +6 card. The card now straddled the divider awkwardly. It was obvious in the picture that the total belonged to both entries together.

Henry: “There are two irons in the ratio, and we're using the supplied simple model in which they have the same charge.”

Kàra dragged the +6 counter toward the divider. It separated into two +3 counters. Each moved over one iron space. The overall ledger remained neutral, but the per-ion field now showed +3.

Kàra: “Plus three each. I was picturing balanced forces for a second, but these are charge totals. Zero total charge doesn't say all forces vanish.”

Ms Luminara looked pleased at the correction rather than disappointed that it had been needed.

Ms Luminara: “That distinction saves the metaphor. In a real solid, attractions and repulsions occur throughout a structure. Our little ledger is not calculating those forces or proving the structure's stability.”

Natalie wrote 2q + 3(−2) = 0 under the picture. Then she wrote 2q = +6 and q = +3. The equation fitted easily once she knew what q represented. Before that, the symbol would only have hidden the same uncertainty behind algebra.

Natalie: “The unknown is each iron ion's charge in elementary-charge units in this model. It isn't the total iron charge and it isn't the number of irons.”

They printed iron(III) oxide. The interpreter placed its old name beside the new one, and Henry made both explanations visible. The old route had read the iron subscript. The repaired route had determined the iron oxidation state.

Jackson asked them to inspect what the original wrong name would mean if someone translated it back.

The request surprised Natalie. She had considered the naming job complete. Now she looked at iron(II) oxide as a new incoming message. Iron(II) supplied Fe²⁺. Oxide supplied O²⁻. One of each balanced the charge, giving the conventional formula FeO for that simple composition.

Jackson: “The wrong name doesn't merely sound a little off. It sends the next reader to a different formula.”

The station projected FeO beside Fe₂O₃. They did not share the same ratio. A correction that looked like changing one Roman numeral had repaired a meaningful chemical claim.

Ástríðr tilted her head toward Jeff.

Ástríðr: “Your six was useful. It found a second kind of wrong answer.”

Jeff: “I'd prefer to have found it on somebody else's board.”

Ástríðr: “Fair. But we would still want it found.”

He kept the +6 card. He wrote total across its top so that it could return to legitimate work. The mistake had not made the number itself bad; it had put a true total in the wrong field.

A narrow service staircase rose beside the lattice chamber. Kàra asked whether they could walk it instead of using the bus for the last short stretch. Her legs wanted a task. The safe teaching platform felt solid beneath her feet as she climbed with Natalie. Through its clear side they could watch the display from above.

Natalie: “I used to think an explanation should become shorter every time I understood it better.”

Kàra: “Did this one?”

Natalie considered the crowded notebook. The final check could be short: three oxides, minus six; two irons, plus three each. But the route by which she trusted that check was longer than the check itself.

Natalie: “The usable part got shorter. The support under it got bigger.”

Below them, Henry tested whether the name printer could distinguish TOTAL CATION CHARGE from CHARGE PER CATION. Jeff supplied his labeled six. The printer refused to use it as a Roman numeral until it had also received the cation count.

The refusal sounded like a small click. Kàra had expected the successful repair to be louder. Instead, the machine simply stopped doing something unwarranted.

At the top of the staircase, Ms Luminara opened a door marked RETURN DIRECTION. A rack of English names waited beyond it, with the formulas concealed on their backs.

Ms Luminara: “Our bridge has carried us one way. Shall we see whether it has a second lane?”

Natalie tucked the notebook under her arm. She wanted the second lane, though wanting it did not make the unfamiliar words any less unfamiliar. This time she knew which drawer she could open before taking the first step.

## 5 The return lane

The service staircase brought them to the rack of names, above the iron gallery. It had two counters, one labeled READ and one labeled BUILD. Between them lay a long felt-covered table. Natalie chose a chair at its end so she could keep her notebook open without balancing it on her knees.

The first envelope said iron(III) chloride.

She knew the words now. That did not immediately produce a formula. For a brief, irritating moment, she remembered the previous formula Fe₂O₃ so vividly that its subscripts offered themselves as if they belonged to the new request.

Natalie: “I'm carrying the last picture into the next question.”

Ms Luminara: “Would you like the whole worked route first?”

Natalie: “Yes. Then I'd like to rebuild it myself with the name still in front of me.”

There was no ceremony about accepting the help. Ms Luminara put Fe³⁺ on one side of the table and Cl⁻ on the other. The Roman numeral supplied the iron oxidation state, matching the ion charge in this case. The chloride reference supplied −1. One iron required three chlorides for a neutral total. She placed the three chloride cards, then wrote FeCl₃ below them.

Ms Luminara: “The three in the name and the three in this formula have a relationship. They still do different jobs.”

Natalie moved the cards away and reconstructed the route. She spoke softly, mostly to make the sequence available to herself: iron(III), one selected cation at plus three; chloride, minus one per ion; three chlorides; smallest whole-ion ratio, one to three; FeCl₃.

Henry lifted an unused label as Natalie paused, then caught her looking at it. He put it on the felt, facedown. She moved the last chloride herself.

Natalie: “Thanks. I was still going.”

Henry: “I couldn't tell.”

Natalie: “Neither could I for a second.”

She checked her three chloride cards against the written name.

Natalie: “Now I can explain where the formula came from. If I simply copied a three, I couldn't know whether it went after Fe or Cl.”

The reverse side of the envelope confirmed the formula. Natalie left it turned over beside her work instead of quickly hiding the answer, as though seeing support longer than necessary might spoil something.

Jeff chose the second envelope. It said copper(II) chloride. The copper identity was unfamiliar enough to prevent him from borrowing iron's picture, but the name supplied the part he needed. He found Cu²⁺ in the drawer, matched it with two Cl⁻ cards, and wrote CuCl₂.

Then Ástríðr opened an envelope labeled iron chloride. No Roman numeral appeared.

Ástríðr: “The station has become economical again.”

Two transparent templates waited behind the name: FeCl₂ and FeCl₃. Both were among the known simple compounds represented in the exercise. The incomplete wording did not distinguish them.

Kàra wanted to choose the first because it had arrived earlier. She caught the impulse as she reached. Familiarity was supplying confidence that the envelope had not supplied information.

Kàra: “We need which iron state. Or a formula. Something that identifies which compound the sender means.”

Jackson checked the incoming record. It contained no additional detail. He placed the envelope on a shelf marked NEEDS CLARIFICATION rather than forcing it into either output slot.

The machine buzzed.

MACHINE: “NO ANSWER PRODUCED.”

Henry: “An answer was produced. The given name is insufficient to choose between these represented possibilities.”

Ms Luminara: “Please engrave that sentiment more briefly before it becomes a platform announcement.”

Henry wrote MORE IDENTITY INFORMATION NEEDED. The machine accepted this as an outcome. Its count of unfinished cards did not drop, but its count of unexplained failures did.

Natalie was surprised by how relieved she felt. Sometimes she could not remember a name. Sometimes the information did not select a name at all. Those experiences could feel identical inside her head until she examined the question.

She drew a small box beside the return lane: Does the message tell me enough? Under it she put the incomplete envelope's words and the two possible formulas. Her notebook now held uncertainty that belonged to the input, not to her ability.

At the far end of the table, Jeff began putting every metal name into parentheses with a numeral. If a Roman numeral fixed one kind of ambiguity, it seemed prudent to use one everywhere. Sodium(I) chloride. Magnesium(II) chloride. Aluminum(III) oxide.

He held up the list.

Jeff: “Can we simply make this compulsory?”

Henry started to answer, then looked at Ms Luminara. He knew the conventional introductory names did not require those numerals, but he did not want to describe a chemically meaningful charge label as nonsense merely because it was unnecessary in their chosen style.

Ms Luminara slid a fresh card across the felt.

Ms Luminara: “Let's inspect what the usual name already tells its reader. Our next platform is about the information a naming convention leaves implicit.”

Natalie closed the envelope marked iron chloride without filling its blank. She felt less like she was leaving a question behind than giving it an honest address. The second lane of the bridge had revealed something the first lane could not: a name was a compressed instruction, and compression worked only when sender and reader shared enough of the code.

## 6 What the ordinary name already carries

The next room resembled a conservatory, except that the plants were tall racks of element cards. Sunlight moved across their brass labels. Sodium occupied a rack near the door, magnesium another, and aluminum a third. Beside each sat the common ion card used for this introductory station: Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺.

A notice over the racks said COMMON IONS IN THIS REFERENCE SET. Henry read it twice. He liked that better than ALWAYS.

Ms Luminara: “For the ordinary introductory naming convention we're using, these metals have a fixed common ion charge. The name sodium chloride lets us use sodium's familiar +1 ion without a Roman numeral. Magnesium chloride uses magnesium's familiar +2 ion.”

Jeff put his longer names beside the rack labels. None burst into flames. The station made no accusation of impossible chemistry.

Jeff: “Then sodium(I) isn't a different count of sodium. It's just extra charge information the ordinary name didn't need here.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. A course may request the conventional shorter name. We should distinguish that preference from saying a stated correct oxidation state is chemically meaningless.”

Natalie compared two cards: magnesium chloride and copper(II) chloride. Both translated, for their supplied simple ionic identities, to one +2 cation and two −1 chloride ions. One name supplied the charge through a shared common-ion convention. The other supplied it explicitly through the Roman numeral.

Natalie: “The arithmetic doesn't become different just because the name tells me the charge in a different way.”

Henry: “And a missing numeral isn't permission to invent a charge. We need the reference convention.”

Kàra wandered to the back of the room, where a periodic table had been printed on a folding screen. She traced the first two groups with a finger. Common Group 1 +1 and Group 2 +2 patterns could help her remember several ion cards. She liked the feeling of a physical organizing principle under the vocabulary.

Then she reached a sign that said MORE ELEMENTS BEYOND THIS INTRODUCTORY ROUTE and stopped herself from turning the nearby transition-metal block into one more rigid pattern.

Kàra: “The table gives useful clues. It doesn't hand me a universal list of every charge an element can show.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly. We will use the familiar patterns where they are warranted and verified identities where they are needed. Today's job is not deriving all oxidation states from a map.”

Ástríðr found a small drawer with zinc and silver reference cards. It gave Zn²⁺ and Ag⁺ as the common introductory ions and noted the usual omission of Roman numerals in their fixed-common-charge names. She almost placed them in the same vertical columns as magnesium and sodium, then recognized that she was arranging by the charge cards rather than relocating the elements in the periodic table.

Ástríðr: “These can share a charge in our reference set without sharing an address.”

She made a separate tray. Its label described its purpose rather than pretending to be another periodic table.

The class built magnesium chloride at a low bench. One Mg²⁺, two Cl⁻, MgCl₂. The name printer tried magnesium dichloride because it had retained a stray molecular-prefix wire. Jackson stopped the print, not because the formula was unbalanced but because the chosen naming route was wrong.

Jackson: “If we leave this wire connected, a correct charge calculation can still produce the wrong style of name.”

Ms Luminara: “Magnesium dichloride can describe the composition in a broader naming system. It would not mean magnesium chloride had become molecular. Here we requested the ordinary ionic name.”

Jeff held his reference card over the wire while Henry followed it to the panel. They disconnected the prefix output for this introductory ionic route. The name became magnesium chloride. No Roman numeral was required by their ordinary convention, and no di- was added for the two chlorides.

Ástríðr looked at the uncluttered name.

Ástríðr: “It says less than the formula does out loud, but it still carries enough information if I know the ion identities.”

Natalie: “That's the part I was missing. I thought the name ought to recite everything I could see.”

They tried aluminum oxide next, taking Al³⁺ and O²⁻ from the supplied set. Two aluminums and three oxides made a neutral +6 and −6, so the formula was Al₂O₃. The ordinary name stayed aluminum oxide. The formula had two and three; the name had neither a molecular count prefix nor a required Roman numeral.

For a moment Natalie found the absence irritating. She wanted the English to look more like the mathematics. Then she translated it back: aluminum's common +3, oxide's −2, two to three. The bridge worked. It simply required knowing the agreed meanings of the names.

They sat on a bench beneath the glass roof while Henry finished tracing the wire. Jeff's cushion fitted exactly between two wooden slats. Kàra watched the light move across the element cards instead of hunting for another problem.

Natalie: “I can't infer the whole language just by staring harder at the subscripts.”

Ms Luminara: “No. Some conventions have to be learned. We can give them structure and reasons without pretending there is no vocabulary.”

Natalie wrote a short promise to herself: learn name and ion together. She did not make it a demand to memorize the whole conservatory that afternoon.

A bell sounded at the far door. Their repaired naming wire had let the next waiting record advance to the category gate. It contained no metal symbol, and the gate rejected its supplied ionic identity.

Jackson took the instruction strip from Kàra. He found the default they had seen beneath FIND A NUMBER AND SAY IT: IF METAL, IONIC; OTHERWISE, MOLECULAR. Removing the prefix wire had brought the next card this far. The old category guess now prevented it going farther.

Ástríðr rose from the bench reluctantly. The sunlight had reached her shoes.

Ástríðr: “That was the rule I hoped we could keep.”

## 7 The passengers nobody counted as ions

The rejected card read (NH₄)₂SO₄.

It waited beneath a sign directing all nonmetal combinations toward the molecular tunnel. The tunnel lock would not accept it. Each time the card approached, two small charged-group icons flashed, and the station sent it back to the same queue.

Ástríðr: “We have invented a very tidy way to go nowhere.”

She read the card's identity line before trying the gate again. It named ammonium sulfate, a supplied ionic compound. Attached reference tabs identified ammonium, NH₄⁺, and sulfate, SO₄²⁻.

Jeff peered through the transparent display window. Nitrogen and hydrogen markers were joined within each ammonium model. Sulfur and oxygen markers were joined within each sulfate model. The models were simplified; their purpose was to preserve which atoms belonged to each charged group, not to teach all their geometry.

Jeff: “The atoms inside these groups are covalently bonded. But the groups themselves have net charges.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. A polyatomic ion is a bonded group with an overall charge. Internal covalent bonds don't prevent the group from being an ion or from participating in an ionic compound.”

Henry read NH₄⁺ aloud as ammonium ion, then pointed at the raised plus sign.

Henry: “The charge belongs to the written group. It is not saying only the last hydrogen carries the whole positive charge as a little label attached to it.”

Natalie had been silently reading N, H, four, plus. She tried reading ammonium instead. The formula stopped feeling like four independent instructions and became a recognizable named unit, with internal composition and overall charge that she still needed to preserve.

Natalie: “A word can help me keep the group together. Provided I remember what the word stands for.”

Ms Luminara gave them transparent handles that clipped around whole ion cards. The handles were fictional aids. Their very obviousness made it difficult to drag away an oxygen or multiply only a hydrogen while pretending to move another copy of an ion.

Jackson clipped one handle around NH₄⁺ and another around SO₄²⁻. He moved one of each onto a balance ledger. The arithmetic showed +1 − 2 = −1.

Jackson: “One of each doesn't make the neutral compound.”

Ástríðr added a second whole ammonium card. Both ammonium ions together supplied +2. The sulfate supplied −2. The total became zero, and the formula field displayed (NH₄)₂SO₄.

Henry tapped the outside 2.

Henry: “This repeats the entire ammonium group. The 4 inside still belongs to each individual ammonium ion.”

Jeff counted two nitrogen markers and eight hydrogen markers on the ammonium side, then one sulfur and four oxygen markers on the sulfate side. He wrote the counts in a separate inventory field. He did not alter the conventional grouped formula to make it resemble his list.

The name printer proposed diammonium sulfate. Natalie looked at the prefix tray from the conservatory and shook her head.

Natalie: “I nearly read that out. But this is still the ionic route, isn't it? We didn't put di- in magnesium chloride.”

She covered the proposed prefix with her thumb and checked the supplied name again: ammonium sulfate. The two ammonium ions were needed in the formula ratio; their requested conventional ionic name used the ion names without reciting that count. As with magnesium dichloride, a broader compositional naming system could state counts explicitly without changing the compound's identity.

The printer accepted the correction, but the classification gate remained closed. Its detector saw no metal and refused to reconsider.

Kàra crouched beside the detector. She could appreciate the appeal of its rule. A common metal cation and a nonmetal anion often did provide a useful first clue in introductory examples. But the rule had promoted a clue into an absolute condition and then ignored direct evidence.

Ástríðr took a blank label and wrote EXCEPT AMMONIUM SALTS. She held it under the old sign. It fitted, and this card would pass.

Ástríðr: “Would that be enough for the gate? We know why this one is different.”

Jackson: “It would repair this rejection. But the record already tells the gate what the compound is. The gate is refusing to read it.”

Ástríðr kept the label in place while Kàra moved the identity tab into the detector's view. Nothing changed. The gate read the element symbols and ignored the tab, exactly as it had ignored the original context line.

Kàra: “Can we make the verified identity arrive before the shortcut?”

Ástríðr lowered her exception label. She had not been wrong to recognize a familiar family. She had been trying to preserve the gate's authority over better information.

Ástríðr: “Use the tab first. Keep my note beside the rule as the case that showed us why.”

They replaced the gate's question with WHAT KIND OF COMPOUND IS THIS KNOWN TO BE? The reference tabs now entered with the formula instead of being left behind at the queue. The ammonium sulfate card passed into the ionic route.

They watched the charged-group icons disappear down the rail. Ástríðr smoothed her unused exception label onto the instruction strip, beneath the crossed-out absolute rule. A small corner refused to stick. She rubbed it down twice before leaving it curled.

Henry asked what the gate should do when no adequate identity tab arrived. This time Ástríðr opened the empty reference drawer before touching the route selector.

Ástríðr: “Leave a place to find out.”

The platform's amber waiting lamp came on. It was quieter than the rejected card's repeated return chime, and the empty slot did not pretend to contain an answer.

Jeff looked down at the ion handles still clipped around the cards.

Jeff: “Can we take these? I think the next machine will try to pull something apart.”

Ms Luminara checked where the admitted record had gone.

Ms Luminara: “The whole-ion line runs through that elevator. We should take the handles.”

## 8 The elevator that multiplied only the last passenger

The whole-ion line brought them to the next held dispatch request, calcium nitrate, beside the elevator. Its input cards supplied calcium, Ca²⁺, and nitrate, NO₃⁻. A diagram beside the doors showed one calcium entry and two nitrate groups. The printed output below it read CaNO₆.

Henry stared at the mismatch.

Henry: “The picture knows more than the printer.”

A small service hatch revealed the mechanism. The printer had seen that two nitrates were needed, then multiplied only the last subscript it encountered. It changed three oxygen atoms into six, kept one nitrogen, and declared the grouping job complete.

Ástríðr: “It has doubled the luggage and left the second traveler behind.”

Ms Luminara: “That image works for the bookkeeping. Remember that the nitrate ion isn't a person with detachable suitcases. Its composition and charge are part of the identity we were given.”

They set the incorrect formula beside the correct diagram. Jeff clipped a handle around NO₃⁻. He moved the whole card into the elevator, then added another identical card. Two nitrogen markers and six oxygen markers now occupied the anion side of the display.

Jeff: “A second nitrate brings its nitrogen too.”

Natalie wrote Ca(NO₃)₂. The parentheses enclosed NO₃; the outside 2 repeated all of it. She checked the total charge: one calcium at +2, two nitrates at −1 each, net zero. Then she checked the inventory: calcium one, nitrogen two, oxygen six.

Natalie: “The grouped formula and the atom inventory agree, but they aren't equally helpful for every task. The parentheses show the nitrate units I used.”

Jackson asked the elevator to take the wrong formula back through its own decoder. CaNO₆ could not reconstruct the two supplied nitrate ions without inventing a missing nitrogen. The output failed the return trip even though it contained the six oxygens the machine had proudly counted.

That mattered to Natalie. A wrong formula could contain a correct total somewhere. One successful count did not establish that the whole representation preserved the input.

Kàra moved a slider marked MAGNIFY. The elevator expanded until its floor looked like a broad platform, with the two nitrate models separated enough to walk between. The handles enlarged too. They were visibly overlays around the models, rather than supposed physical shells.

She followed one handle all the way around its group. There was no place where the outside multiplier belonged only to oxygen. The written parentheses were suddenly less decorative. They marked the scope of an instruction.

Kàra: “It's like brackets around a term in algebra. Multiplication acts on the whole indicated group. But the contents here are chemical identities, so I can't simplify them by changing the species.”

Ms Luminara: “Precisely. We can use the mathematical structure without forgetting what the symbols denote.”

The elevator rose. Each floor showed a different view of the same message: name, ions, neutral ratio, conventional formula, atom inventory. At the top they could see the roof of the station and the growing queue below.

Jackson noticed that the records office had already copied CaNO₆ into a teaching display. Their local repair had not changed the old output. He asked for the record camera and followed the output line down one level. Henry went with him, carrying the original input rather than just the corrected formula.

While they worked, Natalie remained at the window with Ms Luminara. Below, the station roofs resembled pages laid out on a desk. The rails between them looked simple from this height. She knew now how many little decisions each route concealed.

Natalie: “I want to be able to say nitrate and have NO₃⁻ come with it. At the moment I can follow the balance perfectly once someone gives me the card.”

Ms Luminara: “That tells us which part needs practice. Reasoning with a supplied ion and retrieving its identity are related skills, but they're not the same task.”

Natalie: “I used to call both of them knowing it.”

Ms Luminara: “A remarkably crowded word.”

Natalie opened her notebook to the formula-English bridge. She added a small side path from ion name to charged ion formula and back. The bridge had joints now. If one joint caught, she could inspect it rather than assuming the entire structure had collapsed.

Henry and Jackson returned with the old display's record. It had been marked SUPERSEDED and linked to the corrected output. Its route stamp matched the main printer. A second line on the copy list bore only REPRINT, the same word Jackson had seen through the central panel.

Jackson: “We found this copy. There's another printing branch to check when we get back.”

He folded the list with that line showing. Natalie slid it inside the cover of her notebook, away from the loose vocabulary cards.

Jeff tested the elevator once more, this time letting the name enter first. Calcium supplied the common +2 ion from the reference set. Nitrate supplied NO₃⁻. The elevator selected two whole nitrate groups and printed Ca(NO₃)₂. Its return decoder produced calcium nitrate, with the same ion identities and ratio.

Ástríðr: “Round trip, everybody present.”

The voice from the service hatch answered: “INCLUDING BOTH NITROGENS.”

Henry looked delighted, though he tried to conceal it by inspecting the print quality.

On the way down, Natalie asked to stop at the library floor. She wanted a better way to learn the names whose formulas she could not derive from charge balance alone. The request felt different from asking for rescue. She knew exactly which part of the route she wanted to strengthen, and the station had finally given that need an address.

## 9 The library of nearly matching names

The reference library was the quietest part of the station. Its shelves were built into shallow alcoves, with a table in each one and lamps that could be dimmed separately. Nothing moved unless somebody asked it to. After the click and hum of the rails, the stillness felt almost like arriving outdoors after rain.

Natalie chose an alcove with two empty chairs and a little slate. Ástríðr sat across from her. The others spread out without disappearing: Jeff at a rack of prefix cards, Henry beside an index of symbols, Jackson studying the route log, and Kàra at a window where she could see the suspended service bridge.

Natalie laid out chloride, sulfate, sulfite, and nitrate. She knew some of them when she saw them. Asked for the formula without the card, she could not promise that the right oxygen count or charge would arrive.

Natalie: “I keep thinking a memory trick should make all of this inevitable.”

Ástríðr: “I would also enjoy a tiny song that did all the work.”

Ms Luminara pulled up the spare chair. She did not bring a song. She brought two transparent cards: SO₄²⁻, sulfate, and SO₃²⁻, sulfite.

Ms Luminara: “Here is a real pattern within a known pair. Sulfate has one more oxygen than sulfite. Both of these ions have charge minus two.”

Natalie set the cards beside each other. The common sulfur symbol and charge gave the comparison a stable frame. One internal oxygen count changed, and part of the name changed with it.

Ástríðr said ate a little more oxygen, then paused before turning the phrase into a law.

Ástríðr: “That helps me remember which of this pair has more. It doesn't tell me that sulfate starts at four or that either charge is minus two.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly. A good reminder can carry less information than a full definition, provided we remember what is missing.”

They tested the reminder against a second supplied pair: nitrate, NO₃⁻, and nitrite, NO₂⁻. Again, the -ate member had one more oxygen than the corresponding -ite member. But nitrate had three oxygens rather than sulfate's four, and the charge was −1 rather than −2.

Natalie: “So -ate doesn't mean four oxygen atoms. It means something relational within these familiar naming families.”

Henry looked up from the symbol index.

Henry: “And it doesn't mean a specific charge.”

He did not stay to supervise. He returned to the index after putting a small reference tab within reach. Natalie appreciated that. She wanted a companion, not an audience waiting for her next error.

A low desk bell chimed beside her elbow. The library had received a returned card from the display gallery. It was labeled calcium sulfate, but the attached formula read CaSO₃. Whoever had written the label had preserved a plausible name and a plausible formula without checking that they belonged together.

Ástríðr turned the card over. Its incoming record supplied Ca²⁺ and SO₃²⁻. The formula balanced one of each. The mistake was not in charge arithmetic, nor in parentheses, nor in the classification route.

Ástríðr: “The ion name changed.”

Natalie: “Sulfite. Calcium sulfite.”

She felt the click of recognition and resisted saying that the suffix alone had solved it. The charge card and oxygen count established the identity she was naming. Her little reminder helped her retrieve the correct member of a pair she had already learned.

They corrected the label and left the formula untouched. Jackson added the record to their repair list. This was a different failure from Fe₂O₃: changing a Roman numeral there repaired an oxidation-state claim; changing the suffix here repaired which polyatomic ion the name identified.

Kàra came back from the window and listened to the explanation. She had expected the library to be a delay before the real adventure resumed. Instead, one of the station's wrong routes had ended at an ordinary-looking word.

Kàra: “The mechanism can fail in vocabulary too.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. Words can be compact handles on real distinctions. That doesn't make memorizing every handle the same thing as understanding every distinction.”

Natalie made four small cards with the formula on one side and name on the other. She included the whole-ion charge, not just the element letters and subscripts. She could turn them in either direction, but she did not have to do so now. The reference had already helped her make a real correction.

Ástríðr drew an oxygen marker on a spare scrap and offered it to the sulfate card. Then she took it away again and placed it beside the sulfite card as a comparison, not a pretend reaction.

Ástríðr: “The picture says difference of one. It doesn't say removing oxygen from an actual sample is a harmless way to manufacture the other substance.”

Ms Luminara: “A commendably nonexplosive library policy.”

Natalie laughed quietly. Outside, the station's announcements had resumed in a subdued voice, reporting which routes remained paused. Inside, the little set of cards was no longer one undifferentiated list. It had a pair, another pair, and reasons not to confuse the pair relationships with a universal decoder.

She slipped the cards into her notebook. One caught on the folded copy list and bent; she eased it loose instead of forcing the cover shut. The notebook was no longer going to lie flat. At the window, Kàra beckoned Jeff over to help unroll the machine's instruction strip across an empty table. A small printed reference at its edge matched the yellow brochures stacked beneath the index.

## 10 A useful pattern meets its boundary

Jeff brought one of the brochures to the alcove. Across its cover, elaborate lettering promised NAME EVERY ION IN TEN SECONDS. Underneath, in much smaller print, somebody had added in favorable cases. Its page numbers appeared beside several fallback rules on the machine's strip.

Jeff: “We've found what the index was reading.”

Henry unfolded the brochure to a row of rhymes, arrows, and unfinished rules. One proposed that every anion ending in -ide consisted of one atom. He began to write monatomic beside it, then stopped. The calcium hydroxide request protruding from the return slot had an OH⁻ reference tab.

Henry laid his unfinished label across the rule.

Henry: “I nearly made that more official.”

Jeff pulled the request free and put its ion card on the table. Natalie counted one oxygen and one hydrogen without needing to recall the formula.

Natalie: “Two atoms. Hydroxide still ends in -ide.”

Ástríðr found cyanide, CN⁻, in the reference set and added it. Both were polyatomic ions with established -ide names. The suffix was useful vocabulary, but it did not prove the number of atoms in every ion carrying it.

Ms Luminara: “For familiar monatomic anions, the element stem plus -ide is a common naming pattern: chlorine becomes chloride, oxygen becomes oxide, and so on. Established polyatomic names still need their own identities.”

Jeff folded the brochure so the promise no longer covered the examples. He did not throw it away. Some of its small reminders might be useful if their scope were repaired. The problem was the guarantee.

At the next table, Kàra had lined up carbonate, CO₃²⁻, nitrate, NO₃⁻, and phosphate, PO₄³⁻. She covered their charges, leaving the atom counts visible. Two ended in -ate and contained three oxygens, but their charges differed. The fourth oxygen in phosphate did not produce sulfate's −2 charge either.

Kàra: “If all I knew was the ending and the oxygen count, these examples wouldn't give me one charge rule.”

Natalie tried to derive more than the information supported, then recognized what she was doing. She wanted the reference table to be temporary, something she could throw away once she found the concealed formula. But a vocabulary table could be a legitimate source rather than a confession that she had failed to reason.

Natalie: “I can reason from an ion's composition and charge once those are established. This little list doesn't let me invent every unfamiliar ion.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly. More advanced chemistry gives additional tools, and even there one keeps track of what is measured, defined, modeled, or looked up. Today the honest reference drawer is part of the method.”

Ástríðr asked whether learning the pairs was still worthwhile after discovering so many limits. Natalie answered before Ms Luminara did.

Natalie: “Yes. Sulfate and sulfite stopped being two unrelated sounds. The pattern helped. It just didn't replace every fact on the cards.”

She realized she sounded less defeated than she had expected. A limited tool could still save work. It did not need to become an entire language to deserve a place in her notebook.

They returned to the calcium hydroxide request that had stopped Henry copying the brochure. Its charge references were supplied: Ca²⁺ and OH⁻. Henry placed one calcium and two whole hydroxide cards on the slate. Ástríðr wrote Ca(OH)₂. Jeff checked that the two repeated both O and H, and Natalie supplied calcium hydroxide.

They did not need a new principle. They needed to recognize hydroxide as a whole ion, despite the ending, and preserve it while using the principle they already had.

Jackson watched the corrected record travel into the dispatch tube. He then opened the tube's side door and checked whether the name and formula stayed attached. He had learned not to assume that a good local answer would survive the rest of the station.

Jackson: “This one kept its evidence.”

The ion reference had arrived in the same clip as the formula. Jackson tugged the clip gently, making certain it held both sheets, then let the tube carry them onward.

Natalie asked for five quiet minutes. She wanted to try one card without commentary, and she wanted the freedom to stop after that one. Ms Luminara moved the desk bell to the far side of the table so another returned card would not interrupt her.

Natalie turned nitrate over, said NO₃⁻, and checked it. Then she turned carbonate over and hesitated. Three oxygens came easily; the charge did not. She opened the reference rather than squeezing her eyes shut and trying to make certainty appear. CO₃²⁻. She wrote the uncertainty beside the charge field and left the atom count unmarked.

When Ástríðr returned with water, Natalie showed her the two separate records.

Natalie: “One part was available. One part needed support. If I write only wrong, I lose that.”

Ástríðr set down the water and did not ask for a second attempt. They sat for a moment without turning any cards.

Across the room, Kàra compared the brochure's next page with the service bridge sign: HYDROGEN MUST ALWAYS BE POSITIVE. The pending list named sodium hydride. The same index that had overlooked hydroxide was supplying the hydrogen assumption at that door.

Kàra: “We can follow this one to the bridge.”

Ms Luminara rose, gathering the reference cards in a portable tray.

Ms Luminara: “Then we shall take the library with us. It has been invited.”

## 11 Hydrogen at the wrong door

The service bridge crossed a deep, brightly lit shaft. Far below were rows of schematic ion cabinets, each sealed behind glass. They were teaching representations, with no reactive samples for anyone to handle. The bridge's padded rails and stillness option were visible before the class stepped onto it.

Jeff chose to walk across. He had brought the pause lever clipped to a strap, but he did not hold it like a warning against moving. He wanted to inspect a sign halfway along the bridge, where two pathways had been assigned the same hydrogen symbol.

One pathway assumed positive hydrogen. The other was meant for the supplied sodium hydride card, NaH. The station had tried to combine Na⁺ with H⁺, found a positive total, and accused the formula of being defective.

Jeff: “It decided the charge before reading the identity.”

Ms Luminara handed him the card attached to the request: hydride, H⁻.

Ms Luminara: “In sodium hydride, this introductory ionic description uses sodium ions and hydride ions. The hydrogen ion in that description is negative.”

Natalie looked at the formula NaH. With the supplied ion identities, the charge balance was uncomplicated: +1 and −1, one of each, net zero. The name was sodium hydride. The difficulty lay in allowing the correct identity into the calculation.

Natalie: “The formula didn't fail. The assumption did.”

Ástríðr turned the reference card over. It explained that a neutral hydrogen atom has one proton and one electron, while H⁻ has one proton and two electrons. The net charge ledger was therefore +1 − 2 = −1 in elementary-charge units.

Ástríðr: “The element identity stays hydrogen. The electron count changes the ion's net charge.”

Henry: “And the minus sign is raised. H₂ would say something different.”

He placed H₂ and H⁻ side by side. The neutral diatomic molecule and the monatomic negative ion were distinct species. Similar marks could not be interchanged merely because both involved the number two somewhere in an explanation.

Kàra leaned against the rail and looked down at the cabinets. She had wanted chemical naming to feel like a conservation problem. Here it did, but only after someone supplied the right species. Conservation could test an account without choosing all its premises.

Kàra: “The ledger can't rescue me from entering the wrong thing honestly.”

Ms Luminara: “It may expose a contradiction. Then we have to inspect the input. Arithmetic is wonderfully loyal to the numbers we give it, including our mistaken ones.”

They placed the station's H⁺ assumption beside the hydride reference instead of deleting it. Jackson matched the gate's setting to the marked brochure page. The index had taken hydrogen's frequent positive oxidation state in many familiar compounds and turned that pattern into always. Restoring the hydride identity required the same priority they had given the ammonium card: read the supplied species before consulting a fallback.

Jackson: “If we patch only NaH, it could make the same error with a second known metal hydride.”

The reference tray included Li⁺ and H⁻ for lithium hydride. The class used this as a supplied comparison rather than predicting the behavior of every metal-hydrogen combination. LiH balanced one +1 lithium ion with one −1 hydride ion in the introductory model. Lithium hydride took the ordinary ionic name without a molecular prefix.

Henry rewrote the route: USE THE SUPPLIED OR VERIFIED HYDROGEN SPECIES; DO NOT ASSIGN ITS CHARGE FROM THE LETTER H ALONE.

The wording was longer than the old rule. It also kept the station from turning a common case into a universal claim.

Jeff asked whether the electron-count explanation described how somebody should make the material. Ms Luminara shook her head.

Ms Luminara: “No. Bookkeeping identities and writing a balanced formula do not give a safe preparation method. Nothing on this bridge is an instruction to mix substances or produce hydrides.”

Jeff seemed satisfied. He did not need a warning about imaginary catastrophe. He wanted the boundary between understanding a formula and performing chemistry made explicit.

Natalie tried the translation in both directions. Sodium hydride → Na⁺ and H⁻ → one to one → NaH. Supplied ionic NaH → sodium and hydride → sodium hydride. The middle of the bridge had become the most useful part again.

A movement at the far end caught Kàra's eye. The previously locked gate began to open, then stopped beside another formula: Li₂O₂. A maintenance arm extended toward its subscripts with an eraser. The attached label identified peroxide, but the arm had already selected a smaller formula.

Kàra did not rush. She used the nearest pause control, and the arm stopped. The bridge remained steady beneath them.

Kàra: “Before it changes anything, I want to know what its oxygen unit is.”

Ms Luminara smiled and shifted the portable library tray to her other arm.

Ms Luminara: “There is our next question.”

Natalie glanced back at the hydrogen gate. Its green lamp now meant something more specific than success. The corrected route had preserved the identity provided by the reference. She could not infer every hydrogen compound from this one, but she could recognize the habit that had made this one seem impossible.

The class crossed the rest of the bridge slowly. Jeff paused halfway to look down through the transparent floor. Then he continued of his own accord, carrying the hydride card where he could see both its name and its charge.

## 12 What must stay together when a ratio becomes smaller

The eraser waited above Li₂O₂. Its planned output, LiO, glowed faintly on a preview screen. The machine's justification was mathematically impeccable in the narrowest possible sense: two and two shared a factor of two.

Henry read the identity tabs before looking at the preview. The compound was supplied as lithium peroxide. Its ions for this model were Li⁺ and peroxide, O₂²⁻. A note identified this case as an additional illustration of preserving a polyatomic ion, rather than a rule that every oxygen-containing compound followed.

Henry: “The oxygen unit is the peroxide ion, O₂²⁻. It isn't two separate oxide ions.”

Ástríðr placed three cards on the rail: O²⁻, oxide; O₂²⁻, peroxide; O₂, the neutral oxygen molecule. The labels prevented their similar typography from collapsing into one remembered shape.

Ástríðr: “One oxygen atom with minus two. Two oxygen atoms together with minus two overall. Two oxygen atoms in the specified neutral molecule. Three different messages.”

Ms Luminara: “Good. The overall peroxide charge belongs to the O₂ group. We are not treating it as two copies of the oxide ion.”

Natalie clipped a handle around the peroxide card. She had been prepared to defend parentheses, but the formula Li₂O₂ had none. Only one peroxide ion was needed in the composition ratio, so no outside multiplier required parentheses in the conventional formula.

Natalie: “Absence of parentheses doesn't mean absence of a whole polyatomic ion. Here the identity is supplied.”

Jeff moved two Li⁺ cards opposite one O₂²⁻ card. The charges totaled +2 and −2. The ratio of whole ions was already two lithium ions to one peroxide ion. There was no common factor greater than one in those ion counts.

Jeff: “The machine is reducing atom subscripts as if they were all independent ion counts.”

Kàra looked at the original two-to-two arithmetic and understood why it was so seductive. If the previous lessons had simply said always reduce ionic formulas, this would have looked like obedience. The missing words were ratio of whole ions.

Kàra: “The thing we preserve has to be named before we simplify.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. Keeping the peroxide unit intact is part of writing the conventional formula for this supplied compound.”

To test the distinction, they opened a second tray with Mg²⁺ and O²⁻. A deliberately unreduced bookkeeping arrangement used two magnesium ions and two oxide ions, written Mg₂O₂. Here each oxygen entry was a separate monatomic oxide ion. Reducing the whole-ion counts from two-to-two to one-to-one gave the conventional formula MgO.

Natalie set the two examples side by side. In the magnesium oxide exercise, the reduction preserved the supplied ions and their ratio. In lithium peroxide, dividing all visible atom subscripts would destroy the supplied peroxide unit's composition.

Natalie: “The visible pattern two and two isn't enough to tell me whether the change preserves identity.”

Henry: “And a simpler atom ratio could still be useful for a different question. It just isn't the ion-preserving formula requested here.”

They allowed the preview screen to show LiO long enough to label it as an empirical atom-ratio description, not the conventional formula that preserved the lithium peroxide ion composition. Then they canceled the rewrite. The eraser withdrew without touching Li₂O₂.

Jackson inspected the machine's shared settings. The same reduction rule also reached the molecular tunnel. He left that route paused and marked it for a later test. They had not yet toured the molecular models, but now he knew exactly which setting might damage them.

The bridge ended at a sheltered platform with an ordinary wooden bench. Natalie sat with the three oxygen cards and tried to put them in the order they had met them. Her hand stopped above peroxide. There had been so many small marks to protect that she wanted, briefly and unreasonably, a formula with no numbers in it at all.

Kàra sat beside her without asking a question. Through the wall they could hear the elevator descending, stopping, then descending again. Its measured rhythm made the stationary bench feel particularly welcome.

Natalie: “Could you hold one? I keep covering the charge when I compare them.”

Kàra held O²⁻ while Natalie held O₂²⁻. Side by side, both charges stayed visible. They did not attempt another name. After a while Natalie moved her finger under the peroxide subscript, leaving the raised charge unobscured. She put the card back in its handle.

Jeff brought the corrected instruction strip to the bench. Where the old line had said REDUCE SUBSCRIPTS, Henry had written REDUCE THE COUNTS OF WHOLE IONS WHEN APPROPRIATE. The more careful words filled the available space. Natalie asked for the strip to remain beside the two examples; she was not ready to remember them without it.

Ástríðr retrieved a hydrogen peroxide card, H₂O₂, from the library tray. It was explicitly identified as a molecular compound with the established common name hydrogen peroxide. Ms Luminara asked them to keep it for the molecular tunnel. They would not treat its molecular formula as a collection of free ions just because the word peroxide had appeared here.

Ástríðr: “Same word appearing again. Read the whole identity again.”

Ms Luminara: “Precisely. Our luggage is getting heavier, but at least it is labeled.”

The station's lunch bell rang from somewhere beyond the glass. It played two notes, hesitated, and wisely announced no chemical interpretation of them. Jeff picked up his cushion. ## 15 The eraser and the molecule

They repeated the same departure rather than selecting an easier new example. Cu₃(PO₄)₂ returned as copper(II) phosphate. The old incorrect output remained in the repair record with a note that its metal subscript had been mistaken for an oxidation state.

Natalie let her shoulders settle. She crossed REPRINT off the copy list herself, then handed the pencil to Jackson. He checked the restored label before accepting the mark. For once she did not mind waiting for someone else's check.

The third record was ammonium carbonate, with NH₄⁺ and CO₃²⁻ supplied. Jeff asked to run this one. Two ammonium ions balanced one carbonate. He wrote (NH₄)₂CO₃, preserving the whole ions, then counted two nitrogens, eight hydrogens, one carbon, and three oxygens in the formula-unit composition.

Jeff: “No metal, still an ionic compound. The internal covalent bonds don't change the category of the overall salt.”

The classifier accepted the verified identity. Its former no-metal rule did not get a second vote.

Next came a supplied molecular PCl₃ card. Natalie chose the molecular route, read one phosphorus and three chlorine atoms per molecule, and wrote phosphorus trichloride. No Roman numeral was inferred by their introductory naming task. No ionic charge ledger was required to generate that molecular name.

The next record asked for the molecular formula of dinitrogen tetroxide. Ástríðr used the prefix card without hesitation: two nitrogens, four oxygens, N₂O₄. The simplifier stayed inactive because the task asked for the molecular formula. A separate optional empirical-formula field could show NO₂ if requested, with the original preserved.

At each departure, Jackson checked that the corrected record arrived with its context. A formula without its role was no longer considered an adequate delivery. Jeff made sure each destination display remained compatible with the compound identity rather than inventing structures from name fragments.

Ms Luminara watched from the bench. She answered when asked and supplied a reference when the drawer lacked one. She did not turn their work into a test of who could go longest without her.

Kàra looked at the board after the fifth successful return. The map had grown less frightening not because its branches disappeared but because the switches were labeled. She could see where a pause would be useful and where a decision had already been justified.

Kàra: “We should test the waiting envelope too.”

Natalie knew which one she meant: iron chloride, left incomplete on the clarification shelf. A new note had arrived with it. The station had finally supplied the missing identity, and the last departure would depend on whether they read that note instead of reaching for whichever old example felt most familiar.

<a id="ch04-scene-19"></a>
## 19 A barrier rises for the right reason

The clarification envelope had returned with a formula: FeCl₃. Its note identified the supplied simple ionic compound and requested the corresponding conventional name. The missing information was now present. Nobody had to guess which iron chloride the original sender meant.

Natalie set the first envelope beside its new attachment.

Natalie: “Three chlorides at minus one each. One iron at plus three. Iron(III) chloride.”

Henry checked that the Roman numeral referred to iron and that the formula still showed one iron for every three chloride ions. Ástríðr replaced MORE IDENTITY INFORMATION NEEDED with CLARIFIED BY SUPPLIED FORMULA. The earlier uncertainty remained in the record; it had been resolved by additional evidence, not by becoming more confident about a guess.

The desk printed a complete ticket. Its front carried the formula and name. Its back recorded the relevant ion reference and charge reasoning. Jackson sent it along the rail and watched the destination lock open.

For the first time all the repaired route lamps were ready at once.

Kàra did not lift the main barrier immediately. She asked everyone to check the scope board above it. The board listed common introductory ionic naming with verified ion identities, supplied binary molecular naming, whole-ion preservation, and separate requested empirical-formula calculations. It did not claim to determine every unknown substance from a formula, describe every bond from a name, or cover every naming system.

Jeff read the list from the far end of the platform, where someone would actually encounter it. A small qualifier was hidden behind a beam. He moved the board so the limits were as readable as the title.

Jeff: “If the large print promises everything and the small print quietly takes it back, we haven't fixed the old sign.”

Ms Luminara: “Then let us be extravagantly legible.”

She enlarged the text until one line threatened to wrap around the clock. Henry persuaded her to use two lines. The clock survived.

Before the release, Jackson checked the earlier copies one final time. The corrected lattice gallery, ion cabinets, naming wall, and molecular chamber all linked to the current records. Unvisited routes remained marked unchecked. He could account for the bounded repair without claiming he had searched every possible corner of chemistry.

Ástríðr restocked the help drawer. She put the reference ions before the practice cards and the worked explanations beside them, where a visitor could reach them immediately. The prefix list went into a separate compartment labeled MOLECULAR COUNT PREFIXES, with a reminder that other established naming conventions existed.

Henry relabeled the fields: formula role, supplied identity, ion or atom counts, charge information, requested output, and reasoning. It looked like a desk meant for people to use rather than a device meant to impress them.

Jeff added a second cushion. He claimed that no station should require sharing one object of such practical importance. Ms Luminara found another in the bus with suspicious speed.

Natalie stood before the explanation wall she had revised after lunch. The blank arrow from her first notebook page appeared there only as an old draft, with its missing middle explained. The current version showed how the two naming routes differed and where a reference belonged. Someone could follow a short worked path without being forced through every scene the class had visited.

She had not needed to turn the day into a textbook on the wall. The day had given her enough experience to choose what the wall needed.

Kàra lowered her hand onto the barrier control.

Kàra: “Ready?”

They answered in their own time. There was no triumphant countdown. The barrier rose smoothly. The inspected formula cards returned to platform size, their messages unchanged, and began to move.

FeCl₂ entered the ionic route as iron(II) chloride. Beside it, the CO₂ card with the bent sleeve traveled to the molecular tunnel as carbon dioxide. Their twos no longer required them to share a destination. Fe₂O₃ arrived at its display with iron(III) oxide attached. The ammonium salt passed a gate that now read its verified ionic identity. N₂O₄ retained every atom count specified by its molecular formula.

The cards looked almost disappointingly ordinary in motion. No fireworks burst above the station. Their correct routes consisted of small decisions made visible and repeated reliably.

Natalie found that satisfying. At the start, she had wanted the name to appear as if by recognition alone. Now she could enjoy recognition when it came and reconstruct the route when it did not.

The station's enormous TWO sign flickered. A repair menu appeared, offering larger font, louder announcement, and improved confidence. Henry closed all three options. Kàra entered a destination instead.

CLASSROOM.

The letters fitted badly at first. Ms Luminara adjusted the panel, making room rather than abbreviating away the meaning. The bus's own destination sign changed to match.

Ástríðr looked back at the brass desk. Its former UNIVERSAL nameplate lay in the recycling tray. She turned it over and wrote OPEN THE REFERENCE DRAWER on its plain side, then hung it above the drawer where the old title could finally do useful work.

Jackson kept the corrected route record. Henry kept a notation card. Jeff kept the original cushion, and Kàra kept the map with its explicit pause points. Natalie gathered her small ion cards without testing herself on the way to the bus.

Ms Luminara waited at the door until everyone had boarded. She took one last look at the station, where the repaired routes were operating and the unreviewed ones were honestly labeled.

Ms Luminara: “A good day's work. Shall we leave while the machinery still has reasonable expectations of us?”

Jeff settled into his seat at the front window. He wanted to watch the departure. Beyond the glass, the naming station receded with every message still carrying the information that made it itself.

<a id="ch04-scene-20"></a>
## 20 The bridge on an ordinary page

On the way home, the bus passed the junction where it had first insisted on TWO. The road sign now named a destination and showed an arrow pointing toward it. Kàra watched until they had passed the turning, then folded the map and let it rest in her lap.

Kàra: “A destination with meaning. Very bold.”

Ms Luminara: “I thought we'd try it.”

The classroom looked exactly as they had left it. An uncapped marker lay beside the whiteboard. Someone's chair was turned sideways. The clock had advanced, though less than Natalie felt it ought to have after a day spent inside so many different kinds of number.

She put the notebook on the ordinary table and found the first page. FeCl₂ stood on the left. The blank on the right had waited patiently through the entire trip. Natalie wrote iron(II) chloride, then resisted the urge to tear the page out and replace it with a cleaner version.

The arrow in the middle was too small for everything she now understood. She drew a line down to a second page and added the bridge there: supplied ionic identity, chloride name and charge, count from the formula, neutral total, iron's +2 oxidation state in the simple model, conventional name.

Ástríðr pulled up a chair beside her.

Ástríðr: “It doesn't fit.”

Natalie: “No. Apparently I needed more paper.”

Ástríðr laid sulfate and sulfite beside the notebook. Natalie could tell which was which while the formulas faced upward. When Ástríðr turned one card over by accident, Natalie hesitated at the oxygen count again. The hesitation disappointed her for a moment.

Then she turned the card back herself.

Natalie: “That still needs practice. It doesn't mean I can't balance a salt once I have the ion.”

Ástríðr left the card visible. Neither of them turned the moment into an examination.

Across the room, Henry placed the notation card beside the whiteboard. It distinguished a subscript, a coefficient, a superscript charge, a Roman numeral in a Stock name, and a molecular count prefix. Each had an example and a short explanation. He did not claim the card covered every use of every symbol in chemistry.

Jeff put the cushion on a chair that had always been slightly uncomfortable. The improvement was immediate and required no theory. He sat down with the old +6 TOTAL card, which he had kept because it reminded him of a mistake he could now locate precisely.

Jeff: “I thought being careful meant waiting until somebody else was sure. Today I pressed pause because I knew which information was being lost.”

Ms Luminara: “You also asked for a check when you wanted one. Those fit together.”

Jackson laid out the route record. He was interested in the reverse printer that had retained the old rule. Fixing a principle in one place had not guaranteed that every copy used it. The final test had found a meaningful remaining defect rather than simply certifying their confidence.

Kàra looked over his shoulder.

Kàra: “I wanted the last run to be perfect.”

Jackson: “I wanted the last run to tell us whether it was.”

They smiled at each other. The two desires had almost looked identical until a bad output separated them.

Natalie closed the notebook halfway, then opened it again to write one final distinction. Some knowledge was retrieved: chloride, nitrate, sulfate. Some was inferred from stated identities: the +3 per iron in Fe₂O₃. Some was convention: where the Roman numeral went, when mono was omitted in their binary molecular names. Some depended on the question: molecular formula or simplest atom ratio. Mixing these tasks together had made every hesitation feel like the same failure.

She did not write that the language was easy now. It was more navigable. She could ask for the missing ion card, identify an incomplete name, or explain why a convenient numerical shortcut would change the message.

Ms Luminara collected none of the notebooks. She returned the portable reference tray to a shelf where it could be reached without permission. The stories on its cards were not finished for Natalie; they were simply no longer strangers with indistinguishable names.

Henry asked whether the station would remain repaired. Ms Luminara rested a hand on the closed reference tray.

Ms Luminara: “The routes we checked are carrying the records correctly. What would you want the desk to do with a name outside those routes?”

Henry looked at the reference tray before answering.

Henry: “Keep the request. Find which convention it belongs to before changing it.”

Natalie wrote that as a question on the inside cover: Which convention, and what information does it preserve? She left space beneath it. It was a question for another day, not another departure they had to make before going home.

Natalie looked once more at FeCl₂ and iron(II) chloride. The two expressions did not mirror each other mark for mark. They met through meanings she could now describe. The bridge was made of those meanings, with a few sturdy reference cards supporting the places memory had not yet made familiar.

Outside the classroom window, the bus gave a satisfied little chime. Its destination panel briefly displayed TWO, reconsidered, and changed to PARKED.

Jeff settled deeper into the cushion.

Jeff: “A complete sentence would have been nice.”

Ms Luminara: “We must leave the vehicle some ambition.”

Natalie capped the marker someone had left open. The room had become ordinary again, and the ordinary page in front of her contained a route she could take in either direction. She put the ion cards inside it for tomorrow, closed the notebook, and let the day be enough.

Nobody charged Jeff two of anything for the cushion.
