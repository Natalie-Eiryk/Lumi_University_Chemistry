# Transfer questions for the Compound Naming Station

Story ID: luminara.adventure.004

Original questions 1–4 retain their original IDs and wording. Questions 5–14 are new, optional companion practice. These questions are separate from the adventure. A teacher or learner may select any of them. References, spoken explanations, labeled sketches, calculators, and revision are welcome. If a recall-only check is wanted, agree on that mode beforehand. State reasoning as well as a final name or formula.

## Question 1 Copper at two platforms

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q01

CuCl and CuCl₂ are supplied as simple ionic compounds. Chloride has charge −1.

Name both compounds. Explain what the Roman numeral means in each name and why the subscript 2 in CuCl₂ does not mean two copper ions.

## Question 2 A salt without a metal

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q02

A known ionic compound consists of ammonium ions, NH₄⁺, and carbonate ions, CO₃²⁻.

Write its conventional neutral formula preserving whole ions, give its name, and explain why “no metal means molecular” would fail. Count N, H, C, and O separately in one formula unit's composition.

## Question 3 Choosing a naming language

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q03

MgCl₂ is supplied as a simple ionic compound; Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻ are its ions. PCl₃ and SF₆ are supplied as binary molecular compounds.

Name all three. Choose one numeral or prefix in your answers and explain exactly what it describes. Does magnesium chloride need the molecular prefix di before chloride in this naming convention?

## Question 4 A formula that must keep its passengers

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q04

A machine changes the molecular formula H₂O₂ into HO because both subscripts are divisible by two.

Explain what information the original molecular formula carries, what HO can describe in this context, and why the change is unacceptable when the task asks for the molecular formula. Then state whether N₂O₄ and NO₂ name the same molecular composition.

## Question 5 Four oxygen and chlorine tags

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q05

The reference supplies these identities: O₂ is a neutral oxygen molecule; O²⁻ is an oxide ion; O₂²⁻ is a peroxide ion; 2 Cl⁻ means two chloride ions.

For each entry, state the number of atoms in one written species, its charge if any, and what any leading coefficient counts. Explain why the lowered 2 in O₂ and the raised 2− in O²⁻ cannot trade places. For 2 Cl⁻, distinguish charge per ion from combined charge.

## Question 6 A Roman numeral from a ledger

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q06

Fe₂O₃ is supplied as a simple ionic compound, modeled with equal-charge iron ions and oxide ions O²⁻. A sign calls it iron(II) oxide because the iron subscript is 2.

Name the supplied compound correctly. Show total oxide charge, total iron charge, and charge per iron ion separately. Then translate the different name iron(II) oxide into its conventional neutral formula using Fe²⁺ and O²⁻. Explain what changed.

## Question 7 Two ways a name supplies charge information

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q07

In this reference set, magnesium has the common fixed ion Mg²⁺. Copper(II) denotes Cu²⁺. Chloride is Cl⁻.

Write the conventional formulas for magnesium chloride and copper(II) chloride. Explain why their similar charge arithmetic does not require both conventional names to display a Roman numeral. Evaluate the claims “magnesium chloride needs di- because there are two chlorides” and “magnesium(II) necessarily describes chemically impossible magnesium.”

## Question 8 Repair the elevator output

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q08

A request specifies calcium nitrate, using Ca²⁺ and NO₃⁻. A printer doubles only nitrate's oxygen count and produces CaNO₆.

Write the conventional neutral formula. Explain the role of its parentheses, count Ca, N, and O per formula unit's composition, and identify which atom the printer failed to duplicate. Explain why a correct oxygen total alone did not make its formula correct.

## Question 9 What the suffix can and cannot do

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q09

Use the supplied identities: sulfate SO₄²⁻, sulfite SO₃²⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, and nitrite NO₂⁻.

Describe the -ate/-ite relationship within each known pair. Evaluate “-ate always means four oxygen atoms” and “-ate determines an ion's charge.” Then use supplied Ca²⁺ to name CaSO₃ and CaSO₄. Separate the remembered name pattern from the charge-balance reasoning.

## Question 10 An ending is not an atom counter

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q10

Chloride is Cl⁻, hydroxide is OH⁻, and cyanide is CN⁻. An index classifies every name ending in -ide as a monatomic ion.

Repair the index's claim without discarding the familiar monatomic-anion naming pattern. Using Mg²⁺ and OH⁻, write and name the conventional neutral compound. Explain why changing OH to O₂H would not mean two hydroxide ions.

## Question 11 The negative hydrogen card

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q11

Sodium hydride is supplied as a simple ionic compound represented with Na⁺ and H⁻. A neutral hydrogen atom has one proton and one electron.

Write the compound's conventional formula and name. State the electron count of H⁻ and explain its sign without changing the proton count. Explain why H₂ and H⁻ are different species, and why “hydrogen must always be positive” is not an acceptable rule for this case.

## Question 12 Reduce the right things

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q12

Case A supplies lithium peroxide with Li⁺ and the whole peroxide ion O₂²⁻. Case B supplies magnesium oxide with Mg²⁺ and monatomic O²⁻; an unreduced bookkeeping arrangement uses two of each ion and is written Mg₂O₂.

Write the conventional ion-preserving formula for each case. Explain why dividing every atomic subscript in Li₂O₂ does not preserve the specified peroxide unit, while reducing the whole-ion counts in Case B gives MgO. State exactly what quantity is being reduced in the valid reduction. This is formula bookkeeping, not a preparation procedure.

## Question 13 Two molecules do not become one bigger molecule

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q13

CO₂ is supplied as the molecular formula of carbon dioxide. A display is instructed to represent 2 CO₂, meaning two molecules in this counting example.

Give the composition of each molecule and the total C and O counts across both. Explain the different roles of the leading coefficient and oxygen subscript. Would replacing the label with a single molecular formula C₂O₄ preserve the stated meaning? No identification of a substance with that hypothetical formula is required.

## Question 14 The honest edge of a naming rule

ID: luminara.adventure.004.q14

A reference supplies these facts: ammonium sulfate is ionic and contains NH₄⁺ and SO₄²⁻; OsO₄ is molecular osmium tetroxide, also known as osmium(VIII) oxide. The latter numeral is a formal oxidation-state label and does not assert the presence of free Os⁸⁺ ions. The task has not specified a preferred naming convention.

Evaluate the desk rule “if a formula contains a metal it must be ionic, and without a metal it must be molecular.” Explain what evidence you would use instead when a compound's identity is already supplied. Should a checker reject the recognized name osmium tetroxide solely because osmium is a metal? What additional information might a course-specific naming task legitimately request?

## Optional progressive hints

Use one, several, none, or the full [worked key](TEACHER_KEY.md). Hints are available before a first attempt. They are choices, not gates. Stop whenever the useful question has been answered.

### q01 Copper at two platforms

1. Use separate spaces for copper count and charge per copper ion.
2. Total the −1 chloride charges, then use overall neutrality.
3. Each formula has one copper in its ratio; the Roman numeral records its oxidation state.

### q02 A salt without a metal

1. Move NH₄⁺ and CO₃²⁻ as whole cards.
2. How many +1 ammonium ions cancel one −2 carbonate ion?
3. An outside subscript repeats every atom inside parentheses; do the N and H counts separately.

### q03 Choosing a naming language

1. The categories are already supplied; you need not infer them.
2. Ionic naming uses the cation and anion names. The supplied molecular cases use atom-count prefixes.
3. Tri is three and hexa is six. Magnesium's common +2 charge is implicit in this introductory naming convention.

### q04 A formula that must keep its passengers

1. Ask whether the requested output describes one molecule or a simplest ratio.
2. Count atoms before reducing anything.
3. HO preserves H:O = 1:1, but not the stated two H and two O per hydrogen peroxide molecule.

### q05 Four oxygen and chlorine tags

1. Locate each mark: below, above, or before the formula.
2. The peroxide charge applies to its whole O₂ group.
3. For 2 Cl⁻, count two separate ions and add two −1 charges without changing the per-ion charge.

### q06 A Roman numeral from a ledger

1. Begin with the supplied oxide charge, not the numeral proposed in the name.
2. Three oxides total −6; two equal-charge iron ions must total +6.
3. Divide that cation total between the two iron ions. In the reverse case, +2 and −2 balance one-to-one.

### q07 Two ways a name supplies charge information

1. Both cation cards in this question say +2.
2. Two chlorides balance either cation, but their common naming conventions provide the charge information differently.
3. Distinguish an unnecessary explicit oxidation-state label from an incorrect chemical charge, and ionic naming from molecular prefixes.

### q08 Repair the elevator output

1. Put one entire NO₃⁻ card in the tray, then another.
2. The second card brings a nitrogen as well as three oxygens.
3. Parentheses mark the complete group repeated by the outside 2.

### q09 What the suffix can and cannot do

1. Compare within each pair before comparing between pairs.
2. Sulfate and nitrate do not have the same oxygen count or charge.
3. SO₃²⁻ retains the name sulfite when it enters a calcium salt; balancing it does not rename the ion sulfate.

### q10 An ending is not an atom counter

1. Count element symbols and atoms in the supplied ion identities.
2. Hydroxide and cyanide are established polyatomic names ending in -ide.
3. Two OH⁻ ions have two O and two H altogether; show repetition of OH with parentheses.

### q11 The negative hydrogen card

1. Preserve one hydrogen proton when comparing H and H⁻.
2. Net charge in elementary-charge units is proton count minus electron count.
3. H₂ uses a subscript to indicate two hydrogen atoms in the supplied neutral molecule; H⁻ has a raised net-charge sign.

### q12 Reduce the right things

1. Write each supplied whole ion and its charge before examining visible atom subscripts.
2. Two Li⁺ balance one O₂²⁻; the whole-ion ratio is already 2:1.
3. The magnesium oxide arrangement has two monatomic cations and two monatomic anions; their common count factor can be removed without changing either ion's identity.

### q13 Two molecules do not become one bigger molecule

1. Draw two separate boxes, each containing one CO₂ molecule.
2. The coefficient changes the number of boxes. The subscript describes each box's contents.
3. A combined atom inventory does not itself establish one molecule containing that inventory.

### q14 The honest edge of a naming rule

1. Each supplied identity contradicts a different half of the absolute rule.
2. A metal/nonmetal heuristic should not override verified identity.
3. Separate a recognized chemical name from a particular task's requested convention, and formal oxidation state from free-ion charge.
