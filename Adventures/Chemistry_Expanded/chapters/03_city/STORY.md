# The City Without Couples

Story ID: luminara.adventure.003  
Chapter: 03 · Revision checkpoint: Pass 3 · Final-reviewed draft; not canonized

The bus, city, dialogue, instruments and events are newly authored fiction; the journey uses explicitly limited chemical models and depicts no real chemical experiment.

## 1. An Invitation With Too Much Ribbon

The invitation arrived inside a cube of clear light, which rested on Ms Luminara's desk without illuminating anything around it. Natalie put her notebook beside the cube and watched the shadows disagree.

“Is the light a clue?” Natalie asked.

“The light is the envelope,” Ms Luminara said. “The clue is the part the committee hoped we wouldn't read.”

Jeff had already found it. Beneath SALT CRYSTAL DANCE, in lettering small enough to make enthusiasm look like a legal arrangement, someone had written: All guests must arrive in permanent couples. Unpaired arrivals may not enter. Rearrangement is discouraged.

“There's another line,” Jeff said. “No compulsory dancing. Excellent. I object to the first requirement, though. We have seven people.”

“Six students and one extravagantly qualified inconvenience,” Ms Luminara agreed.

Ástríðr turned the cube around. “It says the dance is held in a model of ordinary solid sodium chloride. Are people the guests, or are the ions the guests?”

“That,” Henry said, “seems like something the invitation should know.”

The cube clicked. A narrow paper ribbon emerged from one edge, looped around Natalie's pencil and tried to pull it toward a second pencil. Natalie caught both before they reached the floor.

“An invitation that enforces its interpretation,” Jackson said. “Who else received one?”

A picture appeared in the cube: a hall receding in every direction, populated by repeating lighted spheres. At its entrance a brass usher was fastening ribbon around pairs of spheres. Each completed knot made a bell ring. Each ring advanced a counter marked MOLECULES ADMITTED. Beyond the entrance, an enormous seating chart flickered with gaps.

Natalie remembered drawings in which one sodium atom supplied an electron to one chlorine atom. She could see how a ribbon might grow out of that picture. The drawing had a beginning, a transfer and a tidy result. It was easy to remember. It was easy to mistake its neat ending for the whole explanation.

“I think I understand the usher's first idea,” she said. “I don't think I understand the hall.”

Ms Luminara handed her a fresh sheet of paper. “Good. We can keep both statements.”

Kàra was at the bus window, studying the hall as if a better angle might appear in the glass. “Why are the gaps moving when the usher adds a pair? Adding two things shouldn't make an unrelated part disappear.”

“Unless the things aren't unrelated,” Jackson said.

Their familiar classroom sounds continued around the impossible invitation: a chair leg scraping, a radiator clicking, Ástríðr searching for the pencil she had tucked behind her ear. Natalie appreciated the ordinary noises. A mystery did not have to begin by erasing everything she understood.

Ms Luminara wrote across a yellow card and clipped it above the bus door: DO NOT CONFUSE THE STORY OF ARRIVAL WITH THE REASON THINGS STAY.

Natalie read it twice. “That sounds useful in several subjects.”

“Today we will keep it in chemistry,” Ms Luminara said. “The ions have enough work without being asked to explain our relationships.”

Jeff folded the invitation with deliberation. “Before we go: are we entering an actual sample? Because sodium metal and chlorine gas don't become safe just because someone has booked a band.”

“No actual sample. No handling chemicals. We will enter a teaching model whose scenery makes some relationships visible and leaves others out. The model's errors can mislead its visitors, which is quite enough trouble for one afternoon.”

“Then I'd like the balcony,” Jeff said. “It looks like the place to see what the chart leaves out.”

Kàra glanced again at the receding lights. She wanted the balcony too, and already disliked the transparent floor she could see in the picture. She did not announce either fact. Instead, she asked whether the model could stop moving.

“Absolutely,” Ms Luminara said. “Motion is a display option, not an entrance examination.”

The bus opened its doors. The familiar seats had acquired embroidered plus and minus signs, except the driver's seat, which bore a small question mark wearing a hat. Henry checked that the labels weren't instructions to arrange themselves by charge.

“Only upholstery,” Ms Luminara assured him.

Once everyone was seated, Natalie wrote the first sentence in her notebook: The picture may help and still be wrong about what belongs together. She left room below it. In her former classroom she might have put the polished conclusion on the board first. Here she wanted to preserve the uncertainty long enough to see what changed it.

“Seatbelts, everyone!” Ms Luminara called.

The cube expanded. For a moment the bus seemed to stand inside an enormous ice crystal, except the walls were warm and there was no water dripping from them. Then the clear planes moved apart into a wide entrance road. Ahead stood an archway decorated with so much ribbon that it resembled a carefully wrapped mistake.

The brass usher bowed to the bus.

“Welcome,” it said. “Please identify your permanent partner.”

Ms Luminara lowered her window. “My present companion is a steering wheel. I reserve the right to leave it.”

The usher consulted its clipboard. Behind it another patch of the seating chart went dark.

Jackson leaned forward. “I think we'd better look at the instructions before it pairs the road with the pavement.”

The gate remained closed, but a small side entrance had a different label: INSPECTION VISITORS. No partner required.

Henry pointed it out. “Someone who built this place knew there might be questions.”

Ms Luminara smiled and turned the wheel.

## 2. The Hall Beyond Every Window

The inspection entrance led through a narrow gallery lined with enlarging images. The first showed grains of table salt on dark paper. Natalie could have held a thousand of them without seeing an ion. The next showed one grain larger than a house. The next replaced its smooth surfaces with a structural representation: labeled positions repeating through space.

The bus stopped between the last two pictures. Before stepping down, Ms Luminara unclipped the yellow warning from above its door and slipped it into her pocket. The ribbon around the inspection entrance brushed her shoulder; she left that one alone.

“We're changing what our representation lets us see,” Ms Luminara said. “We aren't claiming that a classroom microscope can simply zoom until these particular colored balls appear.”

“Or that the balls are the colors,” Ástríðr said.

She had noticed a switch offering decorative palettes. When she changed blue to green, the label Cl⁻ stayed put. A neighboring Na⁺ retained its name and plus sign even when both decorations became gray.

“Good,” Henry said. “The evidence doesn't vanish with the paint.”

Beyond the gallery, the hall opened around them. Rows ran left and right, ahead and behind. Above their heads, more positions repeated toward a ceiling too distant to locate. The bus had shrunk relative to the model, though the aisle between the seats remained comfortably ordinary.

Jeff turned in a full circle after stepping outside. “The invitation picture made this look like a dance floor. It's a dance volume.”

The floor beneath the visitors was an invented walkway, Ms Luminara reminded them. The empty spaces they could walk through were made convenient by the display. Real ions were not illuminated balloons separated by tourist paths. Nor was every feature of an electron distribution represented by a hard spherical edge.

Kàra watched a line of alternating labels disappear into the distance. Na⁺, Cl⁻, Na⁺, Cl⁻. She looked upward and found another alternating line. Then she crouched and looked through a low window in the walkway.

“It continues below us.”

“Why wouldn't it?” Henry asked, then caught the tone of his own question. “I mean, the first picture made me assume the floor was the bottom too.”

“It gave us a convenient edge,” Natalie said. “We treated it as the edge of the substance.”

The nearest sphere carried a label that could be enlarged. SODIUM ION. Na⁺. NET CHARGE +1, IN UNITS OF THE ELEMENTARY CHARGE. It did not say sodium atom with a positive mood. It did not say sodium molecule. The neighboring chloride label was equally deliberate: CHLORIDE ION. Cl⁻. NET CHARGE −1.

Ástríðr touched the information rail rather than the glowing sphere. “A plus one and a minus one make zero. That part of the invitation makes sense.”

“It does,” Ms Luminara said. “The trouble isn't that everything the usher knows is false.”

A familiar rattling approached. The brass usher came down a service track carrying two spools, one clipboard and the expression of a machine sure that more stationery would fix a conceptual problem.

“I have found a pair for demonstration,” it announced.

It drew a ribbon from one Na⁺ position to an adjacent Cl⁻, fastening a bow between them. Then it outlined the two spheres with a glowing oval. Across the oval appeared NaCl MOLECULE.

Jeff could still see other chloride positions equally near the selected sodium. “Why that one?”

“The invitation process has established them as a pair.”

“That's the process you're meant to explain,” Jackson said. “It can't also be the evidence.”

The usher offered its clipboard. Every line contained one Na⁺, one Cl⁻ and a tiny picture of a bow. The arithmetic at the bottom was impeccable. There were equal numbers of the two ions. Natalie felt the pull of the tidy page. She could imagine praising a learner for its balance and failing to ask what the bows asserted.

Ms Luminara placed a second clipboard beside it. This one showed the ordinary rock-salt arrangement around a selected ion. Its nearest opposite-charge neighbors lay along three perpendicular directions, on both sides of each direction.

Henry traced them without relying on a spinning picture. “Left, right, ahead, behind, above, below. Six.”

“Six nearest chloride neighbors around this interior sodium position,” Ms Luminara said. “The ordinary structure has that local pattern. We'll keep the conditions with the claim.”

The usher examined its oval. “Additional neighbors have not submitted partner applications.”

Ástríðr laughed, then covered the label with her hand. “If I can't see your bow, can I identify which neighbor is the permanent partner?”

The display showed no special distinction. She rotated the local viewpoint. The assigned partner moved from left to behind, while the other neighbors remained part of the same repeated environment.

“The bow supplies the difference,” she said. “The structure doesn't.”

That was their first useful separation. Equal numbers did not prove independent couples. An illustration could have the right total composition while inventing the wrong groupings within it.

Natalie wrote that down, then drew the bow faintly enough to erase. She did not yet cross out every two-ion picture. A formula could still represent one sodium for one chlorine in the composition. She needed a way to say that without making a tiny isolated object.

From somewhere above came a bell, followed by an announcement that the dance rehearsal had been delayed by incomplete seating assignments. The usher gathered its ribbon.

“Please do not remove approved pairs,” it said. “The entire chart depends on them.”

Jackson watched a second bell fail to sound. “Then we need to find out what else the chart controls.”

## 3. A Perfectly Balanced Wrong Picture

The chart office was a glass room hanging beside the entrance hall. Inside, every ion position had a number, and every number belonged to a two-number envelope. The envelopes were stacked in drawers labeled ARRIVED TOGETHER and THEREFORE STAY TOGETHER.

“That second drawer is doing a lot of work,” Natalie said.

The usher opened a demonstration chart. A sodium position and chloride position stood inside a yellow border. The machine's display said that anything outside the border could be ignored when describing their ionic interactions.

Kàra leaned close. “Ignored because it's far away, or ignored because it wasn't selected?”

“Because it is not the partner.”

“That's a very short force law,” Kàra said.

Ms Luminara supplied the relevant comparison before they attempted a repair. In the introductory ionic picture, electrostatic interactions were not assigned by a remembered transfer event. Oppositely charged ions attracted; like-charged ions repelled. An extended solid involved an arrangement and interactions throughout the structure, not a set of isolated one-to-one bonds chosen by an usher.

“We don't need a complete calculation of the crystal's energy to disprove the favorite-partner rule,” she said. “We do need to be honest that six nearest neighbors aren't the whole interaction story either.”

Jeff found a button marked REMOVE ENVELOPE FROM DRAWING. It did not remove real material; it hid the selected model positions and asked the program which displayed relationships had changed. He read the help label aloud, confirming that this was a comparison of diagrams, not an experiment with vacancies in an actual crystal.

“May I try the prediction first?” he asked.

The usher predicted that only the ribbon inside the envelope would disappear. Every other envelope would be unaffected. Jeff pressed the button. Two spheres faded. One bow vanished. The machine announced NO OTHER CHANGES.

Outside the glass room, they could plainly see the remaining nearby spheres where the missing positions had been. Henry asked for the local-neighborhood view. Several of those remaining positions had lost a displayed nearest neighbor.

The two views disagreed because the program had stored different relationships. No chemical substance had changed its laws to produce the disagreement. They had changed a picture, and the pair-only record failed to represent what that change meant for nearby positions.

Jackson pointed to the rehearsal controls. “The light operators use this chart. When it hides a pair for an entrance effect, the program claims nothing nearby needs updating. But the neighborhood display needs updating. That's why we saw gaps in the wrong places.”

“So this mistake has a practical consequence inside the museum,” Ástríðr said. “And outside the museum, the same picture could give somebody the wrong explanation.”

The usher's head lowered. “All totals remain balanced.”

“They do,” Natalie said gently. “You preserved something important. You didn't preserve everything the job requires.”

She drew two small diagrams. One had four positive signs and four negative signs gathered into four ovals. The other had the same eight signs arranged as a fragment of a repeating pattern. Both contained equal total positive and negative charge. The ovals were an extra structural claim, not an inevitable consequence of the arithmetic.

“Charge accounting tests charge,” she said. “It doesn't, by itself, decide which model of structure is correct.”

Henry wanted to delete the pair envelopes at once. Jackson put a hand on the drawer.

“What happens to every other room that reads these?” Jackson asked. “We might turn off the bad picture and lose the information about composition with it.”

For once, Henry was the one impatient to simplify. He looked at the equal totals and admitted that the envelopes had two jobs mixed together: keeping counts and declaring permanent partners. They needed to separate the jobs before removing either.

The usher produced a long strip of paper listing its dependencies. The species gallery used the ovals to decide what counted as one thing. The formula workshop used them to determine how many things to put in a shipment. The dance controls used them to decide what could change independently.

Kàra sat down on a crate. “That's not one bad caption. That's the same assumption in three rooms.”

“And it won't become three true assumptions if we print it beautifully,” Ms Luminara said.

The class agreed to a temporary hold. The dance announcement would say that the exhibition was under revision. No visitor would be told that independent NaCl molecules filled the ordinary solid. Meanwhile, the old chart would remain available with a visible warning, so they could trace what depended on it instead of erasing the evidence of the error.

Jeff asked the usher whether it could admit the class without assigning pairs. Its gears clicked for a long time.

“Inspection visitors,” it said at last. “Category already available.”

“Use that for us,” Jeff said. “You don't have to invent a chemical category for people with notebooks.”

The gate chimes became quiet. Through the glass, the hall still extended beyond every window. It had not become less astonishing because they had found an error. If anything, the absence of little permanent couples made the structure feel larger.

Natalie closed her notebook for a moment. “I used to think the hard part was making an explanation memorable.”

“What do you think now?” Ástríðr asked.

“That the memorable part needs checking especially carefully.”

They left the chart office with three tasks written on one card: see the neighborhood, preserve the composition, and find out what the ovals were allowed to mean.

## 4. The Balcony With an Ordinary Bench

The direct way to the balcony was a transparent lift. Its floor was not merely glass; it was obligingly absent from the image, so that riders appeared to float above a depth of labeled ions. Kàra stopped two steps from its threshold.

Jeff, who had volunteered for the balcony, looked from the lift to the service stairs. “We can take those.”

“They're longer,” Kàra said.

“The hall is repeating,” Jeff replied. “It probably won't run out before we get there.”

Ms Luminara checked the route rather than dismissing the problem. The stairs had solid treads and a rail. At the top was an opaque bench with a back, positioned so someone could study the structure without looking directly down.

Kàra chose the stairs. She did not call the choice a defeat, and nobody supplied that word for her. Halfway up she began counting the alternating positions visible through a side window, then lost count when Jeff asked whether the stair rail itself was part of the lattice.

“It had better not be,” Kàra said. “I've been relying on it as architecture.”

“It is architecture,” Ms Luminara confirmed from behind them. “A visitor aid in an impossible model. Chemical structures don't come with building-code handrails.”

The bench was as ordinary as promised. Ástríðr sat at one end, leaving room for Kàra without turning it into an event. Natalie laid the local-neighborhood card flat between them. The view could be frozen, turned slowly, or replaced with a text description. Kàra chose the description first.

“One selected sodium ion,” she read. “Nearest chloride neighbors at the positive and negative directions of three perpendicular axes.”

She held two pencils at right angles, then asked Jeff for a third. With three pencils crossing at a central point, the six directions became clearer. The pencils were directions, she said, not chemical bonds drawn to scale. The model could help them orient without pretending that the crystal contained wooden spokes.

Henry selected a chloride position instead. The nearest neighbors changed labels: six sodium ions around chloride. He selected a second chloride farther away, then a sodium near the center of the visible interior. The local pattern repeated.

“If every sodium owns six chlorides,” Henry said, “and every chloride owns six sodiums, ownership has become an impossible filing system.”

“Neighborhood isn't ownership,” Ástríðr said.

She had said it lightly, but the sentence stayed. Natalie drew a ring around it. The six neighbors did not belong exclusively to the selected ion. Selecting another center did not create new chloride ions; it brought some of the same positions into another local description.

Kàra turned on the frozen three-dimensional view. With the depth held still, she could compare it to the pencils. The arrangement had become something she could inspect in pieces instead of something she had to swallow all at once.

“I'm seeing why the first pair picture was so tempting,” she said. “It lets one positive and one negative cancel, and then it puts a wall around the result. The wall makes the rest easy to ignore.”

“Useful accounting boundaries needn't be physical walls,” Ms Luminara said. “We can count the composition without assuming each counted unit is an independent object inside the crystal.”

Jeff asked about the word lattice. He had heard it used for garden fencing, where there were literal strips with gaps between them. Did it imply a chemical framework made of visible rods?

“In this setting, it names the extended repeating arrangement,” Ms Luminara said. “A detailed crystallographic description can distinguish the mathematical lattice from the atoms or ions associated with it. Our introductory use is about the periodic structure. We won't ask the garden fence to carry the whole science.”

Below them the usher tried a new bow, this time enclosing one sodium and all six nearest chlorides. The counter displayed NaCl₆.

“Oh, no,” Jackson said softly.

For a moment everyone watched in silence. The usher had listened. It had removed the one-partner rule and substituted six partners. It had kept the deeper assumption that one selected center and its visible neighborhood must be a self-contained unit determining the composition.

“That isn't failure to hear us,” Natalie said. “It's an incomplete repair.”

Henry was already reaching for the intercom, but Ms Luminara asked what evidence would make the distinction visible. Telling the usher the answer was allowed; leaving it without a workable replacement would not help the next visitor.

Ástríðr moved the center of the viewing window by one position. Some ions stayed inside the view, some entered and some left. The selected neighborhood changed without changing the chemical identity of the ordinary solid.

“The formula shouldn't change whenever we move our camera,” she said.

Jeff drew two overlapping neighborhood sketches. He shaded the shared positions with pencil strokes and put labels beside them. “We need a view that shows why local counts overlap. Then another view for the overall ratio.”

Kàra gave him the third pencil back. “And we should keep the balcony. Just change the lift's sales pitch.”

The sales pitch currently read COURAGEOUS VISITORS ONLY. Ms Luminara peeled it away. Beneath it she placed VIEW OPTIONS AVAILABLE.

When they headed down, Kàra paused at the first window. She was still aware of the apparent height. She also knew where the six directions lay. Both things could be true, and the second did not require the first to disappear.

## 5. Six Neighbors, One-to-One Composition

The usher had made its new diagram exceptionally handsome. A central sodium ion shone inside a ring of six chloride labels, though the ring flattened a three-dimensional arrangement into something resembling a flower. At the bottom it had printed: IMPROVED MOLECULE, NOW WITH SIX PARTNERS.

Ms Luminara put the diagram on the workbench. “There is good observation here and an unwarranted conclusion. Let's separate them before either gets thrown away.”

Henry kept the count of six, changed partners to nearest neighbors, and added ordinary solid NaCl, interior environment. Ástríðr removed improved molecule. The large formula still read NaCl₆.

“Can we prove one-to-one by counting everything?” Jeff asked, looking toward a hall that seemed to continue indefinitely.

“We can examine a properly chosen repeat of the structure,” Ms Luminara said. “We can also show why the neighborhood count doesn't perform that job. We don't need to race an infinite hallway.”

She opened a flat teaching board beside the three-dimensional view. It contained alternating plus and minus symbols on an even-by-even square grid. She labeled it A TWO-DIMENSIONAL COUNTING ANALOGY, NOT THE NaCl CRYSTAL STRUCTURE.

Natalie approved of the label. Without it, a convenient checkerboard could become their next overly memorable mistake.

On the flat board, Henry selected a positive sign away from the edge and counted the nearest negative signs above, below, left and right. Four. The whole even-by-even board contained equal numbers of positive and negative signs.

“If I wrote one positive to four negatives from that neighborhood,” he said, “I'd contradict the count of the whole board.”

Kàra selected a neighboring positive sign. Two negative positions appeared in both highlighted neighborhoods. She did it again, moving across the board. The same negative positions served in several local views.

“The local description reuses positions,” she said. “It doesn't issue each one to a single center.”

Jackson found a setting that outlined nonoverlapping pairs on the same board. He tried horizontal pairs, then vertical pairs. Both ways grouped equal counts. Neither choice established that the symbols interacted only with the member of their chosen pair.

“So even when a counting partition works,” he said, “we have to ask whether it's also a structural claim.”

Ms Luminara nodded. “That's the heart of our repair.”

The usher asked whether a formula unit was one of those pairs. Ms Luminara answered carefully. For this ionic compound, NaCl expressed the one-to-one composition ratio represented by a formula unit. That counting language did not mean there were separate two-ion NaCl molecules sitting intact inside ordinary solid salt.

“You can use a unit of composition without assigning each ion a unique chemical spouse,” she said.

The usher emitted a short apologetic squeak. “The invitation department may have overreached.”

“It happens,” Ástríðr said. “Usually with matching outfits.”

They returned to the actual three-dimensional model. A structure information card identified the rock-salt arrangement and its bulk Na:Cl ratio of one to one. The card distinguished the verified structure from the flat analogy they had used to understand overlapping counts. Henry insisted on leaving both visible. Otherwise, a future visitor could mistake the analogy for the evidence supporting the real structure.

Natalie asked what would happen if they selected a badly cropped little piece of the display. Could it contain more of one label than the other?

“Yes,” Ms Luminara said. “An arbitrary finite window can emphasize a boundary or include unequal counts. That does not establish a different bulk formula. We must say whether we are counting a local environment, a selected fragment, or the composition of the ideal bulk material.”

“Then moving our camera can change the contents of the window,” Natalie said, “but it doesn't rewrite the material's bulk composition.”

She had wanted the distinction in an earlier scene and now had it in a form she could defend. She drew three rectangles in her notebook, labeling them neighborhood, window and bulk composition. She did not fill them with identical numbers.

The usher tried a new caption: Each interior sodium ion has six nearest chloride neighbors; the overall sodium-to-chloride ratio is one to one.

Henry read it without objecting. Jeff read it aloud, checking whether someone who did not look at the picture could understand what had changed. Ástríðr replaced the flower with a simple description of the three perpendicular directions.

The NaCl₆ heading disappeared.

A small indicator lit on Jackson's task card: LOCAL CAPTION READY. Beneath it, the dance controls still showed their old pair-envelopes symbol. Jackson held the corrected card against that little picture. The words on the workbench had changed; the records feeding the hall had not.

“We know what the view should say,” Jackson said. “Before we change what runs it, we need to know why the archive insists on partners.”

Kàra remembered the yellow warning from the bus door. “Then we need the arrival story.”

The usher led them to a passage hung with illustrated electrons. Some appeared as arrows, some as dots, and one had been drawn wearing a little suitcase. Ms Luminara removed the suitcase before anyone could ask whether electrons traveled with luggage.

“The archive can show bookkeeping,” she said. “It mustn't persuade us that a diagram of transfer is the force holding a crystal together.”

Natalie kept the repaired caption beside the old one. The difference was small enough to fit on a card and large enough to change the entire hall.

## 6. The Archive of How They Arrived

The arrival archive smelled of warm paper, though its records were made of light. Natalie suspected that Ms Luminara had added the smell for her benefit. There was something reassuring about a place where an explanation could be taken off a shelf and examined rather than arriving as a voice from the ceiling.

The first record showed a neutral sodium atom and a neutral chlorine atom. Each had a count of protons, a count of electrons and a large arrow between them. The electron arrow was bright enough to dominate everything else.

Kàra watched the animation once. A dot moved from one outlined atom to the other. The sodium label became Na⁺; the chlorine label became Cl⁻. A ribbon promptly appeared between the two.

“There,” she said. “That's the picture I carried upstairs. Transfer, charge, pair. I don't remember choosing the last step.”

“You may not have,” Natalie said. “The animation chose it for you.”

Ms Luminara stopped the record before the ribbon appeared. “The bookkeeping part can be useful. Sodium has eleven protons. A neutral sodium atom has eleven electrons. If we describe it losing one electron, the ion has eleven protons and ten electrons. The net charge is plus one.”

Henry adjusted the neighboring ledger. Chlorine had seventeen protons. A neutral chlorine atom had seventeen electrons. Gaining an electron gave eighteen electrons and net charge minus one. Neither nucleus changed its proton count. Sodium did not become neon; chloride did not become argon merely because an electron count resembled that of another species.

They had used that distinction before, but Natalie was glad the archive did not require them to retrieve it instantly. The numbers were supplied. Their task was to inspect the inference built upon them.

Jeff traced the charge on both sides of the bookkeeping display. The initial total was zero. The final positive and negative charges also added to zero. The electron had not vanished; the representation had reassigned it.

“Conservation works,” he said. “And still no reason for the ribbon.”

“Here the total stays zero because we began with two neutral atoms,” Ms Luminara said. “Conservation means preserving the total for a system that exchanges no charge with its surroundings. It doesn’t require every system to start at zero.”

Jeff added a small boundary around the two ledgers. Inside it, the electron had changed accounts without leaving the system.

Ástríðr asked what happened if the animation began with already existing ions rather than neutral atoms. The usher searched its catalog and found a second record showing ionic positions arranged into the same ordinary structural model. There were no donor names attached to individual ions.

“Does that mean this is how salt is really made?” Ástríðr asked.

“Not by this animation,” Ms Luminara said. “These are two explanatory starting points. We are not presenting a complete preparation route or a movie of an actual reaction. A reaction has conditions, changes in energy and often many particles to account for. A one-electron arrow is much less than that.”

Kàra asked the question that the original movie had made unnecessary. “Does moving the electron just happen because the resulting counts look neat?”

“No. A filled-shell pattern can help organize introductory expectations, but neatness is not a force or an energy source. Removing an electron from an isolated atom requires energy. Other steps can release energy. For a real process, we consider the whole relevant energy account and conditions, not a wish for a completed drawing.”

Natalie drew a box around the sentence requires energy. She had known that in another setting. Here the bright arrow had made the transfer look free, like dragging a file from one folder to another.

“So the archive has compressed two different problems,” she said. “How the ions can form, and why the resulting solid can be stable.”

“And then answered the second with the first,” Jackson added.

The usher asked whether they wanted the transfer record deleted. Jeff said no before Henry could reach for the shelf label.

“It does a job,” Jeff said. “It shows which charge changes when an electron is lost or gained. We should stop it from doing jobs it can't do.”

He wrote a replacement caption: SIMPLIFIED ELECTRON BOOKKEEPING FOR FORMING THESE IONS. NOT A COMPLETE REACTION MECHANISM, ENERGY ACCOUNT OR PERMANENT PARTNER ASSIGNMENT.

Ástríðr thought it was too long for a caption. They made two versions: a short label naming electron bookkeeping, and a readable explanation directly below. The longer account remained available instead of disappearing into tiny print.

The next archive drawer contained hundreds of names attached to donor arrows. The usher had been using those names to bind the pair envelopes. Jackson asked it to make a draft copy of the counts without those artificial partnership fields. He watched the copy change, checking that species identity and charge survived. The old records remained under their warning until the class could test the replacement in the control room.

A sodium ion now had a species label, a charge and a position in the structural display. It had no favorite chloride field.

The usher waited, as if the empty field ought to set off an alarm. Nothing physical in the displayed arrangement required it. The model still had many interactions to describe, but the unsupported biography was gone.

Kàra walked back to the first animation and watched it again with the ribbon disabled. The same arrow now meant less and explained more accurately.

“I don't have to unlearn the counts,” she said. “I have to stop the little story at the place where its evidence stops.”

Ms Luminara took the yellow warning from her pocket. “That is why I brought this.”

Natalie thought the teacher might pin it over the archive door. Instead, Ms Luminara handed it to the usher, who placed it beside its own clipboard.

From beneath the floor came a low humming sound. Jeff looked down.

“Please tell me that's the part about why things stay.”

Ms Luminara's dress acquired a pattern of hills and valleys. “Let's look under the floorboards.”

<a id="ch03-scene-07"></a>
## 7. Under the Floorboards

There was no trapdoor. Ms Luminara sat on the archive's lowest step, and the floor became a large schematic graph. It did not plunge away. It lay quietly beneath their shoes, with a clearly marked separation axis and an energy axis drawn along the wall.

Kàra looked relieved. “A floorboard excursion that keeps the floor.”

“I occasionally exercise restraint,” Ms Luminara said. “The bus finds it unsettling.”

A model positive charge and negative charge appeared at a large separation. When Ms Luminara moved them closer in the simplified electrostatic picture, the attraction became stronger. The energy associated with that opposite-charge interaction decreased relative to the chosen separated reference.

Natalie almost said that the particles wanted to get closer. She stopped herself, less out of fear of a forbidden word than because she now needed the word to do actual explanatory work.

“The energy changes with separation,” she said. “Calling that wanting doesn't tell us how.”

“Exactly,” Ms Luminara said. “A metaphor can begin a question. It mustn't pretend to finish the mechanism.”

Jeff watched the spheres move toward one another. “Then why doesn't the whole model collapse into one spot?”

“That is the question the attraction-only picture cannot answer.”

The graph changed. Ms Luminara added a steeply rising contribution at very short separation, then a schematic total with a low region at an intermediate spacing. The graph had no numerical scale, and a label said it was a qualitative effective-energy illustration, not a fitted NaCl measurement.

“Real atoms and ions have electronic structure,” she said. “They are not classical point charges we can press together without limit. At short distances, the full interaction includes strongly repulsive effects associated with that electronic structure, alongside the other interactions. In this sketch, a stable equilibrium spacing lies at a minimum of the total potential energy.”

Henry held the new archive card above the graph. It listed one sodium ion, one charge and no partner. He looked at the two spheres, then back at the card.

“If we put this curve beside every sodium in the hall, haven't we supplied a partner again?”

“Unless it's six curves,” Natalie began. She stopped when Kàra laid their nearest-neighbor sketch beside her shoe. The sketch had deliberately left out more distant positions.

“We'd still be copying the little picture into a job it hasn't done,” Kàra said.

“Exactly,” Ms Luminara said. “This curve illustrates why attraction alone doesn't justify unlimited collapse. It is not a complete energy account for each ion or for the crystal. That requires the whole arrangement and its many-particle interactions. We can keep this limited illustration without promoting it to a calculation of the hall.”

Henry moved the archive card to the other side of the step. The space between the two displays was only a hand's breadth, but it prevented him from laying one over the other as if they answered the same question.

Kàra sat cross-legged beside the minimum. “And balance doesn't mean all the forces individually disappear.”

She remembered a suspended object whose forces summed to zero while the rope remained under tension. The comparison helped her ask the next question, but she labeled the limit herself: ions in the crystal were not small weights hanging from invisible strings. The force-diagram habit was useful; the imagined ropes were not.

Ms Luminara turned on a modest motion setting. The indicated positions remained organized, while the represented ions moved slightly around them. At nonzero temperature, an ordinary solid was not a photograph of absolutely motionless particles. Their illustration exaggerated and slowed the motion enough to see it.

Ástríðr leaned forward. “So repeated position doesn't mean frozen possession of one exact point forever.”

“Right. The structural picture is an idealized description. Motion, boundaries and imperfections add further detail. We can learn the ordinary repeated pattern without pretending those details cease to exist.”

Natalie held her pencil at one indicated position while the represented ion moved slightly around it. The mark on her page could stay still. She added a small motion note beside it rather than trying to draw every frame. The page became readable again.

Jackson asked what the dance controls actually needed from this deeper view. They did not need a numerical simulation of crystal vibrations to repair a seating diagram. They did need to stop treating a decorative ribbon as the force holding two ions together.

“Then our new chart should state which relationships it draws,” he said. “If it shows only nearest neighbors for readability, it should say those aren't the only electrostatic interactions.”

The usher wrote that down, carefully spelling only. Its previous captions had treated omissions as claims of nonexistence.

Jeff watched the vibrating model for a while, then chose the still view again. “I understand the motion better with the option to stop the animation. That seems contradictory.”

“The representation can be still while its caption explains that the real system isn't perfectly still,” Natalie said. “We don't have to make your eyes perform the physics.”

Ástríðr asked whether the low region in the graph meant the solid could never change. Ms Luminara said no. Stability under specified conditions was not eternal indestructibility. Changes in conditions or interactions could matter. Their afternoon's model was not a complete theory of phase changes, dissolution or chemical reaction, and they would not pretend that one valley answered those questions.

For a minute nobody asked anything. The graph glowed under the floor, and the archive shelves looked strangely peaceful above it. Natalie realized how much of her first mental picture had depended on an ending: the electron moved, the shell filled, the story stopped. The solid they were now describing had an arrangement, continuing interactions and conditions. It did not need a dramatic new event every moment in order to remain a physical system.

Kàra stood and traced the total-energy curve with one foot, staying clear of the raised edge of the display. “The reason things stay isn't a memory of arrival. It's the current system.”

“That's a useful sentence,” Ms Luminara said. “Keep room after it for the details of whichever system you're studying.”

Ástríðr looked from the energy sketch to the word ionic on the door. “Are ionic and covalent two perfectly separate kinds of reality?”

“They are useful descriptions, with idealized endpoints,” Ms Luminara said. “Real bonding can have mixed character. We can use the dominant ionic picture to understand this ordinary solid without claiming that every detail of its electronic structure is exhausted by perfect charged spheres.”

Natalie folded the graph page so that its label, QUALITATIVE ILLUSTRATION, stayed visible. She could now find the answer to Jeff's collapse question without mistaking the sketch for a complete account of the crystal. The unfinished many-particle question went on the back.

The usher rolled up the graph as though it were a rug, which it was not. Beneath it lay a cable leading back to the dance controls.

Jackson followed the cable with his eyes. “Now let's see whether our repaired explanation can survive being used.”

## 8. The Rehearsal That Went Dark

The cable led them through a service door into the dance-control room. Of its three windows, one looked onto the hall, one showed the nearest-neighbor diagram, and one displayed the old pair-envelope records. The last window remained covered with a warning: COMPOSITION COUNTS RETAINED; EXCLUSIVE PAIR ASSIGNMENTS INVALID. Jackson laid the corrected caption and the archive's draft records beside the controls. Neither had been connected yet.

Jackson asked the usher to run the entrance sequence slowly. No visitors were waiting, and the display could be restored from its saved state. Jeff checked both facts before volunteering to operate the controls.

The sequence began by lighting one selected Na⁺ position, then its supposed permanent chloride partner. A spotlight appeared around their oval. The nearest-neighbor window showed the other adjacent chloride positions, but the spotlight controller ignored them.

“Freeze there,” Henry said.

Jeff stopped the sequence. Nothing about the ions was actually responding to stage lighting. The failure belonged to the exhibit. Natalie appreciated that distinction enough to say it aloud: they were testing the faithfulness of a representation, not discovering crystal structure by controlling theater lamps.

The next cue hid the selected two positions and drew a gap. The nearest-neighbor diagram needed several incident connections removed from view. The pair program removed only its one bow. Lines remained pointing to empty space.

Ástríðr winced. “It looks like a bridge whose end has gone missing.”

“Useful visual description,” Ms Luminara said. “Keep the bridge in the picture. Don't make it a literal rod holding the ions.”

The usher offered the simplest patch: erase every line attached to a vanished sphere. That repaired the immediate display but left the underlying pair list unchanged. When the spheres reappeared, the program restored only the old bow.

Jackson shook his head. “We'd have to patch the same wrong assumption at every cue. We need the relationship record to match the display's purpose.”

Henry began a new record for each displayed position. It identified the species, the charge, the location in their schematic model and the nearest-neighbor links relevant to the chosen view. The count record separately tracked the represented sodium-to-chloride ratio. Neither required an exclusive partner.

Kàra objected that a list of nearest neighbors might still conceal interactions with more distant ions. Henry had anticipated the objection only partly. He had written relationships instead of nearest-neighbor links.

He changed the heading before defending it. “You're right. This is a local structural view, not the complete force calculation.”

Henry slid the corrected heading back to Kàra. She read it, nodded and held the page steady while he entered the first set of links. Jeff waited at the controls. This time they would be testing the record they had actually written, rather than hoping the corrected sign had changed it for them.

They tried the new record on a small section first. Jeff hid one selected position. Every visible local link ending at it disappeared. Restoring the position restored those links. Moving the center of attention highlighted a different neighborhood without changing the overall composition label.

Ástríðr checked the caption from the walkway, where a visitor would actually encounter it. From there, the words NOT INDEPENDENT MOLECULES were partly hidden behind a decorative banner. She removed the banner.

“The explanation existed,” she said, “but not where anyone could see it.”

The second rehearsal progressed farther. Then the lights went dark across an entire row. The usher made a distressed whirring noise.

Jeff kept one hand on the stop control. “The display is stopped. Nothing's being erased. Let's find the first wrong step.”

A far-end controller was still reading the old pair envelopes. It assumed that each envelope could appear only once. The new local views shared positions, as neighborhoods necessarily did, and the old controller rejected them as duplicates.

“So the same chloride appears in two neighbors' records,” Jackson said. “The controller thinks we've invented another chloride.”

Henry pointed to the unchanged position identifier. “It's a repeated reference to one position, not a second ion.”

They repaired that distinction. A local link record could refer to a position already mentioned elsewhere. Counts would come from the set of displayed positions, not from adding every occurrence in every neighborhood list. The method was their fictional display design; the underlying lesson was the same one they had seen at the balcony.

Natalie wrote: Counting mentions is not always counting things.

She paused. It sounded broad enough to become a slogan. Under it she added the specific case: A chloride position can occur in several sodium-neighborhood descriptions. That does not multiply the chloride ions in the crystal.

The third rehearsal worked on the small section. Nobody rang a triumph bell. Jackson asked them to try a different center, a different viewing direction and a still-text version. Kàra chose a position near the edge of the displayed window and made sure the caption did not pretend that the crop showed every neighbor beyond it.

Only then did the usher copy the repaired view to the full hall. The lights returned in rows and layers, with no ovals separating imaginary couples. The structure had not changed because their explanation improved. Their display had become less misleading.

Ástríðr watched the alternating labels reappear. “It looks less like a ballroom seating chart now.”

“More like a city,” Jeff said.

“A city without couples,” Natalie said, and wrote the phrase across a clean page.

Ms Luminara nodded toward the remaining two tasks. “A useful title. Now we must make sure the city hasn't outlawed genuine groups just because its first grouping was wrong.”

## 9. Biscuits in the Unclassified Courtyard

The courtyard had a fountain that circulated no water. Its streams were decorative lines of light, clearly labeled as such because Henry had developed a look that made undocumented metaphors uncomfortable. Around the fountain stood chairs that did not need to represent anything.

Ms Luminara set down a tin of biscuits.

“Ordinary refreshments,” she said. “The crumbs have not been assigned educational roles.”

Ástríðr immediately dropped one onto her notebook. “Good. Mine refuses to cooperate with the diagram.”

They sat without arranging themselves by charge or by confidence. Jeff leaned back and looked at the hall through an archway. Kàra chose a chair facing the flat garden wall. Natalie rested her pencil across the page and let the recent distinctions settle instead of writing another conclusion before she understood it.

The usher remained at the courtyard entrance holding the retired ribbons. Its instructions had lost several jobs at once. It could still welcome visitors, maintain the displays and provide directions, but it kept looking down at the spools.

Ástríðr brought it an empty biscuit tin. “Temporary storage,” she said. “We don't know all the useful jobs a ribbon can have.”

“The lattice had no approved pair boundaries,” the usher said. “Perhaps no boundary should be drawn.”

Henry stopped halfway through a biscuit. “That is the opposite overstatement.”

He sounded sharper than he intended. The usher's head tilted. Henry put the biscuit down and started again.

“We found that one particular boundary was wrong for ordinary solid NaCl. That doesn't tell us there are no discrete molecules or bonded groups elsewhere.”

“Thank you,” Ms Luminara said. “A correction can overshoot if we send it farther than the evidence goes.”

Natalie glanced at her new title, The City Without Couples. She had nearly written without groups beneath it. The pencil hovered there until she set it across the page.

“I like a clean rule,” she said. “I also like discovering that a clean rule was wrong. Both can make me too quick.”

Jeff turned his cup between his hands. “I liked the stop button today. Not because I wanted to leave. Because I wanted enough time to see which thing changed first.”

“You used it well,” Jackson said. “The first failure would have been harder to trace if we'd let all the cues run.”

Natalie reached for her pencil and found a small ache where she had been gripping it. She set it down again. Across the courtyard, Henry was tapping a pattern with one finger on his cup. Jeff answered it against the chair arm, adding a beat that made Henry look up.

“Was that deliberate?” Henry asked.

“The second time,” Jeff said.

They tried it once more and lost the rhythm together. Ástríðr smiled into her tea. Nobody rescued the pattern by explaining it. Kàra tilted her chair back, found its safe balance and looked at a patch of ordinary sky above the wall.

The usher offered Ms Luminara its old scorecard, which counted pairs completed per minute. She turned it over and drew the route to the next room on the blank side. For now she left the card beneath the biscuit tin.

“Does the dance have to happen today?” Ástríðr asked.

“The exhibit can open when its claims are ready,” Ms Luminara said. “There's no queue outside, and the musicians are very patient recordings.”

“Then stop announcing a delay,” Ástríðr told the usher. “Say it's being revised. People can choose whether to come back.”

The usher changed the announcement. Natalie heard the new version drift through the archway, calm enough that she did not feel she ought to stand up halfway through her tea. The work still mattered. Whoever came later would meet these labels without the class standing beside them to explain what they had meant.

The courtyard grew quiet. In the distance the hall's lamps moved through their rehearsal, slower now. Without music, the repeating pattern looked almost like a woven fabric extending through space, except Natalie knew that fabric would import threads and knots they had not established. She allowed herself to enjoy the resemblance without writing it as a mechanism.

Kàra noticed. “You started a comparison and didn't say it.”

“I was checking what it would make us imagine.”

“That takes the fun out of some comparisons.”

“Only if every comparison has to become a law,” Natalie said.

Ástríðr lifted the biscuit tin. “This is a collection of ribbons held by a lid. It's also a metaphor for my excellent tidying. Neither claim explains ionic bonding.”

Jeff laughed so hard that he had to set down his cup.

The usher asked whether it could keep the old pair chart in the archive as a record of the mistake. Ms Luminara said yes, with a clear explanation of why it had been replaced. They did not need to pretend the class had begun with the right picture. An account of repair could help the next learner recognize a similar assumption without feeling that everyone else had understood it instantly.

Natalie dated her original sketch and drew an arrow to the revised one. She did not erase the first. Beneath it she wrote: Right charge total; wrong exclusive grouping.

The faint bow was still visible through her first diagram. Natalie pressed the page flat rather than rubbing harder with the eraser. A biscuit crumb fell from the fold onto her knee, and she brushed it away.

The last biscuit remained in the tin. Henry offered to divide it, then remembered Ms Luminara's warning about assigning educational roles to crumbs.

“That's ordinary sharing,” she said. “Permitted.”

When they stood, the usher retrieved three display cards from the route board. N₂. CO₂. NH₄⁺.

“Three arrivals,” it announced. “Two drawers.”

Jackson looked at the labels on the drawers and sighed with recognition.

“Then the courtyard was a very good idea.”

## 10. Three Displays and Two Bad Drawers

The species gallery was bright, orderly and wrong in a particularly efficient way. Every new display approached a machine with two drawers: IONIC and COVALENT. The machine selected one drawer, stamped the card and sent the object to the corresponding wing.

The N₂ card had been stamped COVALENT COMPOUND. CO₂ carried the same stamp. NH₄⁺ had been returned to the entrance with a note saying CONTRADICTORY INFORMATION.

Ástríðr picked up the ammonium card. “What contradiction?”

“Covalent bonds detected,” the machine replied. “Overall charge detected. Please remove one.”

“No,” Henry said, with the quiet firmness he reserved for a symbol being asked to change its job.

Ms Luminara stopped the conveyor. She did not ask the class to guess until the machine approved of them. Beside each display she placed a card of supplied facts. N₂ was a neutral discrete molecule containing two nitrogen atoms. CO₂ was a neutral discrete molecule containing one carbon and two oxygen atoms. NH₄⁺ was a charged bonded group containing one nitrogen and four hydrogens.

“We have enough information to separate the questions,” she said. “Which atoms are present? What is the overall charge? What kind of entity is described? What bonding description are we using inside it?”

Natalie drew columns instead of two boxes. She remembered the lattice count: an answer to one question could be correct without answering another. The gallery had taken bond description and treated it as a complete classification of everything else.

Jeff began with N₂. “Two atoms, one kind of element. Neutral. A molecule, but not a compound.”

The machine protested that two atoms had been detected. Jeff placed two nitrogen labels side by side and asked it how many distinct element symbols were present. The answer was one.

“Number of atoms isn't number of elements,” he said. “The two doesn't turn one element into two elements.”

Kàra inspected the representation between the nitrogen atoms. Ms Luminara described covalent bonding at the level they needed: shared electron density in the bond, within a quantum account richer than the drawn links. A line in a structural drawing was not a wire, and electrons were not little ribbons tied between hard balls.

“Covalent can describe the bond,” Kàra said. “Elemental can describe what substance it is. Those labels don't compete for one drawer.”

CO₂ passed through the same set of questions. The displayed entity contained different elements and was a neutral molecule. Carbon dioxide, the substance made of those molecules under the stated conditions, was a molecular compound. The covalent description of its internal bonds did not make it identical to elemental N₂.

Ástríðr held the two cards together. “The old drawer preserved a resemblance and erased a difference.”

“That is an excellent description of a bad sorting system,” Jackson said.

The ammonium display waited beside them. The plus sign sat high and to the right of the whole formula. Henry enlarged it and moved the card slightly so everyone could see it.

“The four counts hydrogen atoms,” he said. “The plus tells us the whole written species has net positive charge. It isn't another hydrogen, and it doesn't say the fourth hydrogen is the only charged part.”

Ms Luminara confirmed the group-level reading. NH₄⁺ was a polyatomic ion: an ion made of multiple atoms. Its N–H bonds were covalent in the introductory description. Having covalent bonds inside did not cancel the net charge of the group.

The machine asked which drawer should receive it.

“Neither drawer can do the entire job,” Natalie said.

She had expected to solve the problem by choosing a more clever label. Now she removed the drawers' fronts. Beneath them were ordinary shelves. The shelves could hold displays with several separate descriptors, as long as each descriptor answered an identified question.

Kàra wrote three large headings on the gallery window: WHICH ATOMS? WHAT OVERALL CHARGE? WHAT INTERACTIONS?

Henry added a smaller fourth line beneath them: WHAT ENTITY OR SUBSTANCE IS THE CLAIM ABOUT? It reminded them that an ion, its internal bonds and a bulk compound containing it belonged to different levels of description.

Jackson checked what the gallery sent to the formula workshop. Previously it sent only the one drawer stamp. Now it would send the formula, the charge if present, the supplied identity and the scope of the classification. The workshop would not have to reconstruct all that information from a single word.

Before they restarted the conveyor, Jeff asked for one more test. He wrote O₂ and H₂O on blank cards, supplying the facts that both were neutral discrete molecules. He wanted to see whether the method worked after the familiar letters changed.

Ástríðr identified O₂ as an elemental molecule and the H₂O entity as a molecule of the molecular compound water. Henry explained that both could contain covalent bonds without belonging to the same substance category. The machine no longer asked anyone to remove a charge merely because a group had internal bonds.

Natalie stood back from the repaired gallery. It was slightly less tidy than before. There were more words and fewer absolute partitions. But every word now had a job she could state.

The usher unrolled one of its recovered ribbons and held it beside ammonium.

“Would an outline be wrong here?” it asked.

Ms Luminara smiled. “That's the right next question. Let's find out exactly what we mean to enclose.”

## 11. A Boundary Worth Keeping

Ástríðr laid a ribbon loosely around the ammonium display. It enclosed one nitrogen label and four hydrogen labels. She left the plus sign visible just outside the outline, where it could apply to the whole enclosed group.

“This ribbon doesn't say who donated an electron to whom,” she said. “It marks the species we're talking about.”

Henry checked the words on the display. “And it doesn't claim the ribbon itself is a bond.”

The usher watched with evident relief. Its ribbons had not become scientifically forbidden objects. Their meaning had become conditional on what they represented.

Natalie wanted to look inside the group before the workshop treated it as a tile. A whole-ion tile was useful for formula accounting, but she did not want the convenience to make ammonium seem like one enormous atom.

Ms Luminara opened a small drawing desk. “We can make a limited Lewis-model visit. We won't pretend this one example teaches every possible structure.”

The desk supplied neutral-atom valence contributions for the example: nitrogen, five; hydrogen, one per atom. Four hydrogens contributed four. The neutral contributions totaled nine, and the positive charge meant one fewer electron in the whole-ion valence budget, leaving eight.

Kàra frowned at the subtraction, then wrote it in words before symbols. “Positive charge means a shortage of negatively charged electrons relative to the neutral-atom total. So five plus four minus one equals eight.”

“Exactly,” Ms Luminara said. “The charge adjustment belongs to the whole species once. We don't subtract one separately from every atom.”

Jeff placed four bond lines between the central N and four H labels. Each line represented a shared electron pair in the Lewis drawing. Four pairs used eight electrons. There was no remaining lone pair on the nitrogen in this model.

Henry checked the total inventory first, then each atom's local check. Each hydrogen counted its one bond pair as two electrons. Nitrogen counted four bond pairs around it, eight electrons in the local octet check.

Natalie wrote the hydrogen total beside the nitrogen total: eight and eight. Her pencil continued to sixteen before she noticed what she had done.

“That can't be our budget. I've counted something twice.”

Jeff laid two small counters beside each drawn bond, eight counters altogether. Henry pointed first from a hydrogen toward its pair, then from nitrogen toward the same pair. Jeff moved no counters and added none.

“The same pair enters both atoms' local checks,” Henry said. “But the whole drawing still has only eight valence electrons.”

Natalie crossed out the addition sign, leaving both local checks intact. She looked toward the lattice hall. “Different chemistry, related counting caution. I knew not to add every neighborhood. I still added overlapping electron checks.”

Ms Luminara agreed with the narrow comparison. A shared electron pair in a Lewis model was not a chloride ion shared among neighborhood descriptions. The two situations had different physical meanings. The reusable habit was to identify the entity and the counting question before adding numbers.

The flat drawing looked like a cross. Ástríðr tilted her head. “Are the hydrogens actually in a flat cross?”

“No. Connectivity on paper doesn't automatically specify the three-dimensional shape.”

The desk offered a stationary model with four equivalent N–H directions arranged tetrahedrally. Ms Luminara named the introductory electron-domain model: four bonding regions around nitrogen and no central lone pair gave tetrahedral geometry for ammonium. It was a model of spatial arrangement, not a claim that rigid sticks or drawn electron pairs were tiny mechanical arms.

Kàra could see two hydrogens better than the others from her first angle. She moved her chair instead of spinning the display. A previously hidden hydrogen became visible.

“The formula counted it even when the picture hid it,” she said.

The usher tried to infer the formula from the first viewpoint and got NH₂⁺. Jeff stopped it.

“You were given the species identity,” he said. “A partly hidden display doesn't authorize deleting atoms.”

They made the text description independent of the perspective: one nitrogen bonded to four hydrogens; tetrahedral arrangement in this model; net charge plus one for the whole ion. A visitor could use the description without manipulating the model.

Natalie found herself wondering where the plus was located. The superscript made it visually easy to imagine a positive speck sitting just beyond the fourth hydrogen. Ms Luminara separated notation from physical distribution. The superscript reported the net charge of the whole species. More detailed descriptions of charge distribution required their own models; the typography did not supply a location.

“Even if a Lewis formal-charge calculation assigns a bookkeeping charge to an atom,” she added, “that is not permission to interpret the entire electron distribution as a literal integer charge stuck at that point.”

Henry did not ask them to calculate every formal charge. He was satisfied that the outline's job had been stated precisely: preserve the whole ion's identity and overall charge for the formula task.

Ástríðr retied the loose ribbon around the display's base, where it could not hide a hydrogen label. “The group is real in the description. My knot is just an index finger.”

“An unusually festive index finger,” Ms Luminara said.

The class returned the Lewis desk to its original size. Natalie kept the page with sixteen crossed out. Eight counters still lay on the desk; she slid them into its small tray before closing it. Beside the corrected budget she kept the four shared pairs, the tetrahedral description and the whole-ion charge. This one worked example would help her read the next tile. It was not a universal recipe for drawing unfamiliar polyatomic ions.

The usher carried the intact ammonium tile toward the workshop. This time the ribbon did not bind it to a particular negative ion. It identified what must remain together in their accounting.

Jeff opened the workshop door. On the central balance sat one ammonium tile beside a carbonate tile, and a large red display read −1.

“At least,” he said, “the next problem is honest about its sign.”

## 12. The Whole Ion Comes Along

The workshop walls were covered in pockets, each containing a labeled model tile. The tiles were not samples or ingredients for actual synthesis. They were durable representations for composing formulas from supplied ions. At the center stood a balance whose display read NET CHARGE, IN UNITS OF THE ELEMENTARY CHARGE.

“Not mass,” Henry said before touching anything.

“Correct,” Ms Luminara replied. “It looks like a balance because opposing totals are useful to picture. The reading is charge arithmetic.”

One pocket held ammonium, NH₄⁺. Another held carbonate, CO₃²⁻. The carbonate tile displayed one carbon, three oxygens and an overall charge of minus two. Its internal composition was printed beneath a transparent cover.

Ástríðr tried lifting the cover and found that it could open, revealing a more detailed reference view. Ms Luminara explained why it was not locked: polyatomic ions were not literally indestructible. They could participate in chemical changes. The cover was a rule of their present formula-building task, which began with these specified intact ions.

“So we preserve the group because the question gives us that group,” Ástríðr said. “Not because chemistry makes it impossible for the group to react.”

Natalie nodded. “That distinction keeps the tile from becoming a tiny sealed box in nature.”

The existing attempt placed one ammonium tile and one carbonate tile on the charge balance. Plus one and minus two gave minus one. The display had not failed. It was accurately reporting that this chosen ratio did not make a neutral ionic composition.

Jeff reached for another ammonium tile, then paused to state his prediction. “Another plus one should bring the total to zero. It brings the whole ammonium ion, including one nitrogen and four hydrogens.”

He placed it on the balance. The display changed to zero.

Jeff grinned and lifted his hand from the tile as though the reading might change if he touched it. Natalie grinned back. She had been keeping the atom counts in her notebook and, on seeing zero, drew a tick beside the whole line.

“Two ammonium ions for every carbonate ion,” Jeff said. “The ratio of whole ion counts is two to one.”

The printer produced N₂H₄CO₃.

Henry stared at it. “That is not what we placed on the balance.”

The printer had duplicated the first atom in each positive tile while leaving the internal hydrogen count unchanged. Its duplication rule treated a polyatomic ion as though only its first symbol could receive the multiplier.

Jackson pulled the printout from the machine. “This is the downstream consequence of not knowing what one thing was.”

Ástríðr laid the two ammonium tiles side by side. One nitrogen and four hydrogens in the first. One nitrogen and four hydrogens in the second. Together, two nitrogens and eight hydrogens. The carbonate contributed one carbon and three oxygens.

Natalie counted them twice in different ways: by adding tile contents, then by reading the proposed formula. The disagreement appeared at hydrogen immediately. She looked at the tick she had just written.

“I marked the whole line right because the balance said zero.” She crossed out the tick and put a smaller one beside charge only. “The reading never checked this printout.”

Jeff held the second ammonium tile beside the paper until she had marked its four missing hydrogens on the printout. Neither of them had chosen the wrong tile. They had let the successful part of the task stand for the part still unexamined.

Ms Luminara showed the grouping notation: (NH₄)₂CO₃. The outside subscript two multiplied the entire NH₄ group. There was one carbonate, so no outside two followed carbonate. Its internal three still counted three oxygen atoms per carbonate ion.

Henry pointed to each symbol in turn. “Parentheses mark the repeated group. The outside subscript counts copies of that group. The inside four stays part of ammonium's identity.”

The printer asked whether it should put plus and minus charges over the completed formula. Ms Luminara distinguished the written neutral compound formula from the separate ions listed as inputs. Their charges remained essential to the reasoning, but the compound's formula had no overall charge superscript because the selected composition was neutral.

Ástríðr looked from the formula to the element list. “There's no metal.”

“Correct.”

“And we are describing an ionic compound.”

“Yes. Ammonium is the cation. A metal-plus-nonmetal shortcut does not exhaust ionic compounds.”

A low cabinet rolled in through a service hatch: the gallery's former sorting machine, with its drawer fronts removed. It delivered the new card on an open shelf. Under WHOLE SPECIES the card said ammonium ion; under INTERNAL BONDING it said covalent. Natalie checked those separate lines against the compound formula. This time the gallery had passed on enough information. The missing hydrogens had been lost here, in the printer.

Kàra inspected the newly printed formula and drew a large oval around two ammonium tiles and one carbonate tile. She stopped herself before tying a ribbon around the oval.

“Are we making a three-ion molecule now?”

“No,” Ms Luminara said. “The formula records the ionic composition ratio. Preserving whole polyatomic ions does not turn the neutral ionic solid into separate bundles consisting of exactly two ammoniums permanently assigned to one carbonate.”

Natalie let out a small breath. The distinction was subtler than the earlier repair. Some groupings were meaningful within the material: the polyatomic ions had internal bonding. The particular set used to state a formula ratio was not automatically another discrete molecule layered around them.

Jeff separated the counting arrangement from the structure card. “These tiles tell us how many of each ion go into the ratio. They don't give the entire crystal architecture.”

The charge display stayed at zero as they looked at it. Ms Luminara did not call that proof that every imagined combination could be synthesized or remain stable. Neutrality was a constraint on the formula task, not a certificate for every property of a real substance.

The usher printed the corrected card and, for the first time that afternoon, asked before adding a decorative border.

“Does this outline mean a molecule?”

“It means a label,” Henry said.

“Approved,” Ástríðr added, and handed back a ribbon.

## 13. The Cargo That Lost Its Oxygen

The sign room answered their corrected ammonium carbonate card with a request for calcium nitrate, built from the supplied ions Ca²⁺ and NO₃⁻. Beneath it was a practical question: would the correction work for a repeated negative ion too? A paper carrier brought the request through a miniature delivery door and waited with unnecessary engine noises.

“We have invented a complicated way to move a note six feet,” Jackson said.

Ms Luminara patted the carrier. “It has ambitions.”

The request was clear enough to begin without guessing charges or memorizing ion names. Calcium carried plus two. Nitrate was the whole group NO₃⁻, carrying minus one. The workshop still displayed a sign reminding visitors that its tiles were accounting models, not a method of preparing chemicals.

Natalie placed one tile of each on the balance. The charge total was plus one. She knew the likely repair, but waited long enough to describe why it was needed.

“One nitrate supplies only minus one against calcium's plus two. A second whole nitrate supplies the other minus one.”

She added it. The balance read zero.

The carrier's packing slip printed CaNO₃₂.

Ástríðr tilted the paper. “Is that thirty-two oxygens?”

“As printed, yes: the subscript is thirty-two,” Henry said. “It doesn’t mean two nitrate groups. We shouldn’t repair it silently and then pretend the notation was fine.”

The carrier explained that it had appended a two to the last subscript instead of multiplying the whole group. This was a representation failure as well as a chemistry risk: a reader could not safely infer the intended grouping from its confidence.

Henry placed parentheses around nitrate and put the outside two after them: Ca(NO₃)₂. He then read the formula aloud as one calcium and two nitrate groups. Each nitrate contained one nitrogen and three oxygens.

Jeff laid six oxygen counters in two rows of three. “Two nitrogens, six oxygens. One calcium.”

Kàra preferred a compact arithmetic check. She wrote Ca: 1; N: 2 × 1 = 2; O: 2 × 3 = 6. The group multiplier applied to each atom count inside the parentheses. It did not change the identity of nitrate from NO₃⁻ into some invented group with a different oxygen count.

The carrier accepted the repaired formula, turned toward its door, then stopped. Its payload report listed one calcium tile, two nitrogen counters and three oxygen counters.

Jackson laughed once, without amusement. “The sign is fixed. The internal packing list is still wrong.”

The class had corrected the visible text but not the record used by the next room. The sign room would receive a polished formula alongside contradictory atom counts. Jackson asked the carrier which record it treated as authoritative.

“Most recent printed label.”

“Then why didn't your counts update?”

“Printed labels and payload contents are independent.”

Natalie brought her notebook alongside the packing slip. This time her ticks occupied two separate margins: charge on the left, atom inventory on the right. She covered the charge tick with her thumb while checking the oxygen line. The display's successful zero could no longer distract her from the three missing counters.

They rebuilt the carrier's accounting from whole-ion records. One calcium ion. Two nitrate ions. The atom totals were derived by counting each complete group, and the charge total was derived separately from the ion charges. Henry insisted on retaining both checks. A formula could pass one while failing the other if the inputs or notation were corrupted.

Jeff proposed sending a test card back through the gallery and forward again. He wanted to see whether its identity survived the complete route. Ástríðr volunteered to read the returned card without knowing which version the carrier had used.

The first return read CaN₂O₆. Its total atom counts matched, but the group structure of the ionic formula was no longer apparent. Ms Luminara explained that expanded atom counts could be useful in a counting note, while Ca(NO₃)₂ was the appropriate conventional formula preserving the nitrate grouping for their task.

“Equivalent totals don't mean equally informative notation for every purpose,” Natalie said.

They kept CaN₂O₆ only as a labeled total-atom inventory, not as the preferred displayed ionic formula. The second return preserved Ca(NO₃)₂ and supplied the atom totals on a separate line.

The carrier departed successfully, making the triumphant noise of a vehicle that had delivered an important organ rather than a card. Jeff saluted it with one oxygen counter.

Kàra remained at the balance. “We've used two different twos today. Calcium's superscript two-plus, and the outside subscript two after nitrate. They happen to have the same magnitude here.”

“But they answer different questions,” Henry said. “One is the charge on one calcium ion. One counts nitrate groups in the formula.”

“And if we add a coefficient in front?” Ástríðr asked.

Ms Luminara placed 2 Ca(NO₃)₂ on a separate inventory card. The leading two multiplied the entire formula-unit count in that representation. It gave two calcium ions, four nitrate ions and, within those nitrates, four nitrogen and twelve oxygen atoms. It did not turn the compound into a new substance or put a charge of plus two on the whole card.

Natalie copied all three roles in a vertical list: superscript charge, subscript count inside a formula, coefficient multiplying the entire specified entity. She added a warning not to trust the fact that the same numeral happened to appear.

The sign room sent back a small thank-you note with its final panel on the reverse. Natalie checked the nitrate groups before turning it over to read the message. Jackson kept the returned card: they now knew what had actually reached the other room.

Jackson placed it beside the ammonium carbonate panel. “Now the next room gets the formula and the meaning.”

“An ambitious delivery,” Ms Luminara said, and the little carrier honked.

## 14. The Smallest Fair Ledger

By the time the aluminium and oxide tiles arrived, the workshop had become confident. Confidence took the form of a jaunty tune from the printer and a banner announcing ALL RATIOS WELCOME.

Henry took down the banner.

“Some ratios are welcome to be examined,” he said. “That's different.”

The supplied ions were Al³⁺ and O²⁻. The class was asked for the simplest neutral ionic formula from those identities. Nobody needed to derive the charges from a periodic table, and Ms Luminara reminded them that a stated ion charge should not be replaced by a half-remembered prediction.

Ástríðr began with one of each. Plus three and minus two left plus one. She added another oxide, giving plus three and minus four, a total of minus one. She looked at the balance with affection rather than irritation.

“It's hovering around zero without landing.”

“Because we're changing whole-ion counts,” Kàra said. “We can't shave a little charge off the corner of the oxide tile.”

Jeff proposed a second aluminium. With two aluminium ions and two oxide ions, the total became plus two. The sequence could have turned into random adjustment. Natalie stopped to list totals instead: multiples of three for positive charge, multiples of two for negative-charge magnitude.

Three, six, nine. Two, four, six.

“Six is the first shared magnitude,” she said. “Two aluminium ions give plus six. Three oxide ions give minus six.”

The balance displayed zero when the corresponding tiles were placed on it. The formula was Al₂O₃.

Henry checked the language. “Two and three are counts of ions in the ratio. The individual charges remain plus three and minus two. The subscripts don't mean aluminium has charge plus two or oxide has charge minus three.”

The printer hummed thoughtfully, then produced Al₄O₆.

“That balances too,” Jeff said.

“It does,” Ms Luminara replied. “What else did the request ask?”

Jeff reread simplest. Four aluminium ions and six oxide ions doubled every whole-ion count in the two-to-three ratio. The same composition could be represented by the smaller whole-number ratio. For the conventional ionic formula in this task, Al₂O₃ was the appropriate result.

Jackson asked whether the machine should therefore divide every pair of even subscripts by two. The question made Natalie uneasy before she could identify why. The rule sounded temptingly mechanical.

Ms Luminara brought out a second pair of tiles: Ca²⁺ and O²⁻. One of each balanced directly. If a crossing shortcut produced Ca₂O₂ from those monatomic ions, its counts could be reduced to the one-to-one ratio CaO.

“Check the intact ion identities, total charge and simplest ratio of ion copies,” she said. “Don't turn the appearance of even digits into a universal permission to rewrite formulas.”

Kàra pointed to ammonium carbonate. The outside two repeated ammonium. The internal four belonged to the ammonium ion. Dividing that four while simplifying an unrelated ratio would alter the ion itself.

“And carbonate's three stays carbonate's three,” Ástríðr said. “It isn't a loose number waiting for arithmetic.”

The workshop opened a symbolic ledger. It represented the count of Al³⁺ ions by a positive whole number a and the count of O²⁻ ions by b. Neutrality required 3a − 2b = 0. Natalie wrote the smallest solution a = 2, b = 3, then larger solutions that were multiples of it.

Henry appreciated the equation because its letters had stated jobs. It did not rely on diagonal arrows being memorized. Someone could use the least common multiple, reason with tiles or solve the simple relation. The method mattered less than preserving the constraints.

Ástríðr asked whether the zero proved they could make the compound by putting those numbers of particles in a bag. Ms Luminara said no. Their accounting was not a synthesis instruction. Charge balance did not establish the conditions of formation, the structure, the energetic favorability of an imagined process or every possible substance's existence.

“So our successful result answers the question we were given,” Ástríðr said. “It doesn't answer all the questions we can ask afterward.”

Natalie drew a final line beneath Al₂O₃, then closed the ledger. Ástríðr returned the unused tiles to their pockets. The printer's jaunty tune resumed, and for a few moments nobody minded it.

The usher printed a new workshop banner: WHOLE IONS, ZERO NET CHARGE, SIMPLEST ION RATIO. Jackson clipped their successful examples to an outgoing packet for the sign room. On its cover, above the reduction instruction, he wrote FORMULAS. The workshop's purpose seemed too obvious to repeat.

Jeff read the banner from the doorway, then looked at the packet. “That will help until somebody takes it into the molecular gallery.”

The printer stopped humming.

Jackson looked toward a connecting conveyor that they had not inspected. The outgoing packet had reached a junction. Above it, two arrows pointed toward the sign room and the molecular gallery. A red light traveled along the second branch, carrying a card with N₂O₄ printed in careful type.

“I didn't label the kind of formula,” he said. He reached the junction after the packet had passed.

A distant machine replied with an efficient chime. FORMULA SIMPLIFIED.

Henry closed his eyes for one second.

Ms Luminara picked up the workshop banner and added a line beneath it: FOR THIS IONIC FORMULA TASK.

“An excellent place to have put that earlier,” she said. “Now let's recover the card.”

## 15. The Formula That Must Not Shrink

The molecular gallery occupied a quieter wing. Instead of an endless repeating hall, its displays showed distinct molecular entities, with labels explaining what each representation included and what it omitted. The conveyor had delivered the N₂O₄ card to a machine called the Universal Simplifier.

A sign above it said: WHY USE SIX ATOMS WHEN THREE WILL DO?

Jeff took one look and unplugged the sign's decorative lamp. “That's not helping.”

The input record was still intact. It identified N₂O₄ as the molecular formula being represented: two nitrogen atoms and four oxygen atoms in one molecule. The output card read NO₂. The machine had applied the ionic workshop's ratio-reduction habit to actual molecular atom counts. Jackson retrieved his packet from its intake slot and circled his own heading. FORMULAS had been wide enough to admit the wrong task.

Ástríðr held the original model beside the changed card. “The card has half the atoms the model is meant to describe.”

“The elemental ratio is equivalent,” the machine said.

“That wasn't the requested information,” Henry replied.

Ms Luminara put two labels on the bench: MOLECULAR FORMULA and EMPIRICAL FORMULA. She supplied their jobs. A molecular formula gave the actual numbers of atoms of each element in one molecule. An empirical formula gave the simplest whole-number ratio of the elements. Those descriptions could coincide for some substances and differ for others.

For N₂O₄, the simplest N:O ratio was one to two, so NO₂ could serve as its empirical formula. That did not authorize replacing the stated molecular formula. As a molecular formula, NO₂ described a different atom count per molecule.

Natalie drew one box around two N labels and four O labels, then another around one N and two O labels. “Same ratio across the element counts. Different specified entity.”

Jackson asked the machine what downstream displays used the output. It listed a name panel, an atom-count counter and an entry that summarized the molecule's composition. He stopped the outgoing conveyor and put the three unfinished cards face down. They could correct the source record before any of those rooms treated it as settled.

“So a harmless-looking simplification could change all three,” he said. “It would be wrong to call this just a formatting preference.”

The machine offered to preserve the original molecular formula in a hidden note. Ástríðr rejected that solution. A visitor should not need to discover a hidden note to learn that the main label had silently changed the entity.

Kàra wondered whether the lesson meant molecular formulas could never be expressed as ratios. Ms Luminara said they could, when that was the question. The mistake was treating two legitimate descriptions as interchangeable without preserving what information had been discarded.

“A ratio can be calculated from the molecular formula,” she said. “The ratio alone generally doesn't restore the original molecule's atom counts.”

Natalie turned both molecular cards face down and left only 1:2 visible. She had just been looking at N₂O₄, so that was the answer her mind supplied. Then she pushed the cards into different positions.

“If I hadn't watched you turn them over,” Jeff said, “that ratio wouldn't tell me which one you meant.”

“No,” Natalie said. “I was remembering the answer from before we hid it. I wasn't recovering it from the ratio.”

She turned the cards back. Both fitted. From the stated N₂O₄ molecular formula she could calculate one-to-two, but the return journey needed additional information. The machine's hidden note contained precisely the information its main label had thrown away.

The Universal Simplifier asked whether it should be shut down permanently. Henry hesitated. It could still calculate empirical ratios when explicitly asked. Once again, deleting a machine was less useful than narrowing its job.

They renamed its controls. One button read PRESERVE MOLECULAR COUNTS. Another read CALCULATE EMPIRICAL RATIO, KEEP ORIGINAL. The ionic-formula procedure remained in the workshop, with intact-ion identities visible before reduction. Jackson wrote a fresh cover naming that task and kept the first cover for their archive. Henry checked the new heading against the instructions before they sent the packet again. No mode activated merely because two digits shared a factor.

Jeff sent the same input through both molecular-gallery controls. The first preserved N₂O₄. The second returned N₂O₄ as the molecular formula and NO₂ as the empirical formula, each clearly labeled. The original record was not overwritten.

Ástríðr read the atom inventory. Two nitrogen and four oxygen per N₂O₄ molecule remained where it belonged. She then read the empirical ratio aloud without calling it a three-atom piece taken out of that molecule.

The usher, who had followed with a basket of ribbons, asked whether the N₂O₄ display could be outlined as one molecule. Ms Luminara confirmed that the supplied molecular identity supported that boundary. It was a different situation from drawing a permanent NaCl pair inside the ordinary ionic solid.

Kàra stood between the galleries, looking back toward the lattice hall. “So neither 'always draw an outline' nor 'never draw an outline' survives the walk.”

“The outline has to answer to the structure and the question,” Natalie said.

She was tired enough to appreciate the simplicity of that sentence. The simplicity had been earned by keeping the differences, not by discarding them.

A message arrived from the main hall: the dance rehearsal could resume as soon as the workshop certified its remaining sample cards. Jackson checked the list and found two that would make the new reduction rule sweat: lithium hydride and sodium peroxide, each accompanied by supplied ion identities.

“Do we have to cover every exception?” Jeff asked.

“No,” Ms Luminara said. “But these two test whether our rule preserves what it says it preserves. We can inspect them without pretending to predict all chemistry.”

The Universal Simplifier's lamp lit once more, now above a much smaller sign: PLEASE STATE WHAT YOU WANT SIMPLIFIED.

Ástríðr gave it an approving nod. “That is a less glamorous job title.”

“More employable,” Henry said.

## 16. Two Cards That Refused the Shortcut

The final sample cards were stored in a shallow drawer marked KNOWN IDENTITIES PROVIDED. Ms Luminara placed that heading on the table with the cards. She did not want a test of their formula method to become a contest in guessing unfamiliar chemistry from a few symbols.

The first card identified lithium hydride using Li⁺ and H⁻ for the introductory ionic description. The second identified sodium peroxide using Na⁺ and the intact peroxide ion O₂²⁻.

Henry read the minus sign on hydrogen twice. “The card supplies hydride, H⁻. We shouldn't replace it with H⁺ because we've seen that more often.”

“Precisely,” Ms Luminara said. “A familiar example isn't permission to overwrite the stated identity.”

Kàra pictured her earlier ledger. A negative ion had more electrons than the corresponding neutral-atom electron count, not more negative protons or a different element. They did not need a new electron-configuration tour to respect the charge provided here.

One Li⁺ and one H⁻ gave a neutral total and the formula LiH. The workshop accepted it. The species gallery initially hesitated at hydrogen's identity, then consulted the supplied information instead of substituting a general shortcut.

Ástríðr was pleased. “It's asking the evidence before the habit.”

The sodium peroxide card was more treacherous. Two sodium ions supplied plus two. One peroxide ion supplied minus two. The neutral formula was Na₂O₂, preserving the two oxygens within the peroxide group.

The Universal Simplifier, watching from the neighboring gallery, began to chime and then stopped itself.

“State the counted units,” it requested.

Jeff held up two sodium tiles and one peroxide tile. “Two monatomic sodium ions. One polyatomic peroxide ion. The simplest whole-ion ratio is two to one.”

Natalie drew a bracket under the O₂ on the tile. Its two was internal to the ion identity. Reducing the atom subscripts of Na₂O₂ to NaO would not preserve the supplied peroxide ion in their ionic formula task.

“That is exactly where 'reduce every ionic formula's visible numbers' would fail,” she said. “Our better rule says reduce counts of intact ions when the whole-ion ratio has a common factor.”

To make the distinction visible, Henry built a deliberately doubled accounting set: four Na⁺ ions and two O₂²⁻ ions. The whole-ion counts four and two shared a factor of two. Reducing those counts returned two sodium ions and one peroxide ion, still Na₂O₂. It did not cut the peroxide tile in half.

Kàra compared the earlier Ca²⁺/O²⁻ example. There the given negative ion had been monatomic oxide. One calcium ion and one oxide ion gave CaO. Here the given negative ion was peroxide, O₂²⁻. The similarity of a letter O could not erase the difference between the species.

“Same element symbol inside different ion identities,” she said. “Different group and charge information.”

Ms Luminara kept the scope narrow. These were supplied identity checks, not a rule for predicting every hydride or oxygen-containing material, and not instructions for handling either substance. They would not infer properties or safe behavior from a charge-balanced formula.

The usher carefully copied the distinction into the workshop's reference drawer. Henry noticed that its text-only export rendered peroxide as O2−2, which could be misread. They changed the accessible text to O₂²⁻ alongside the unambiguous plain-text form O2^2-. The caret had a defined role in that fallback representation: it introduced the charge, while the plain 2 immediately after O counted oxygen atoms.

Ástríðr read the export aloud. “Two oxygen atoms in one ion, net charge minus two.”

The words were longer than the formula and useful for exactly that reason. They exposed whether the person or program had interpreted the two different twos correctly.

Jeff asked if the class had finally found a rule without exceptions. Ms Luminara shook her head, smiling before he could mistake that for a demand to keep searching forever.

“We have a well-scoped method for the tasks in front of us. Preserve the specified ions; balance their total charges; express the simplest whole-ion ratio in suitable notation. We also know what this method doesn't establish. That is enough to use it responsibly.”

Natalie felt an unexpected release in the word enough. She had spent much of the afternoon watching simple explanations become more qualified. Now the qualifications were not pulling the finish line away. They were marking the finish line of this particular task.

The final card passed through the gallery, the workshop and the sign room. It returned with the ionic identity, the intact peroxide group, the neutral formula and the correct whole-ion ratio still aligned. The class had not demonstrated that every possible program input was safe. They had tested a purposeful set that targeted the mistakes they had actually found.

Jackson put the cards in order of the failures they challenged: favorite partner, bond label as complete category, lost group contents, mistaken symbol role, universal reduction. He left a space at the end for future problems.

“That's not an invitation to invent more work today,” he said when Jeff noticed the gap. “It's a place to put a problem if one appears.”

The usher closed the drawer gently. “The sample cards are ready.”

Outside, the hall's lights waited in their repeating positions. For the first time since arrival, the class could hear music warming up without an error bell cutting through it.

Ms Luminara straightened her hat. “Then we may have earned a rehearsal with people in it.”

## 17. The Signmakers Disagree

The sign room smelled of ink and toasted dust. Four mechanical signmakers were arguing around a panel so large that it blocked the doorway. Each had tried to make the repaired exhibit easy to understand, and each had emphasized a different part.

The first panel said NaCl = ONE SODIUM + ONE CHLORINE. The second said EVERY SODIUM HAS SIX CHLORIDES. The third said THERE ARE NO MOLECULES. The fourth was an excellent paragraph printed in letters too small to read without standing on a chair.

Natalie squeezed past the edge of the panel and caught a brush as it rolled toward the floor.

“I recognize this meeting,” she said. “I want one sentence that will stop it.”

Jeff took the folded invitation from his pocket and held it out. Ms Luminara passed it to Natalie. “Try reading their signs as someone arriving with this.”

Natalie put the brush down. She knew what all four panels were trying to say because she had helped do the work. A visitor would not. She asked each signmaker what it was trying to preserve. The first wanted the one-to-one composition. The second wanted the ordinary interior nearest-neighbor pattern. The third wanted to prevent isolated NaCl couples. The fourth wanted to include every qualification they had discussed.

“Those are useful aims,” Natalie said. “The problem is what a visitor can infer from the sentences as written.”

Henry repaired sodium and chlorine in the first panel to sodium ions and chloride ions in this solid. Neutral atoms were not interchangeable with the labeled ionic species. He then removed the plus sign between the words, which looked like a reaction statement even though it was meant as an inventory separator.

Ástríðr revised the second panel to say that an interior sodium ion in the ordinary rock-salt structure had six nearest chloride neighbors. The possessive has was not forbidden, but the word neighbors needed to be present so nobody mistook it for exclusive ownership. She added the reciprocal local statement for chloride and sodium in a smaller explanatory line.

Kàra took the third panel. “There are no molecules where?”

The signmaker waved at the entire exhibit, including the molecular gallery.

“Then we have replaced one error with another,” Kàra said. “We need the ordinary solid NaCl boundary.”

She wrote that its crystal was an extended ionic structure, not separate two-ion NaCl molecules. She did not claim that no meaningful NaCl molecular species could exist under any conditions in all of chemistry. The afternoon had been about an identified ordinary solid.

The fourth signmaker refused to shorten its paragraph. It feared that any shorter version would become false. Jeff asked it to read the text while standing at the entrance, where visitors would first see the panel.

The signmaker could not.

“Then the accurate paragraph needs a different place,” Jeff said. “A short sign can point to it without pretending to contain everything.”

They made a large main caption, a nearby explanation and optional deeper notes. The main caption stated the composition and the extended structure. The nearby explanation separated six nearest neighbors from the one-to-one ratio. The deeper note described the limits of the ionic model and the fact that nearest-neighbor lines were not a complete electrostatic calculation.

Jackson followed the route a new visitor would take. He entered through the gate, encountered the simple caption, moved to the local view and then saw the comparison with species in the next gallery. The order mattered. If the ammonium tile appeared before its whole-ion label, a visitor might treat its ribbon as another forbidden pair bow. If the ribbon lesson appeared without the molecule examples, they might assume all discrete grouping was mistaken.

“The sequence is part of what the exhibit says,” Jackson observed.

Ms Luminara agreed, while reminding them that a reader could enter at different points. Every room still needed enough context to stand on its own. The route could support understanding without becoming a compulsory march.

Ástríðr tried the exhibit with all decorative colors removed. The plus and minus signs remained legible. Element symbols retained their case. Captions identified who or what each label described. A few pale arrows disappeared; she darkened them and added textual descriptions of their function.

Henry checked a line reading 2 NH₄⁺. The leading coefficient counted two ammonium ions. The internal four counted hydrogens per ammonium. The superscript plus was the charge per ion. Together the two ions contributed net charge plus two, but the expression was not to be printed as a new bonded molecule.

Natalie listened as Jeff read it without looking at the colors. “Two ammonium ions, each with one nitrogen and four hydrogens, each charge plus one.”

“That works,” Henry said.

The usher asked whether the repaired panels should carry a seal saying SCIENTIFICALLY COMPLETE. Jackson answered immediately.

“No. Say what was checked.”

They wrote a modest note: the exhibit's claims concerned the specified models and examples; the visible comparisons had been checked for charge, identity, counting scope and notation. It did not certify every conceivable chemical input, every structure or every learning outcome.

The signmakers disliked the lack of a gold seal. Ástríðr gave them a decorative star labeled DECORATIVE STAR. That seemed to satisfy everyone.

Natalie stepped out of the room and approached its doorway again with the invitation in her hand. The first caption no longer required her to remember their conversation. She folded the invitation away and tried the nearby explanation. Only then did she bring the brush back inside.

The first music cue sounded from the hall. This time the sign room did not send a contradictory update after it.

Jeff opened the door with a small flourish. “All inspection visitors may enter, with or without a partner.”

Kàra checked the balcony options on the route board. The ordinary stairs and bench remained marked.

“Then I'm going back up,” she said. “I want to see the whole sequence.”

## 18. The Dance Without Pair Assignments

The Salt Crystal Dance began with a welcome instead of an instruction to find a partner. The brass usher stood beside the archway, its ribbons stored in a basket rather than wound around ion positions.

“This exhibit represents ordinary solid sodium chloride,” it said. “Its formula records a one-to-one ratio of sodium ions to chloride ions. Its structure is extended. Please choose a view.”

Natalie entered at walking speed. She could still remember the first version so clearly that she almost expected the gate to reject her for standing alone. Nothing happened. The lack of a false requirement felt surprisingly spacious.

The class divided by preference. Kàra and Jeff took the stairs to the balcony. Henry stayed near the local-neighborhood display. Ástríðr chose the gallery entrance, where she could hear how the next room was introduced. Jackson stood by the controls, with a clear view of both the display and its status panel. Natalie sat halfway down the main walkway, her notebook closed.

Ms Luminara remained at the gate long enough to count everyone, then let the rehearsal proceed without narrating each success.

The first cue illuminated the repeating arrangement. Na⁺ and Cl⁻ labels alternated along the represented directions. The display did not place little ovals around sodium–chloride pairs. Its introductory line described a bulk composition, and a separate line identified the ordinary structural model.

From the balcony, Jeff could see the hall's breadth. He had wanted that view since reading the invitation. It was better than the promise on the picture because he now knew what he was looking at and which parts the representation had invented for him.

Kàra rested one hand on the bench. The apparent depth still asked for more attention than she wanted to give it. She looked at the labels across the hall instead. “Above and below,” Kàra said softly. “Still the ones the first floor hid.”

Jeff nodded. The six surrounding positions did not leap into an exclusive molecule with the center. When the selected center moved, some positions appeared in more than one local description. The composition heading stayed NaCl.

Downstairs, Henry watched the display shift to a chloride center. Six nearest sodium neighbors appeared. A caption explained that the count was local coordination information, not the number of sodium ions owned by that chloride. Henry made no correction.

The next cue showed the arrival-bookkeeping animation without the ribbon. The electron arrow was labeled as a simplified account of forming the displayed ions, not a full reaction movie. Natalie watched the same dot move from one account to another. She no longer mistook the final frame for a pair contract. Yet the count change remained easy to follow. The correction had not required making the animation dull.

At the gallery entrance, Ástríðr listened to the three species descriptions. N₂: neutral elemental molecule. CO₂: neutral molecule of a molecular compound. NH₄⁺: polyatomic ion with internal covalent bonding. The separate questions remained separate, and the plus sign was not erased to make ammonium fit a drawer.

The usher brought out a ribbon for the whole-ion display. It explained what the boundary marked and did not tie the ammonium ion permanently to a particular carbonate ion. Ástríðr gave the slightest nod. The prop had recovered a legitimate job.

In the workshop sequence, two ammonium tiles and one carbonate tile produced the neutral ratio and the formula (NH₄)₂CO₃. A second line gave the atom inventory. A note made clear that the tile set represented formula composition rather than an isolated three-ion molecule in the solid.

The nitrate card traveled through the miniature carrier and returned with its parentheses intact. The carrier honked at the correct moment, which was no moment at all from a chemical standpoint and an excellent moment from its own. Natalie laughed, and the usher's head turned as though laughter might be another value it should measure.

“Don't count that,” Ms Luminara murmured to it. “Just allow it.”

The final sequence contrasted Al₂O₃, derived from supplied Al³⁺ and O²⁻ charges, with the molecular formula N₂O₄. The display said why the simplest whole-ion ratio mattered in the first task and why the actual molecular atom counts must remain in the second. Sodium peroxide waited in the optional reference drawer as a check against reducing internal ion subscripts.

Then the informational lights dimmed to a comfortable level and the music became ordinary music. No one had to solve a problem to keep it playing. The chemical ions did not get up and dance; the event belonged to visitors in a fictional exhibit. The display remained a display.

Ástríðr offered Natalie a hand, and Natalie stood for a few steps before deciding she preferred to watch. Jeff came down from the balcony and tapped out a rhythm on the edge of a chair. Henry, unexpectedly, joined him. Kàra stayed near the stair rail, smiling at a syncopation that neither rhythm-maker seemed to have planned.

“This is not an analogy for ionic structure,” it said carefully.

“Correct,” Natalie replied, laughing. “You may enjoy it without assigning it a formula.”

For a while, that was exactly what everyone did.

## 19. What the Old Chart Was Allowed to Keep

When the music ended, the hall did not empty abruptly. The class returned cups, gathered notebooks and found pencils that had rolled beneath chairs. Ms Luminara took the steering wheel back to the bus before the bus developed opinions about being left without it.

Natalie stayed in the chart office with the usher. The original pair chart lay on the workbench beside the repaired records. Its little bows now seemed more hopeful than authoritative.

“May I keep the equal totals?” the usher asked.

“Yes,” Natalie said. “They were right.”

“And the name NaCl?”

“Yes, with its proper job. The formula represents the one-to-one composition here.”

“And the statement that a sodium ion and chloride ion have opposite charges?”

“Yes. What we removed was the claim that each sodium had one uniquely assigned partner and that those exclusive pairs were independent molecules in the ordinary solid.”

The usher made a list of retained information. Natalie was glad it asked that way. A revision could feel like having one's entire contribution discarded, even when most of the useful work remained. The class had spent the afternoon distinguishing facts, representations and inferences. The record of their repair should preserve those distinctions too.

Henry entered carrying the NaCl₆ sign. He had not thrown it away. On its back he had written: Correct local count used as the wrong kind of total.

Kàra followed him with the three-pencil sketch. She laid it beside the sign. “This helped me see the six directions. It shouldn't be displayed alone as if the pencils were literal bonds.”

Jeff contributed the stop-control card from the rehearsal room. He had written a short procedure on it: state a prediction, change one display condition, compare the relevant view, restore the model. It was a way of investigating their fictional representation, not a general protocol for experiments with chemicals.

Ástríðr brought the biscuit tin full of retired ribbons. She divided them into two small bundles: bows once used to invent NaCl couples, and loose outlines now used to identify whole groups in selected displays. Then she removed the dividing card.

“Actually, these are the same ribbons,” she said. “Their meaning came from how we used them. Sorting the physical objects won't preserve that.”

Natalie laughed. It was a perfect correction to the archive she had been preparing. They stored examples of the old and new uses with explanatory captions instead of pretending one spool had been chemically mistaken while another was chemically wise.

Jackson arrived last. He had checked that the sign room, gallery and workshop were all using the current records. The old chart remained only in the archive, labeled as an earlier misleading representation. It would not silently feed the live exhibit again.

“Otherwise,” he said, “we could come back next week and find the old assumption reintroduced by the one room we forgot.”

The usher placed a small card beside its operating instructions: If an input lacks the information needed for the requested classification, ask for the missing information rather than guessing from a slogan.

Henry approved. A formula alone did not always prove the structure or bonding family the program needed. Their sample cards often supplied identities deliberately. The absence of a metal was not a universal molecular-compound test; the presence of a familiar pattern could be useful without becoming proof in every case.

Natalie opened her notebook to the first page. The sentence about a memorable explanation now had several arrows and a biscuit stain. She considered copying it neatly. Instead she made a clean summary on a new page and kept the worked pages behind it.

“What changed most?” Ástríðr asked.

Natalie looked at the two versions of the chart. “I stopped expecting one kind of answer to do every job. Arrival bookkeeping, present interactions, species identity, local neighborhood, overall ratio. Each one can be clear. The confusion came from using one in place of another.”

Kàra leaned against the doorframe. “For me, it was the idea that adding detail always means abandoning the simple model. We kept the one-to-one ratio. We just stopped drawing it as a marriage license.”

Jeff nodded. “I wanted the balcony because I thought a wider view would show the answer. It helped, but only after we knew what question the view could answer.”

Henry admitted that he had wanted to delete several faulty machines rather than repair their job descriptions. Jackson laid down the old packet cover headed FORMULAS beside them.

“I was so careful about what came out of the old envelopes,” he said, “and then I sent out that.”

Henry laid the corrected packet beside its old cover. The replacement heading named the ionic task and kept the supplied ion identities with it. “That's why I read it before you sent it the second time.”

“And why I didn't ask you to destroy the simplifier,” Jackson said.

Their first instincts had pulled in opposite directions. The afternoon's work bore marks from both of them.

Ms Luminara returned from the bus and listened from the doorway. She did not convert their reflections into a final score. The corrected exhibit was evidence of a set of distinctions they had worked through with support. It was not proof that every person would independently answer every new chemistry question tomorrow.

“Keep the tools,” she said. “You don't have to carry the entire afternoon in working memory.”

The usher offered one biscuit and one napkin to each person. “One-to-one ratio,” it announced, then added, “This distribution does not establish the structure of sodium chloride.”

“Now you're showing off,” Ástríðr said affectionately.

Natalie took a final look through the office window. The hall's pattern remained larger than the portion she could see. That no longer felt like the model withholding its ending. A finite view could be useful without containing the whole thing.

She closed the notebook, keeping the question card on top.

## 20. The Useful Souvenir

At the bus door, Natalie returned the invitation to Jeff. He checked it one last time. The line about permanent couples had been replaced. Visitors could enter alone, together, quietly, by the stairs, through the still view or with the explanation already open.

“That's a much better dance invitation,” he said.

The usher asked whether they wanted souvenirs. It offered the retired NaCl₆ sign, a ribbon spool and a tiny model of the miniature delivery carrier, which immediately began making miniature unnecessary engine noises.

“No ions, no samples and none of the floor,” Ms Luminara said before the offer grew more ambitious.

Henry chose the three main classification questions, with the added reminder to identify the whole entity being described. Jackson chose the corrected dependency map, which showed what the gallery supplied to the workshop and which counts the sign room used. Jeff kept his balcony sketch and the small card explaining the six directions.

Ástríðr took a short ribbon with two attached labels. One read WHAT THIS OUTLINE MEANS. The other read WHAT IT DOESN'T MEAN. She wound it around her pencil case, where its immediate job was simply to keep the case closed.

Kàra chose a still view of the ordinary rock-salt arrangement. Its caption distinguished local nearest neighbors from bulk composition, and the text description remained on the back. She did not choose the dramatic perspective looking down through the transparent lift. She had seen what she wanted to see without needing to turn the afternoon into a contest with that image.

Natalie took a copy of the old and new pair captions. She wanted the small difference in wording beside the large difference in meaning.

The usher looked at her selection. “Why keep the wrong one?”

“So I can remember the assumption we changed,” Natalie said. “And so I don't tell myself the right explanation was obvious from the start.”

Ms Luminara boarded last. Before closing the door, she turned the invitation over and wrote beneath its original date: Arrival stories, present interactions and classification labels are different tools. Use each for its own job.

The usher read the sentence slowly. Then it untied the last bow from the archway. The ribbon fell in a loose curve, no longer connecting two labels that the structure had never assigned to one another.

The bus pulled away through the enlarging gallery in reverse. The enormous structural representation became an image beside a grain, then the grain became one among many on dark paper. Natalie watched the change in scale without imagining the ions had vanished. They had moved outside the resolution of the present picture.

“That view doesn't stop being real because we can't see its details from here,” Jeff said.

“And our colored balls don't become literal because they helped us think,” Henry added.

Ms Luminara gave them both an approving glance in the mirror. “Two excellent things to remember on the way home.”

The classroom returned with a small click from the radiator. The cube of light on the desk had collapsed into an ordinary envelope. Nobody had missed lunch; the bus kept its own eccentric account of time.

Natalie opened her notebook while the others put away their souvenirs. She laid the old pair caption against the new one, then turned to the crossed-out sixteen and the tick she had moved beside charge. Those were small marks. They recalled moments when she had understood a warning and still needed help applying it.

She began a clean summary, stopped after two lines and tucked the captions into the page. The worked examples were already there. She wanted something she could use when the next unfamiliar formula arrived.

Ástríðr sat on the edge of a nearby desk. “What would you ask the next time a formula feels confusing?”

Natalie considered the calcium nitrate slip, the ammonium ribbon and the peroxide tile. “What is the whole thing I'm describing? What does each number do? Which identity am I supposed to preserve?”

Kàra, who had been drawing the three axes in the margin of her own page, added, “And am I counting a neighborhood, a ratio or an actual entity?”

Jackson looked toward the bus. “Then what will the next person do with my answer?”

Ms Luminara did not turn their questions into a chant. She wrote them on separate cards and left them where anyone could use them. A good tool did not have to become another piece of compulsory memory performance.

Henry found the miniature carrier under a seat. It had followed them despite not being selected. In its paper tray lay a blank name card beside the correctly written formula Ca(NO₃)₂.

“It says the formula is ready,” Henry reported. “The name panel wants to know which naming rules it should use.”

Jeff grinned. “Please tell me there isn't a universal naming machine.”

Ms Luminara's expression was sufficiently innocent to be suspicious.

“One adventure at a time,” she said.

Natalie closed her notebook. She could still picture the city extending past every window, with no permanent couples hidden between its lines. More important, she could say why the first picture failed and what the better one preserved. The wonder had survived the correction.

On the desk, the envelope's final ribbon loosened by itself. Ástríðr caught it before it fell and tucked it into her pencil case.

“Useful,” she said, “when you know what it's for.”
