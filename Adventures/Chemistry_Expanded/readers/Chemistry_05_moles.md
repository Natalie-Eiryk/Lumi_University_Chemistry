# Ms Luminara and the Mole Counting Station

Story ID: luminara.adventure.005  
Content version: 2.2.0-pass3  
Status: final-reviewed private-study draft; not a course submission or canonization claim

The bus, station, machinery, displays, dialogue, and investigations are fictional teaching models; all isotope numbers explicitly marked artificial are invented, and no real gas handling, isotope separation, or nuclear construction takes place.

## 1. The cart that could not arrive

The delivery cart had passed the same lamppost seventeen times when the station decided to ask for help. Its wheels made four brisk clicks at the joint in the rail, paused at a barrier, and rolled back around the square. A parcel sat on the cart beneath a beautifully printed label. The label said ONE MOLE. It had a border, an embossed seal, and no other useful information.

Above the barrier, a sign asked HOW MANY? Across the square, another asked HOW HEAVY? Each time the cart approached one sign, a mechanical finger pointed toward the other. Each time it returned, the first finger repeated its instruction. Rain fell on neither warehouse. It fell only into the space between them, where the cart had begun to collect a small, rather discouraged puddle.

The bus landed in that puddle.

Its suspension sighed. Its dashboard announced NO CARGO: OVERLOADED WITH QUESTIONS.

Ms Luminara looked at the message over the top of her glasses.

Ms Luminara: “A persistent maintenance issue. Seatbelts may now become less philosophical and more unbuckled.”

Jeff was already at the window. Beyond the square, bridges rose between warehouses whose far walls disappeared into a pearly distance. Conveyors carried tickets, model containers, and occasionally entire punctuation marks. One comma swung past in a sling. On the roof of the counting office stood a display so tall that a cloud had caught halfway up it.

Jeff: “I would like to go up there.”

Kàra turned toward him.

Jeff: “On a staircase with sides. I can have both wishes.”

Ms Luminara parked beside a bench with room for all of them and set a thermos on its arm. The bench remained attached to the bus by a short covered walkway. Anyone could stay there and watch the displays; nothing in the station required being physically carried into a difficult idea. A brass pause handle stood beside the door, within reach of the shortest and tallest visitor alike.

Natalie opened her notebook to a page headed COUNTING. Beneath it she had written, in a smaller hand, “I understand a dozen.” She left the rest blank. That seemed a more honest beginning than copying a long number whose usefulness she had not yet felt.

Henry stepped down and read the parcel label twice.

Henry: “One mole of what?”

The cart rang a bell. The barrier lifted halfway, then stopped.

Ástríðr: “It liked the question more than the answer it didn't get.”

Jackson followed the rail past the two pointing fingers. A route card on the barrier recorded each refusal: counting office awaiting entity; weighing office awaiting substance; dispatch awaiting both. Beneath those lines an automatic summary said DELAY CAUSED BY EXCESSIVE DETAIL.

Jackson: “The detail is what it's missing. The summary has blamed the thing that would let the cart move.”

He lifted the summary out of its holder and turned it over. The back was blank, which improved it considerably.

Ms Luminara: “The station normally connects three questions: what are we counting, how much of it, and what mass corresponds to that amount? Someone has shortened its receipts until the answers no longer know which questions they belong to.”

Kàra inspected the parcel through a transparent inspection panel. Inside was a shifting patch of light, beautiful and completely unhelpful. She tried counting bright spots. As soon as she reached nine, the display changed scale and the nine became a field of thousands.

Kàra: “If that's supposed to tell us what's in there, it has chosen a hostile method.”

Ms Luminara: “That picture is only a placeholder. We mustn't identify a substance by a decorative glow.”

Natalie drew a rectangle around ONE MOLE. She nearly wrote “unknown mass,” then added “unknown entity” above it. The second absence came first. A mass could not identify the intended counting unit by itself, and an amount could not supply the identity either.

The cart tried to depart. Jeff placed a small stop block on the rail before it reached the puddle again. Its motor clicked off. Nothing exploded. The practical difference between being trapped in a loop and standing still with a question was surprisingly large.

Jeff: “We can stop moving while we work out where moving would help.”

Ms Luminara: “A principle with applications well beyond railways.”

She unfolded the original dispatch slip. Its faded ink could still be read under a lamp: OXYGEN, MOLECULAR, O₂. The abbreviation had not been wrong. The station's shortened copy had discarded it.

Henry copied O₂ molecules onto the parcel label. The cart's bell rang again, this time with an ordinary, satisfied sound. The counting barrier lifted. The weighing barrier stayed shut.

Ástríðr looked from one to the other.

Ástríðr: “We repaired a real problem, and the whole station hasn't become perfect.”

Ms Luminara: “Useful repairs often have that disappointing courtesy.”

Jackson kept the discarded label. Its bottom edge was cut so close to ONE MOLE that the border had lost a corner. He laid it against the original dispatch slip; the name of the substance lay below that edge. Someone, or something, had decided where a complete statement ended. He clipped both papers to a board headed FIRST VERSION, with the larger slip showing beneath the smaller one.

Natalie added a question beneath her dozen: “A dozen what?” It looked almost too simple beside the enormous rooftop display. Then she watched the cart roll, at last, toward a desk that could receive it. Simplicity had done something the beautiful seal had not.

They followed the cart. The puddle remained behind them, no deeper for having been called a delay.

## 2. A number with room beneath it

The staircase to the counting display had broad landings, handrails, and a notice that read EACH STEP REPRESENTS A STEP, NOT TEN TO ANYTHING. Jeff approved. He had expected the station to make scale into a physical prank. Instead, it offered a small observation gallery beneath the enormous number.

6.02214076 × 10²³

The digits shone steadily. There was no flickering final digit, no ellipsis, no last piece arriving late. Below them, in smaller letters, stood EXACT NUMBER OF SPECIFIED ENTITIES IN ONE MOLE.

Kàra spread her arms as though she could measure the number's width against her own body. The gesture was hopeless enough to be funny, and she laughed at it before anyone else could mistake it for an attempt at calculation.

Kàra: “My imagination has declined the assignment.”

Natalie: “Mine too. I can say a power of ten. I can't picture this many separate things. Those are different abilities.”

Ms Luminara lowered a shutter over most of the digits. It did not alter their value; it simply left the class looking at a manageable part of the room. A tray came out from beneath the display holding twelve plain wooden counters.

Ms Luminara: “Then let's start with a count your eyes can check.”

Ástríðr arranged the counters in three rows of four. Henry rearranged them in two rows of six. The tray still held twelve. Jeff swept them into a little mound, then separated them again because the mound hid one counter behind another.

Jeff: “Looking harder isn't the same as having a better arrangement.”

The desk printed three receipts: twelve counters; one dozen counters; three groups of four counters. Natalie laid them side by side. Different expressions preserved the same count. The counters had not physically changed when a clerk called the collection a dozen.

Ms Luminara: “A mole gives us another fixed relationship between a number of entities and an amount. The SI unit for amount of substance is the mole, symbol mol. One mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ specified entities.”

Ástríðr: “A dozen with an extraordinarily ambitious hat.”

Ms Luminara: “For the counting relationship, yes. But keep the name of the quantity too. A mole is a unit of amount of substance. It isn't a small animal, a secret kind of gram, or a particular container.”

Henry wrote the symbols on a fresh card. N meant the number of the specified entities. n meant amount of substance. N_A meant the Avogadro constant, exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. The relation was N = n × N_A.

He held the card where everyone could see it before setting it in the desk's reference slot. Nobody had to earn the relation by reconstructing the definition unaided.

Kàra looked up at the covered display.

Kàra: “If the constant is exact, is the number on every container exact?”

Ms Luminara: “The conversion is exact. A measurement of a particular sample can still be uncertain. Think of exactly twelve items per dozen. That doesn't make your estimate of how many dozens are in a dark storeroom exact.”

Jackson opened a cupboard below the tray. Inside lay cards for atoms, molecules, ions, electrons, and other specified groups of particles. The station had not made one card called particles and used it everywhere. He took out that imaginary blank card anyway, drawing it on scrap paper.

Jackson: “If we write only particles, the next desk may choose a different kind.”

Henry: “And the number alone won't tell it that it chose differently.”

The gallery floor changed into a shallow model landscape. Twelve large tiles became twelve trays, then twelve shelves of trays. The view zoomed away until the shelves looked like dots. A notice remained visible: REPRESENTATION ONLY. THE DISPLAY DOES NOT SHOW EVERY ENTITY.

Natalie watched until her eyes began to search for an individual shelf they could no longer resolve. It was tempting to feel that the count had become doubtful because the view had become crowded. Yet the uncertainty was in what she could see, not in the arithmetic of the designed model.

Natalie: “I think I was asking the picture to certify the number. But a picture can explain a grouping without containing a visible mark for every member.”

Jeff: “Like the map downstairs doesn't need one full-sized railway on it.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly. The scale bridge is useful when we know which relationships it preserves.”

They tried an exactly half-mole ticket for a counting thought experiment at the desk. The display showed half the defined count, 3.01107038 × 10²³, and asked for the entity. Henry withheld the stamp until Jeff selected O₂ molecules. A quarter-mole ticket would likewise represent a quarter of the count. There was no need to imagine every member before the relation could be used.

Ástríðr asked whether one molecule could be described as an amount smaller than one mole. Ms Luminara said yes: one divided by the Avogadro constant in the appropriate relation. Choosing a large counting unit did not require every collection to contain a whole number of those units. One could have half a dozen biscuits without splitting a biscuit, and half a mole of molecules without splitting molecules.

Kàra let her hands fall to her sides. The number was no smaller. It had stopped demanding that her body be its measuring stick.

At the bottom of the staircase, the waiting O₂ cart gave a single bell. They now knew how to interpret its amount. What remained was to check what its scanner had actually counted.

## 3. The scanner sees twice

The oxygen cart rolled into a low room with glass walls. A red line swept across its model contents, and the counting desk printed a receipt so quickly that the paper curled against Jackson's sleeve.

ONE MOLE O₂ MOLECULES: 1.204428152 × 10²⁴ MOLECULES.

Henry did not touch the calculator. He put the receipt beside the definition they had brought from the gallery.

Henry: “These don't agree.”

Jeff looked through the inspection window. At the available magnification, each O₂ molecule appeared as a pair of joined spheres. The red line brightened once at the first sphere and again at the second. Farther down the track, the scanner added both flashes to a column titled MOLECULES.

Jeff: “It sees two parts and calls each part a whole.”

The desk proposed a repair: DIM RED LIGHT BY HALF.

Kàra: “No. It can be perfectly dim and still be counting the wrong thing.”

She reached toward the setting, then stopped long enough to ask what the image represented. Ms Luminara confirmed that the joined spheres were a simplified molecular model. Real atoms were not billiard balls attached by a visible rod. For today's count, the useful relationship was that each O₂ molecule contained two oxygen atoms.

They replaced the enormous model collection with a window showing three O₂ molecules. Henry put a loop around each pair. Ástríðr placed six smaller markers, one over each atomic position. Jeff made two separate tally rows and ran the scanner slowly enough to hear every click.

Six clicks. Three loops.

No calculation with an exponent was needed to locate the error.

Natalie drew the same picture, then wrote “3 molecules, 6 oxygen atoms.” She had expected the trouble to live in the huge number. Instead, the small picture exposed a category change that the exponent had merely magnified.

Natalie: “If I start with the correct number and change the noun afterward, I've changed the claim. That's what the scanner did.”

Jackson went to the dispatch window. The receiving warehouse had requested one mole of O₂ molecules. Its order was being marked fulfilled when the atom tally reached the molecular target. That would stop the model shipment after only half the requested number of molecules had passed.

Jackson: “The mistake isn't just an untidy receipt. It tells the gate to close early.”

They watched a rehearsal. The gate counted oxygen atoms and shut with half of the model carts still on the departure side. The opposite warehouse displayed an amount twice what it had actually received in molecular terms. Nothing physical had doubled. The report had.

Ástríðr laid one palm on the rail.

Ástríðr: “If I were on the receiving side, I'd have no reason to know which kind of count the sender used. The label needs to travel with the number.”

Henry took a narrow strip of tape and wrote O₂ MOLECULES along it. He stuck it above the scanner's larger loops. A second strip said O ATOMS WITHIN O₂ MOLECULES. The longer phrase made him pause.

Henry: “Within matters. We haven't separated the oxygen atoms from their molecules.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. Counting constituent atoms doesn't claim that free atoms are present as separate species. You're describing composition.”

Kàra slid the small model under the light again. This time the two rows advanced together: one molecular click for each loop, two atomic clicks for its contents. She had not made the counts equal. She had made their relationship explicit.

For one mole of O₂ molecules, the desk now showed one mole of molecules and two moles of oxygen atoms within them. For half a mole of molecules, it showed half a mole of molecules and one mole of constituent oxygen atoms. The chemical subscript supplied the factor of two.

Natalie asked whether they were allowed to cancel “molecules” in a conversion the way they canceled units. Ms Luminara distinguished the formal quantity from its entity label, then kept both visible in the teaching expression: 1 mol O₂ molecules × 2 mol O atoms per 1 mol O₂ molecules = 2 mol O atoms. The labels made the composition relationship inspectable even where the formal SI unit was mol on both sides.

Natalie: “So the units don't do all the thinking for me. I also need the identity of what's being counted.”

Ms Luminara: “They are excellent handrails. They are not a substitute for knowing which bridge you're on.”

They let the full model shipment run through the counting room. The gate stayed open until the correct number of molecular entities had passed. At the room's exit, the cart received a counting acknowledgement for one mole of O₂ molecules. Its second line, no longer hidden, recorded the corresponding two moles of oxygen atoms within those molecules. The weighing destination still waited beyond the bridge.

Jeff remained beside the inspection window after everyone else had begun gathering the papers. Three pairs still floated in the small display. He could see how a machine had arrived at six. Its first observation was not nonsense; its interpretation was wrong.

Jeff: “I'm glad we didn't erase the six. We needed it. We just needed it beside the right question.”

Ms Luminara: “That's the repair.”

Henry pinned both receipts to Jackson's board. One showed a correct tally assigned to the wrong entity. The other preserved two correct tallies with their meanings intact. The difference looked slight on paper. Across the station, an entire gate had learned when to stop.

## 4. What the subscript brings along

On the walkway out of the scanner room, Ástríðr found a tray of model molecules arranged like small sculptures. Some contained two joined spheres, some three, and one had a cluster large enough that she had to turn it to see every part. A sign offered a shortcut: COUNT THE LARGE OBJECTS; IGNORE EVERYTHING INSIDE.

She turned the sign facedown.

Ástríðr: “Ignoring the inside was useful for counting molecules. It won't answer every question somebody asks about them.”

A new parcel waited beside the tray, labeled CO₂. Its destination was the Composition Loft, a room that collected information rather than matter. The request asked for two reports from the same model sample: number of CO₂ molecules and number of oxygen atoms within them. The clerk had left a blank space between the requests, as though the second required a separate sample.

Jackson: “We should be able to answer both without sending the parcel anywhere else.”

Henry picked up one CO₂ model. It contained one carbon position and two oxygen positions. He placed it beside a second identical model. Natalie drew the pair in her notebook: two molecules, two carbon atoms, four oxygen atoms, six atoms altogether. She put brackets around each molecule, then a larger bracket around the collection.

The brackets helped, but they also tempted her into treating the larger bracket as another molecule. She crossed out a hurried label before it became part of her explanation.

Natalie: “A group I draw for convenience isn't automatically a chemical entity. This outside bracket is just my collection boundary.”

Ms Luminara: “Good distinction. The formula describes the composition of each molecule here. Your pencil can collect two molecules without bonding them into a new one.”

Kàra walked around the tray. She was thinking about multiplication as something physical: every additional molecule brought the same internal composition with it. Nothing needed to be pulled apart to keep the account. The two oxygen atoms per molecule were a repeated relationship, unlike the sample size, which could change.

Kàra: “The subscript is a passenger count built into this kind of vehicle. The number of vehicles can vary.”

Henry: “As long as we remember atoms aren't passengers that can step out without a chemical change.”

Kàra: “Yes. That's where my bus metaphor stops.”

Ms Luminara smiled at the actual bus visible through the window. Its own metaphors stopped rather less predictably, but that was a matter for the transport department.

The desk accepted a model amount of two moles of CO₂ molecules. Ástríðr predicted two moles of carbon atoms and four moles of oxygen atoms within them. Jeff added the amounts of atoms to obtain six moles of atoms in total. He almost called that six moles of CO₂, then caught the extra name as it reached the paper.

Jeff: “I can add the constituent atom counts if the total question is about atoms. I can't rename the result as molecules.”

They sent the report to the Composition Loft. Three separate windows opened: molecular entities; constituent atoms by element; all constituent atoms. The parcel itself stayed on the tray. The station had gained three descriptions without gaining material.

A smaller sign beneath the windows asked whether the oxygen atoms were free to leave through the atomic exit. Henry blocked the request. The report concerned atoms within molecules. A count was not permission to change their chemical state, and the exercise did not describe a reaction that would separate them.

Natalie leaned against the railing and considered how many times a classroom calculation could skip that sentence. One wrote a factor of two, canceled a label, and arrived at an answer. It was easy for the procedure to make the atoms seem as though they had been poured into a different box.

Natalie: “I want my notebook to keep the scene, not just the factor. The same sample can be described at two levels.”

Jackson: “And the receiving desk needs to know which level controls its next action.”

Ms Luminara: “The formula connects those levels. In a molecular formula, the subscripts tell you the numbers of constituent atoms of each element per molecule; an unwritten subscript means one. Scaling up preserves that relationship.”

Jeff asked what happened if there were two different molecular species in one sample. Ms Luminara said they would need more information about the mixture before a single formula could stand for everything. Today's sealed display contained the one specified species; that assumption belonged on the card rather than in their imaginations.

Ástríðr wrote PURE CO₂ MODEL beneath the parcel name. Then she added MODEL AMOUNT SUPPLIED. Their counts followed from what the exercise stipulated, not from a photograph proving purity or a scale proving composition.

That distinction quieted the room for a moment. The station had been offering authoritative-looking pictures. The class was learning to ask what information the pictures were entitled to provide.

Henry returned the models to the tray without pulling any apart. Kàra restored the shortcut sign with a new final line: FOR MOLECULE COUNT ONLY. A useful instruction had become trustworthy by becoming narrower.

From the far side of the bridge came the sound of two scale pans descending by different amounts. The cart, now correctly labeled and counted, had reached the warehouse called HOW HEAVY. It was about to discover that getting the number right did not settle the mass.

## 5. Two containers, one amount

The weighing warehouse smelled of polished wood, warm metal, and the faint paper dust of old receipts. Its balances stood on heavy benches. Their display windows were surrounded by ornate lettering that made every measurement look like a royal announcement.

On the nearest bench sat two identical model containers. One was labeled ONE MOLE NEON ATOMS. The other said ONE MOLE O₂ MOLECULES. Their empty-container masses had already been allowed for; the screens displayed the modeled masses of the contents alone.

Neon: approximately 20.18 g.

O₂: approximately 32.00 g.

The ceiling flashed SAME AMOUNT MUST MEAN SAME MASS. A mechanism beneath the neon container began feeding lead-colored imitation weights into a side tray to make the scales agree.

Jeff pressed the pause handle before the first imitation weight could fall.

Jeff: “If it changes the contents to make a slogan true, the scale isn't the problem.”

Ástríðr took two boxes from a demonstration shelf. One held a dozen light paper balls. The other held a dozen smooth stones. She counted both, shut the lids, and put them on the bench.

Ástríðr: “A dozen balloons and a dozen bowling balls would also like a word, but I found these closer.”

Kàra lifted the boxes in turn. Equal count did not imply equal mass. The familiar example was so immediate that she wondered why the mole version had momentarily seemed different.

Kàra: “The new word made me expect a new rule. But multiplying both kinds by the same count won't make the individual masses match.”

Natalie read the data cards clipped to the containers. The supplied approximate molar mass for neon was 20.18 g/mol. For O₂ it was 32.00 g/mol. Each card identified the substance and the kind of entity to which the amount referred.

Natalie: “Mass per mole. Like a rate between two quantities, except I need the correct material.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly. Molar mass, M, connects mass m and amount n for the specified substance: m = n × M.”

Henry wrote the neon relation in full: 1 mol neon atoms × 20.18 g per mol neon atoms gives approximately 20.18 g. Beside it he wrote the corresponding oxygen relation. The mol unit canceled in each calculation, leaving grams. The values were different because the supplied molar masses were different.

The ceiling sign proposed another explanation: OXYGEN HAS TWO ATOMS, THEREFORE TWICE AS HEAVY AS NEON.

Jackson groaned.

Jackson: “It has replaced one unsupported shortcut with another.”

Henry compared the numbers. Thirty-two was not twice 20.18. Counting how many atoms a molecule contained did not supply the masses of those atoms. Even a correct composition count needed substance-specific information before it became a mass calculation.

Ms Luminara placed a card reading approximately 16.00 g per mole of O atoms beside the O₂ model. Each mole of O₂ molecules contained two moles of O atoms. Under the supplied ordinary-composition approximation, that gave approximately 32.00 g per mole of O₂ molecules. The factor of two compared oxygen with oxygen, not oxygen with neon. Oxygen and neon were different elements.

Ástríðr looked at the paper balls and stones again.

Ástríðr: “My analogy showed why equal count needn't mean equal mass. It didn't calculate either mass.”

Ms Luminara: “A good analogy can remove a false requirement without supplying all the missing data.”

They let the scales settle. Nothing in the room hurried them. The inequality remained, no less genuine for failing to satisfy the ceiling.

Natalie sat at the end of the bench. In physics she was used to keeping a quantity separate from the number used to express it. Here the familiar discipline had to survive unfamiliar names. “One mole” was not a mysterious package that weighed whatever a mole ought to weigh. It specified an amount. The substance and its molar mass connected that amount to a mass.

Natalie: “When I imagined one mole as a standard scoop, I quietly gave it a volume and maybe a weight. Neither belonged in the definition.”

Kàra nodded toward the identical containers.

Kàra: “Even the picture encouraged the scoop. They look like they should hold equal physical parcels.”

Ms Luminara: “These are model containers sized for the display. Their drawn dimensions don't encode volume, density, or pressure. We can compare the given counts and masses without pretending the artwork supplies everything else.”

Jackson removed the automatic weight feeder. He did not throw it away; it might be useful for a different demonstration. He disconnected the instruction that said equalize every one-mole sample. Then he replaced the ceiling slogan with a question: WHICH MOLAR MASS APPLIES?

A slot opened at the entrance to the bridge. It was shaped to receive a data card. Kàra tried the neon card against the oxygen cart. The slot accepted the physical rectangle, but Henry stopped the print command.

Henry: “The machinery fitting doesn't make the information relevant.”

They added a substance check before the arithmetic. The bridge no longer relied on a universal stamp, nor on whether a card happened to fit through a hole. The appropriate molar-mass card traveled with the specified entity.

Jeff released the pause handle. The neon scale stayed at approximately 20.18 g, the oxygen scale at approximately 32.00 g. The ceiling did not complain. For the first time that afternoon, the station allowed two different correct answers to stand beside the same numerical amount.

## 6. Natalie's fence posts

The bridge to the mass office was unfinished in a peculiar way. Its railing had posts but no rails between them. Each post carried a unit: grams, moles, or number of specified entities. Planks lay in piles nearby, printed with conversion factors. A notice said ALL PLANKS FIT BOTH WAYS. Henry read it and waited for the inevitable qualification.

Natalie recognized the arrangement before Ms Luminara named it. She drew a long horizontal line in her notebook and several short vertical strokes through it.

Natalie: “Fence posts. I used to set conversions out this way so I could see what belonged upstairs and downstairs. I haven't used it much with chemical names.”

She did not begin at the far end. She wrote the given quantity first: 2.50 mol neon atoms. The requested destination was grams of neon. The supplied card read 20.18 g neon per 1 mol neon atoms.

Kàra put the card on a plank and turned it over experimentally. Both orientations were possible as statements of the same relationship. Only one orientation would cancel the starting mol unit.

Natalie: “If I'm starting in moles and need grams, the moles in the factor must be underneath. Then I'm left with grams.”

She wrote: 2.50 mol × (20.18 g / 1 mol) = 50.45 g before rounding.

The bridge extended one span. Its construction was theatrical; the dimensional check was not. Ms Luminara reminded them that real units did not physically hold up a bridge. They made the logic of the conversion visible.

Jeff tried the factor upside down, deliberately, on a spare slate. His expression left mol²/g. The arithmetic engine still produced a number. It even printed a cheerful tick.

Jeff: “It will multiply anything I give it. The tick only means it finished multiplying.”

Jackson crossed out the tick and replaced it with CALCULATION PERFORMED. A second line, RESULT INTERPRETED, remained blank.

The distinction pleased Natalie more than the completed span. She knew how comforting a clean calculator display could be. It felt like an independent witness even when it merely repeated her own badly posed operation with more digits.

Natalie: “The units let me catch the wrong orientation before the number becomes persuasive.”

Ms Luminara: “And an estimate lets you make a second, different check.”

Ástríðr estimated that two and a half moles, each about twenty grams, should have a mass around fifty grams. The result 50.45 g fit that rough picture. A result near one eighth would not. The estimate did not replace the calculation; it helped them inspect it.

Henry asked why the answer on the dispatch slip became 50.5 g while Natalie's intermediate value had more digits. Ms Luminara pointed to 2.50 mol and the approximate molar-mass card. They were treating the amount as supplied to three significant figures. The receipt specified a halfway-rounding choice: round a positive value exactly halfway between two reports upward. Under that stated convention, 50.45 g became 50.5 g. Another convention could settle the tie differently; this receipt was not setting a rule for every classroom. The extra digits were useful inside the calculation, not evidence that the model amount had been known more precisely.

Natalie kept both entries, labeled “before rounding” and “reported result.” Crossing one out would have made the other look arbitrary.

The next gap in the bridge led from moles to a number of neon atoms. Henry chose the Avogadro-constant plank. Jeff wanted to multiply the fifty-gram result directly by it, because the constant was the one enormous number he now associated with particles.

He stopped himself when the units remained g/mol rather than giving a count.

Jeff: “I skipped the amount. Knowing which famous number belongs somewhere in the problem isn't enough.”

Natalie laid out the full route from mass: grams, then moles using the reciprocal molar mass, then number of specified entities using the Avogadro constant. She drew no extra step through an imaginary “mole mass.” The factors themselves carried the relationship.

Kàra added a small sign at the fork: CHOOSE THE DESTINATION BEFORE ARRANGING THE PLANKS.

Then Henry spoiled their satisfaction in a useful way. He set down a factor labeled 20.18 g neon per mol neon and another starting quantity labeled mol O₂ molecules. The formal mol units could cancel if one ignored the names. The resulting grams would still be scientifically wrong for the oxygen sample.

For a moment Natalie stared at her fence posts with disappointment. She had wanted them to catch every kind of error.

Natalie: “So the method works only if I also check the substance.”

Ms Luminara: “It checks a particular part of the reasoning. That is enough to make it powerful. It needn't be an oracle.”

Ástríðr put three small marks beside the completed example: appropriate relationship, correct orientation, sensible magnitude. They were not three versions of the same check. Each could notice something the others missed.

The class crossed the bridge slowly. Natalie ran her hand along the new railing. Her drawing had not made chemistry familiar by pretending it was all physics she already knew. It had given her a dependable place to begin, and then chemistry had required her to add the identity of what was being counted.

At the far end, she left an empty fence-post line beneath the worked example. It was room for a future calculation, not evidence that she owed the page another answer before she could leave.

## 7. Back across the bridge

The next receipt arrived from the opposite direction. Its top line said MASS OF O₂ CONTENTS: 8.00 g. Its second line asked for amount of O₂ molecules. A third, written later in a different ink, asked for number of oxygen atoms within those molecules.

Jeff carried it to the bridge and looked down the route they had just completed.

Jeff: “We built it going the other way. Is reversing it just turning the fraction over?”

Ms Luminara: “For the same molar-mass relationship, yes. Let's see what that means rather than trusting the gesture.”

Ástríðr took the O₂ card, 32.00 g per mol, and placed it beside the eight-gram amount. Eight was a quarter of thirty-two. Before entering any digits, she predicted a quarter of a mole of O₂ molecules.

Natalie set up the fence posts: 8.00 g O₂ × (1 mol O₂ molecules / 32.00 g O₂) = 0.250 mol O₂ molecules. The grams canceled. The molar mass belonged to O₂, so the substance labels agreed too.

Kàra: “And if we had used sixteen because oxygen is an element, we'd have answered a different amount question.”

Henry: “We'd have calculated the amount of oxygen atoms represented by that mass, under the corresponding approximation, and then mislabeled it if we called it molecules.”

They checked both descriptions rather than forbidding one. The 0.250 mol of O₂ molecules contained 0.500 mol of oxygen atoms. The factor of two came from the formula. It did not come from changing the sample's mass.

Jeff wrote the count of molecules as approximately 1.51 × 10²³ and the count of oxygen atoms within them as approximately 3.01 × 10²³. He kept the full calculator values on a scrap sheet but rounded the reported counts to reflect the supplied 8.00 g. The exact constant did not erase the limited precision of the starting mass and approximate molar mass.

The counting display objected to the word approximately. Its dictionary contained EXACT next to the Avogadro constant, and the station had copied the word into every result that used it.

Jackson traced the copying rule to a little roller in the printer. He disconnected the roller and laid the tickets in order: measurement, conversion relation, arithmetic, final statement.

Jackson: “Exact information can be part of an approximate conclusion. The weakest information hasn't disappeared just because a precise fact joined it.”

Natalie thought of the enormous rooftop digits. They had looked like a promise that the station could know everything completely. But a definition fixed one relationship. It did not guarantee that a balance had no uncertainty, that a sample was pure, or that every atomic-mass card was exact.

Natalie: “I was mixing certainty about a rule with certainty about the thing the rule was applied to.”

Ms Luminara: “That's worth keeping.”

They sat on the midpoint bench of the bridge while the corrected printer warmed. Beneath them moved a soft field of clouds. Kàra watched shadows cross its surface and asked whether weighing a sample was really counting particles at all. Nobody had watched the atoms arrive individually.

Ms Luminara: “It is an inference from a measured mass and an appropriate molar mass, together with the specified composition. If those inputs are good for the sample, the relationship lets you infer amount and then number.”

Henry: “So the balance doesn't recognize molecules.”

Ms Luminara: “Correct. A balance reading alone doesn't identify the substance. Different materials can have the same mass.”

Jeff set two identical eight-gram model cards on the bench, one for O₂ and one for a substance left unnamed. The first had a route to amount through the supplied O₂ molar mass. The second did not. Its absence of identity could not be repaired by pressing the calculator harder.

Ástríðr liked the blank second card. It made an honest stopping point visible. The station had been inclined to fill every blank, even when filling it meant smuggling in an assumption.

Ástríðr: “Can we leave this one saying not enough information?”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. That is a complete answer to a request the available data cannot support.”

Jackson filed the unnamed card in a tray marked NEEDS IDENTIFICATION, separate from the tray marked CALCULATION ERROR. If they confused those cases, they would ask someone to redo arithmetic when what they needed was information about the sample.

Before leaving the bench, Natalie checked the eight-gram calculation backward. The 0.250 mol multiplied by 32.00 g/mol returned 8.00 g. That round trip helped locate a reversed factor or a slipped decimal. It would not prove the original sample was O₂. She wrote that limit beneath the check.

The bridge now worked in both directions. Its strength came from knowing which relation could be reversed, which input had to be supplied, and which certainty had never been promised.

At the far end, the station offered them a certificate saying QUALIFIED TO WEIGH ANYTHING. Jeff returned it unsigned and accepted a small card showing the three quantities instead. It fitted into his pocket. It was also true.

## 8. The quiet canopy

The station's refreshment canopy stood between the warehouses, a roof of pale glass supported by columns that looked like stacked commas. There were no lesson screens beneath it. There was tea, water, a loaf wrapped in a cloth, and a tin labeled SIX BISCUITS. Ms Luminara set the tin where it could be reached without asking and opened a window toward the bridge.

For several minutes the class watched ordinary rain make its extraordinary journey straight down into the old puddle. The first cart had left a track through the water. Jackson stood his board against a dry column. Its oldest label had begun to curl away from the clip, and he caught it just before a draft could carry it back into the square.

Kàra had been restless on the bridge. Under the canopy she folded her arms and went still. Natalie recognized the kind of pause in which an idea continued working even after the explanation had stopped.

Kàra: “I keep picturing the two one-mole containers. Part of me still thinks equal count ought to make them the same size.”

Jeff found two storage boxes beneath the bench, one tall and narrow, one short and wide. They held the same number of folded napkins. He tipped the napkins onto the table and arranged them into identical piles.

Jeff: “The outside size was the packaging. But for gases, doesn't the space actually matter?”

Ms Luminara: “It does. Volume can connect to amount under specified conditions and an appropriate model. Today we have not been given the temperature, pressure, or a gas-volume relation. Our molar-mass route doesn't require us to invent those.”

She left the question open at that boundary. No universal gas law descended from the canopy. The class had enough new bridges without building one from unspecified weather.

Natalie drew a small box around what the station had supplied: identities, model amounts or masses, and conversion data. Beside it she wrote “not supplied: volume conditions.” She was used to thinking of omissions as failures in a problem. Here the omission could also define the problem's scope. The mistake was to use a quantity the picture had never actually provided.

Ástríðr pulled one biscuit from the tin and held it up.

Ástríðr: “The drawing on the lid shows biscuits larger than these. Shall I request a refund from perspective?”

Henry counted five biscuits remaining, then glanced at the label and smiled rather than correcting the tin. The label described its starting contents. The current state had changed in a plainly visible way.

Henry: “We need a time on some labels too.”

Jackson wrote INITIAL CONTENTS beside the six. He did not calculate moles of biscuits. The analogy had already served its purpose, and the biscuits were more useful as biscuits.

Natalie tried to turn back several pages and found that the corner of the mass calculation had become damp. She separated it carefully from the page beneath. While she was occupied, Henry moved her cup farther from the notebook. He said nothing about the arrangement of liquids and paper. She was grateful for that.

Ástríðr tore a piece from the loaf and slid the cloth toward Jackson. He still had one hand on the curling label.

Ástríðr: “I can hold that. You can eat.”

Jackson relinquished the board, although his eyes followed it until she rested it flat on an empty chair. For the first time since the square, both his hands were free. He brushed paper dust from his sleeve and took the bread.

Jeff was looking up through the canopy. The cloud had finally drifted off the enormous display, leaving every digit visible.

Jeff: “I went all the way up those stairs and didn't notice the view in the other direction.”

Henry: “There were blue roofs.”

Jeff turned toward him.

Henry: “I looked while you were reading the sign about the steps.”

The admission made Jeff smile. He asked which roof belonged to the bus shelter, and Henry pointed it out. Natalie left the damp page open to dry instead of writing down the answer. The two of them could have a conversation she didn't preserve.

A drop slid down the outside of the glass. Kàra followed it until it joined another, larger drop and vanished below the sill. Ms Luminara poured more tea. Nothing rang for several minutes.

Ástríðr asked whether it was possible to understand a calculation and still need the written route every time. Ms Luminara said that using a reference did not invalidate the reasoning. The question was whether the route preserved the relevant relationships, not whether it had been performed from memory at speed.

Henry retrieved the reference card from his pocket. The last word on it had folded around the edge, where Ástríðr could not see it. He smoothed it out, then asked Natalie if he could borrow the wider card beneath her cup. Together they fitted the full entity label at the top and defined shorter labels for the working below. This time Ástríðr could follow the route without turning the card over.

Kàra stood and looked through the canopy toward a lower warehouse they had not visited. Its windows flashed with a different kind of light. Instead of count or mass, its sign said AVERAGE.

A tapping sound reached them: tap, tap, pause; tap, tap, pause. On a display behind the nearest window, a sketch of an atom repeatedly failed inspection. The sketch had a small wedge missing from one neutron.

Jeff set down his cup.

Jeff: “That machine looks as though it is solving the wrong problem very energetically.”

Ms Luminara did not turn the tea break into a race. She closed the thermos, asked whether anyone wanted to remain beneath the canopy, and waited while Natalie marked her place. Then she brought the pause handle with them. It detached from its post and traveled as a portable control, which was exactly the sort of ridiculous feature Jeff found reasonable.

The counting station had learned to keep entities and masses distinct. Now it was trying to turn a statement about a collection into an instruction for making an individual. Natalie opened a fresh page before they reached the door.

## 9. Pause before the explanation

The tapping came from a drawing machine. Its pencil arm traced a nucleus, erased part of the outline, and tried again. On the work order above it, elegant lettering announced AVERAGE ATOM REQUIRED: MASS 10.2 u. Below that stood an instruction in much smaller type: ADJUST NEUTRON CONTENT TO MATCH DECIMAL.

Jeff pulled the pause handle.

The pencil stopped a hair's breadth above the page. Its motor hummed once, then fell silent. Everyone could hear the rain outside again.

Jeff: “I don't have the full explanation yet. But making part of a neutron to satisfy a receipt doesn't sound like counting.”

Ms Luminara: “You can pause before you know the complete explanation.”

She placed a boundary card over the pencil arm. It said FICTIONAL DRAWING MACHINE. NO NUCLEUS IS BEING BUILT. The sketch could frighten or mislead without being a physical nuclear process. They would investigate the instruction that had produced it.

Henry read the work order from the bottom upward. The input described a collection. The output requested an individual. Between them a rule said EVERY MEMBER MUST EQUAL COLLECTION AVERAGE.

Henry: “That's a much broader rule than anything about atoms.”

Ástríðr took three pencils from a cup: two short, one long. She lined up their ends, then imagined their average length. None needed to have exactly that length. A statement about the group could lie between its members' values.

Ástríðr: “If the machine used this rule on us, it would resize the pencils until it forgot there had been different lengths.”

Natalie recognized the statistical shape of the problem. Recognition brought relief, but she resisted stepping straight into an explanation. She still needed to know what the values represented in this particular collection.

Natalie: “I know why a mean needn't be an observed value. I don't yet know what the two kinds of atom are, or why their masses differ.”

Ms Luminara opened the side of the model cabinet. Two rows of counters rested beneath a label large enough to read from the door: ARTIFICIAL SIMPLIFIED ISOTOPE EXAMPLE. INVENTED MASSES: 10 u AND 11 u. NOT DATA FOR A REAL ELEMENT.

Each counter had the same proton-count badge. The two kinds carried different neutron-count badges, though the display deliberately did not assign a real elemental name or claim that adding those counts gave exactly the invented masses.

Ms Luminara: “Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different neutron numbers. The proton number establishes the element. Changing the neutron number distinguishes isotopes of that element.”

Kàra studied the badges before looking at the assigned mass values on the front. She had been inclined to identify the heavier counter as a different element simply because its total differed. The shared proton identity stopped that jump.

Kàra: “So different mass doesn't automatically mean different element. We need to ask what changed.”

Henry: “And the 10 u and 11 u are assigned model masses. They're not being presented as exact sums of proton and neutron masses.”

Ms Luminara: “Right. The unit u is the unified atomic mass unit, also called the dalton. Real isotope masses generally are not whole numbers in that unit. Integer neutron counts do not require integer masses in u.”

Natalie wrote “decimal mass ≠ fractional neutron” and put a firm box around it. Then she made the box less final by adding the reason: mass and particle count were different quantities. The average could have a decimal even when the model members used integer masses, and an individual real isotope mass could also have a decimal for a different reason.

Jeff returned to the stopped drawing. Its mistake was not simply that it had encountered a decimal. It had taken a collection summary as an individual construction specification and assumed the decimal part had to be a piece of a neutron.

Jeff: “There are two leaps in it. Average means every member; mass in u means neutron pieces.”

Jackson copied both leaps to the FIRST VERSION board. If they only banned decimals from the machine, they would create a new false rule. If they only changed 10.2 to 10, they would destroy the information the average was meant to preserve.

The room had no need for an emergency now. The pencil arm rested, harmless but accusatory, above its impossible sketch. Ástríðr covered the drawing with a sheet of clean paper so the class could work without looking at a torn neutron every time they turned around.

Ástríðr: “Let's make it describe what is here before it tries to make anything match.”

Ms Luminara moved the output tray from beneath the pencil to beneath a blank graph board. A graph could show a distribution: how many model atoms had each assigned mass. It would not pretend that a mean was a photograph of a typical nucleus.

Natalie approached the cabinet with her notebook closed. For this part she wanted to touch the model counters and see how many there were before abbreviating the whole collection into two percentages. Her statistical intuition had found a way into the problem. It still had to meet the actual data.

<a id="ch05-scene-10"></a>
## 10. Eighty, twenty, and the missing middle

The cabinet contained one hundred counters. Eighty represented the artificial 10 u isotope. Twenty represented the artificial 11 u isotope. All labels repeated that these were invented values for a simplified model, so nobody had to remember a warning from another room while using the numbers.

Ástríðr stood between the two rows and made her first prediction aloud.

Ástríðr: “If I hear ten and eleven, I want to say ten and a half. That's halfway. But the rows aren't equally long.”

Kàra pulled out four trays of twenty light counters and one tray of twenty heavy counters. The same two possible masses now had visibly unequal representation. Halfway gave each kind an equal voice in the calculation, even though the collection did not contain equal numbers of them.

Natalie: “It's an ordinary mean over atoms. If I list every atom, eighty entries are ten and twenty entries are eleven. Grouping the equal entries shouldn't change the result.”

She placed a card marked 10 u beside each light counter, then stopped after the tenth. The pile of repeated cards made the idea clear without requiring another ninety copies. Henry wrote the full mass total instead: 80 × 10 u + 20 × 11 u = 1,020 u.

They divided that total by the number of model atoms, one hundred. The average mass per atom was 10.2 u.

The drawing machine tried to wake up at the number. Jeff kept the pause handle down.

Jeff: “We know what it means now. That doesn't make its old instruction safe to resume.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly. A better answer is not enough if the next step still misinterprets it.”

Henry wrote the shorter form beneath the total-and-count calculation: 0.80 × 10 u + 0.20 × 11 u = 10.2 u. The fractions were eighty out of one hundred and twenty out of one hundred. Their sum was one. They were fractions of the atom count, not fractions of the mass.

Natalie traced the two expressions with a finger. The weighted form no longer looked like a new recipe she had to memorize separately. It was the same total divided by the total number, with the division distributed into the two count factors.

Natalie: “The weights tell me how often each value occurs among the atoms. Chemistry hasn't changed the mean. It has made me specify the population.”

Ms Luminara: “That's the statistical bridge.”

Kàra wanted to see 10.2 without a calculator. She put every counter on an imagined baseline of 10 u. If all one hundred were light, the total would be 1,000 u. Twenty heavier counters each added 1 u beyond that baseline, bringing twenty extra units of mass across the hundred atoms. The extra mass averaged 0.2 u per atom.

Kàra: “So ten plus two tenths. The extra is spread across the calculation, not physically smeared across the nuclei.”

Henry nodded at the qualification. It protected the most tempting misreading of the method. The baseline picture was an accounting device. None of the actual model counters changed.

Ástríðr put her halfway prediction beside the answer rather than throwing it away. Ten and a half would have been right for an equal-count mixture of the two invented isotopes. Her intuition had supplied a result for a different condition.

Ástríðr: “I wasn't wrong that halfway can happen. I was wrong to assume equal representation because there were two categories.”

Jackson inspected the station's earlier averaging command. It read ADD AVAILABLE MASSES; DIVIDE BY NUMBER OF LABELS. The machine had counted two labels instead of one hundred atoms. That explained why its first output had been 10.5 u before somebody manually replaced it with 10.2 u and triggered the fractional-neutron sketch.

Jackson: “They corrected the result without correcting how the station thought about the collection. Then a second rule made the corrected result into another mistake.”

Jackson laid the two instructions end to end. There was barely a hand's breadth of paper between them. He had been looking for the moment the machine went wrong; now he needed room for two moments, and for the earlier correction that had fixed a number between them.

They replaced the first command with total mass divided by number of atoms, or the equivalent count-fraction weighted sum. They replaced the second with a histogram: eighty marks above 10 u, twenty above 11 u. A thin pointer at 10.2 u indicated the mean, with no bar beneath it.

Jeff noticed the empty space first.

Jeff: “The answer is where there isn't an atom.”

Natalie: “A valid summary, and not an observed member.”

The quiet after that observation lasted longer than the calculation. Kàra looked from the empty middle to the untouched counters. Ástríðr moved her prediction card beneath a new heading, CONDITIONS THAT WOULD MAKE THIS TRUE. Henry checked the labels on both axes. Jackson followed the output cable and confirmed that the histogram no longer sent instructions to the drawing arm.

Ms Luminara finally named the repair: an atom-number-weighted average describes the collection's mean atomic mass. It need not describe any individual atom in that collection.

Jeff released the pause handle. The pencil drew two bars, a mean pointer, and a very small flourish in the corner. Nobody objected to the flourish. It was the first thing the machine had made that did not ask the matter to become a misunderstanding.

<a id="ch05-scene-11"></a>
## 11. The mean moves; the counters do not

Kàra returned to the artificial isotope cabinet carrying an empty tray. The pencil had drawn the right graph once. That was encouraging, but the old machine had also printed 10.2 u after someone supplied it by hand. She wanted to distinguish a working mean calculation from a machine that had learned to repeat their preferred answer.

She placed the empty tray below the cabinet and left the original histogram visible for comparison.

Kàra: “Let's give it a collection for which the right answer has to change.”

Kàra: “If I take away some of the heavy counters, the mean should move toward ten.”

Henry began to calculate, but she asked him to wait. She first wanted to say what remained unchanged: the assigned isotope masses, the shared elemental identity, and the masses of individual counters. Only the composition of the collection would change.

She removed ten of the twenty heavy counters. The original hundred-counter collection was now a ninety-counter collection: eighty light and ten heavy. The total assigned mass fell from 1,020 u to 910 u. The average became 910 divided by 90, approximately 10.111 u.

Ástríðr frowned at the display.

Ástríðr: “I expected ten point one. I pictured ten heavy out of a hundred, but we don't have a hundred anymore.”

Natalie opened her notebook at the previous page. Its eighty-percent and twenty-percent entries belonged to the earlier collection. Keeping those percentages after removing counters would preserve an old description of a new sample.

Natalie: “The denominator moved too. I have to recount the population I'm averaging over.”

They restored the original collection before trying a different operation. This time Kàra replaced ten heavy counters with ten light ones, keeping the total count at one hundred. Now there were ninety light and ten heavy, and the average was exactly 10.1 u within the artificial model.

The two manipulations had both reduced the number of heavy counters by ten. They had not produced the same collection. One removed atoms from the model population; the other replaced them. The distinction had been hidden inside the everyday phrase “take away ten heavy ones.”

Jackson labeled both operation cards precisely. If the station received only the phrase, it might do the wrong operation and still give a correct calculation for what it had actually done.

Jackson: “The input needs to describe the change, not only the intention to make the mean lower.”

Ms Luminara: “And a lower mean doesn't tell us which operation caused it.”

Jeff asked to try the reverse. He added ten heavy counters to the original eighty-light, twenty-heavy collection without removing anything. Now the total count was one hundred ten, and the heavy count was thirty. The average rose, but it did not rise to 10.3 u. Thirty out of one hundred ten was not thirty percent.

Jeff: “I wanted the decimal to copy the number of heavy counters. It only does that in the special hundred-counter display.”

Henry wrote the general artificial-model relation: mean mass = 10 u + f × 1 u, where f was the fraction of atoms belonging to the 11 u isotope. In this model the mean's displacement above 10 u directly represented that fraction times the one-unit mass gap. It was the fraction, not the raw heavy-atom count, that belonged in the expression.

Natalie saw another familiar statistical distinction. A mean was unchanged when an entire population was duplicated: double every count, double the total mass, divide by double the number. But adding only one kind altered the composition. “More atoms” was not enough information to predict what would happen to the mean.

They tested this. Jeff duplicated the original eighty-to-twenty collection into a second tray and combined them. Two hundred model atoms now had a total assigned mass of 2,040 u. The mean remained 10.2 u. The histogram bars doubled in height; the mean pointer stayed in place.

Ástríðr watched the pointer with evident pleasure.

Ástríðr: “A bigger sample doesn't have to have a bigger average. It can just have more of the same proportions.”

Kàra: “And a smaller sample can have a bigger average if the composition changes the other way.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. Total, count, and mean can change differently. Keep them in separate columns.”

The station had been trying to save paper by using one column for all three. Natalie put an empty sheet under its printer and invited it to be less economical. Three columns appeared. They were easier to read than the single overloaded one, despite occupying more space.

For the last trial, Ástríðr asked for an equal-count mixture of the two invented isotopes. Fifty light and fifty heavy gave 10.5 u. She placed her original halfway prediction beside the new result. It no longer looked like a discarded failure. It had found the condition under which it belonged.

Natalie wrote a sentence slowly enough to think about every part: “A change in the mean can come from a change in proportions without a change to any individual isotope mass.” Then she added, “The mean alone doesn't tell me how many atoms there are.”

The graph room became quiet. No machine demanded that they manufacture a 10.5 u atom. The pointer could move while every counter retained its identity. They had gained a way to describe change that did not invent a microscopic process merely because a macroscopic summary had shifted.

As they restored the counters to the cabinet, a work order arrived from the Archive of Mixed Batches. Two batch-mean cards were clipped to it, but their dispatch copies had no counts. The archive wanted a mean for the combined collection. Beneath the request, an automatic suggestion offered to recover the missing counts from the means themselves.

Natalie held the order beside the two-hundred-counter trial. Its mean was the same as the original hundred's. She thought she already saw a problem with the suggestion, but Henry had found a drawer labeled REVERSE LOOKUP. They could inspect what the station proposed before trusting it with the archive's request.

<a id="ch05-scene-12"></a>
## 12. A receipt that runs backward

The reverse-lookup drawer contained a demonstration card headed ARTIFICIAL 10 u / 11 u MODEL. Its body gave only an average: 10.6 u. The box for heavy-isotope atom fraction was blank; the neighboring box, labeled TOTAL ATOM COUNT, was blank too.

Henry: “We can run this relation backward. I'm not sure it will fill both boxes.”

Jeff looked toward the bridge they had built between grams and moles.

Jeff: “Another reversible route?”

Ms Luminara: “Within the stated two-isotope model, yes. Let's be clear about what information makes it possible.”

They knew there were exactly two kinds of model atom, assigned masses 10 u and 11 u. The fractions summed to one. They were not being asked to identify an unknown real element from an average or infer an unlimited mixture from a single number.

Natalie began with the baseline picture Kàra had used. If every atom had the lighter assigned mass, the average would be 10 u. The observed model mean was 0.6 u above that. Each heavy model atom contributed 1 u above the light baseline. Therefore sixty percent of the atoms had to be heavy.

Natalie: “I can see the fraction as how far the mean has traveled across the one-unit gap.”

Henry supplied the algebra without replacing her picture. If f was the heavy atom fraction, then the light atom fraction was 1 − f. The relation became 10(1 − f) + 11f = 10.6. Combining terms gave 10 + f = 10.6, so f = 0.60.

Ástríðr made a ten-counter model with six heavy and four light counters. Its total assigned mass was 106 u, and its mean was 10.6 u. Kàra made a hundred-counter model with sixty heavy and forty light. It had the same mean. The two models differed in count and total mass, not in composition fraction.

Jackson: “Then the receipt still doesn't tell us how much material was in the original collection.”

Ms Luminara: “Correct. A fraction tells you a proportion. You need another piece of information to establish the collection's size.”

Jeff placed an empty frame around the ten-counter model and another around the hundred-counter model. Both fit the receipt. That made the limit visible: a solved fraction did not imply a uniquely reconstructed sample.

Natalie nearly wrote “we recovered the mixture” and changed it to “we recovered the two-model-isotope proportions.” The longer phrase prevented a larger claim from slipping in under a smaller success.

A second damaged card appeared in the same file. It used the same artificial masses but listed an average of 11.4 u. Henry's algebra produced a heavy fraction of 1.4. The calculator showed no alarm. The graph board placed the mean pointer beyond both bars.

Kàra: “One hundred forty percent of the atoms can't be the heavy kind if the remaining fraction has to be ordinary, nonnegative atoms of the light kind.”

Ástríðr: “And no amount of choosing between ten and eleven can make the average heavier than eleven.”

Ms Luminara: “The result tells us the stated data and the two-isotope model don't fit together. It doesn't authorize a negative count of light atoms.”

They considered possible causes without selecting one unsupported explanation. The average might have been copied incorrectly. The sample might include a kind excluded from the model. The assigned masses might belong to another exercise. They could flag the inconsistency, but the card alone could not say which part was wrong.

Jackson created a tray marked CHECK SOURCE DATA. It joined NEEDS IDENTIFICATION and CALCULATION ERROR from the weighing office. Different problems were beginning to acquire different destinations. A station that treated every difficulty as bad arithmetic would lose the very evidence needed to repair them.

Natalie kept the impossible result beside the valid one. Its value was diagnostic. The arithmetic had not failed; it had exposed an assumption that could not be sustained with those inputs.

Natalie: “I sometimes treat a number outside the expected range as permission to change the calculation until it comes out nicer. Here I should first ask whether I copied the problem and chose the model correctly.”

Henry: “And if both are faithful, the inconsistency itself is the answer we can support.”

The room's pencil arm began to draw a question mark above the 11.4 u card. Jeff let it. A question mark was a better product than a counterfeit isotope.

Ms Luminara asked them to return to the 10.6 u card and check it in the forward direction. Sixty percent heavy, forty percent light: 0.60 × 11 u + 0.40 × 10 u = 10.6 u. The check confirmed the algebra within the specified model. It still did not establish sample size or make these artificial values real isotope data.

Ástríðr slid the ten-counter model back into its tray. She had begun by expecting the average to identify an atom. Now it could help recover a proportion, but only because the possible members had been specified. The mean was useful without being omniscient.

Natalie returned the two demonstration cards to their separate slots. The valid reverse calculation had recovered a fraction; it had left TOTAL ATOM COUNT empty. She wrote that distinction on the archive's work order. They would need the original batch inventories, not a cleverer way of rearranging the means.

Jackson checked the route printed on the backs of the clipped cards. Both had passed through central dispatch. He turned his FIRST VERSION board so the cut edge of the original mole label was visible. The archive's count fields had occupied much the same strip of paper. It was a reason to compare the originals, not yet proof of what had happened to them.

<a id="ch05-scene-13"></a>
## 13. Two averages are not two atoms

The Archive of Mixed Batches had a long sorting table and a wall of drawers, each with a little window for a receipt. The windows were spotless. The drawers were opaque. It was an excellent arrangement for trusting a summary without seeing what had been summarized.

The original mean cards still occupied two matching windows: 10 u for Batch A, 10.5 u for Batch B. Both named the artificial 10 u/11 u teaching model. The archive had combined those means and printed 10.25 u for the merged collection.

Natalie began to copy the calculation. Two numbers, add and divide by two: her pencil moved before the missing count fields caught her attention again. She stopped with the division sign half drawn.

Natalie: “I've nearly done exactly what we came here to check.”

Ástríðr did not offer another halfway prediction. She tried the handle beneath the first card.

Ástríðr: “Then let's see what is behind these.”

The first drawer came easily. Batch A contained twenty artificial 10 u model atoms and no heavy ones. The second drawer stuck halfway. Jeff supported its underside while Kàra eased it over the catch. Batch B held eighty model atoms: forty assigned 10 u and forty assigned 11 u. Inventory strips ran along the inside edges, where the dispatch copies had not reached.

Ástríðr placed her hands beside the two counts. The larger batch had four times as many atoms. Their matching windows had concealed that difference. Natalie put down her pencil and helped carry the trays to the table. She wanted the merged collection in front of her before making another summary.

Jackson brought the trays together. The merged collection contained sixty light model atoms and forty heavy ones. Its mean was 10.4 u, which the counter display confirmed by totaling 1,040 u over one hundred atoms.

The archive printer protested that it had used a legitimate averaging operation. Henry agreed that it had calculated the unweighted mean of the two batch means correctly. That was simply not the requested mean over all atoms in the combined collection.

Henry: “The arithmetic can be valid for the wrong question again.”

Natalie set the two summaries on the table with their missing companions: batch counts. Batch A contributed twenty percent of the combined atom count; Batch B contributed eighty percent. The pooled mean was 0.20 × 10 u + 0.80 × 10.5 u = 10.4 u.

She checked the answer against the combined counters before circling it. The principle was familiar; that had not stopped her from starting the wrong calculation. Now the word “weight” gave her another reason to slow down. In this room, filled with masses, it was easy to hear it as an instruction to use a batch's mass as the statistical multiplier.

Natalie: “Here the weights are counts, or count fractions. That word doesn't mean I should put each drawer on a scale and use its mass fraction.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly. A statistical weight describes how a value contributes to the calculation. We must specify what supplies that weight.”

Kàra took the original erroneous 10.25 u ticket and tried to imagine a collection for which it would be right. If the two batches had equal atom counts but kept their respective means, the ordinary average of the means would work. She made two ten-counter trays: ten light in one, five light and five heavy in the other. Their pooled average was 10.25 u.

Kàra: “Again, the shortcut has a condition. Equal numbers behind each summary.”

Jeff returned the real twenty-and-eighty counts to the table. He could see why the shortcut was attractive. The two receipts looked equal in size, used the same font, and occupied matching windows. The graphic design had made the summaries seem equally representative.

Jeff: “The cards are the same size. The populations aren't.”

Jackson: “It can report the two batch means separately. It can say the combined mean needs the relative counts. It shouldn't quietly assume equal counts.”

Natalie drew two possible hidden populations that fit the same pair of mean cards. In one, the lighter batch dominated, so the pooled mean lay nearer ten. In the other, the higher-mean batch dominated, so it lay nearer ten and a half. The two summaries alone could not distinguish them.

Natalie laid a strip of string around each batch tray, then drew the two loops into one large boundary. The individual counters retained their assigned masses. Her denominator now counted everything inside the combined boundary. She left the string in place while Ástríðr compared it with the two smaller loops in the notebook drawing.

Ástríðr: “At the first cabinet I gave the two isotope labels equal influence. Here there were two batch labels waiting for us to do it again.”

Natalie looked at the unfinished division sign in her notebook.

Natalie: “And I was quite ready to help.”

Natalie: “Now the word weight really does mean mass. We need to keep the two meanings apart.”

## 14. Equal mass is a different promise

The next room had two shallow pans. A label above them identified the same artificial teaching model: light isotope assigned 10 u; heavy isotope assigned 11 u. The station had placed 110 u of assigned model mass in each pan. A central receipt announced FIFTY PERCENT OF EACH ISOTOPE.

Henry covered the percentage with a blank strip of paper.

Henry: “Fifty percent of what?”

Jeff counted the counters in the left pan. Eleven light model atoms, each assigned 10 u, gave 110 u. In the right pan, ten heavy model atoms, each assigned 11 u, also gave 110 u. The mass totals matched. The atom counts did not.

Ástríðr: “Half the mass came from each kind. Slightly more than half the atoms are the light kind.”

She said it twice, more slowly the second time. The sentence felt as though it ought to contradict itself until she kept the two denominators visible: total mass for one fraction, total atom count for the other.

Natalie drew two boxes beneath the receipt. The mass-fraction box contained 110 u divided by 220 u, giving 0.50 for each type. The atom-fraction box contained eleven divided by twenty-one for the light type and ten divided by twenty-one for the heavy type. The heavy atom fraction was approximately 0.4762, not 0.50.

Kàra traced the count with a fingertip.

Kàra: “It takes fewer heavier atoms to supply the same mass. That direction makes sense before we calculate.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. Equal mass is not the same condition as equal number. That's why isotope abundances used in this atomic mean must be atom-number fractions.”

The room's averaging machine had multiplied 10 u and 11 u by 0.50 each and printed 10.5 u. That was the answer for equal numbers, a different collection. The actual collection held twenty-one model atoms with a total assigned mass of 220 u. Its mean was 220 divided by 21, approximately 10.4762 u.

Jeff grimaced at the long decimal.

Jeff: “The wrong answer is tidier.”

Natalie: “Tidiness isn't a measurement.”

She kept the exact fraction 220/21 u beside the rounded decimal. The fraction expressed the artificial model's arithmetic without pretending that the decimal stopped where the printer ran out of space.

Henry checked the alternative weighted form: (11/21) × 10 u + (10/21) × 11 u. It gave the same result. This time the weights came from the atom counts, as the question required.

Jackson followed the incorrect fifty-fifty label to its source. The weighing office had correctly reported that half the total mass came from each isotope. During transfer through dispatch, “mass fraction” had become merely “fraction.” The averaging room had then read the shortened label as an atom fraction. Nobody needed to make a numerical error for the final result to become wrong.

Jackson: “It's the original missing-entity problem wearing a different coat. We kept the number and lost what it was a fraction of.”

Ms Luminara: “That connection is important. A denominator is part of the meaning.”

Ástríðr rearranged the twenty-one counters into a circle. Eleven light, ten heavy. Then she rearranged them into two rows. Neither arrangement changed the fractions. What mattered was not where the counters sat or how equal the pans looked, but which quantity had been divided by which total.

Kàra asked whether a mass fraction could ever be useful. Ms Luminara said certainly. It answered a different question about composition. The repair was not to ban it. The repair was to label it and convert appropriately when a number-weighted mean was required.

Natalie imagined receiving only masses of the two model isotopes. She could divide each mass total by its per-atom mass to recover a proportional count, then normalize those counts by their sum. With the invented values, 110 u divided by 10 u per model atom gave eleven model atoms; the other division gave ten. The familiar dimensional method had reappeared inside the statistical problem.

Natalie: “My fence posts can get me from mass contributions to counts. Then my weighted mean uses the count fractions. Those are two stages, not one interchangeable set of percentages.”

Henry added that for real macroscopic samples, amount and molar-mass relationships could play the corresponding role. They did not need to perform such a measurement here. The small artificial collection was enough to show why the denominators differed.

For a while they left both receipts on the table: fifty percent by mass, and about 47.62 percent heavy atoms by number. Both were correct for the same model collection. The station's problem had been its insistence that one percentage must replace the other everywhere.

Ástríðr: “The phrase ‘same sample’ doesn't mean every description of it has the same numerical value.”

Ms Luminara: “Right. Good descriptions agree by the appropriate relationships, not by all looking alike.”

Henry lifted the blank strip from the percentage on the original receipt. Jeff wrote BY MASS beneath it. Jackson attached their count-fraction calculation to the outgoing copy, then watched the two sheets disappear together into the transfer slot.

A narrow return copy emerged from the corridor printer. It said FIFTY PERCENT. Nothing underneath.

Jackson: “We have just watched it lose the words again.”

Jeff reached for the return copy before anyone could feed it into the averaging machine. This time they had both the complete record they had sent and the shortened record returned. Jackson pinned them side by side and followed the transfer line on the wall toward central dispatch. The line passed a sampling window, where another held receipt was tapping against the glass. He stopped to read its source label before adding it to the evidence.

## 15. The little sample at the edge

The held receipt belonged to a sampling window beside the archive. It claimed that the exhibit's population label needed correcting. Through the glass lay a large artificial collection with an exactly stipulated inventory: eighty percent 10 u model atoms, twenty percent 11 u model atoms, the same invented isotope values as before. A sampling drawer could bring out ten counters for inspection without changing their assigned identities.

Kàra asked to repeat the check that had produced the complaint. Her first ten-counter sample held nine light and one heavy counter. Its average was 10.1 u. The population card above the window still said 10.2 u.

Kàra: “Either the little sample is wrong, or the big label is wrong.”

She looked at Ms Luminara before pulling the drawer again.

Ms Luminara: “They describe different collections. A small random sample needn't reproduce the whole population's proportions exactly. We can check the calculation for these ten without deciding that the larger label must change.”

Kàra watched the nine light counters. They had genuinely come from the model population. Nobody had altered their masses. She counted them again, leaving the tenth counter where it was.

Kàra: “Then this can be a real part of the collection without being a miniature of its exact proportions.”

Natalie felt the statistical familiarity return, this time with a useful warning. It was easy to teach a hundred-counter mixture and let its neat percentages imply that every handful contained the same ratio. The hand-sized picture could accidentally become a claim about perfect local uniformity.

Natalie: “The eighty-twenty inventory describes the whole stipulated population. It doesn't require every group of ten to contain eight and two.”

They returned the counters and mixed the model before taking another sample. This one contained seven light and three heavy, for a mean of 10.3 u. A third contained eight and two, giving 10.2 u. The third sample matched the population composition in this particular draw. It did not make the first two samples faulty.

Jeff kept the sample labels attached. He had seen the station replace a whole population with a summary, then mistake the summary for every member. It could make a related mistake by treating a small sample's mean as the exact population mean without considering how the sample was obtained.

Jeff: “We should say which ten we're describing. And whether we're making an estimate beyond them.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. In this exhibit, we know the complete population by stipulation. In a real investigation, sampling and measurement may be how we learn about a population we cannot enumerate completely.”

Henry asked whether larger samples always produced a closer mean. Ms Luminara declined the word always. Larger well-chosen samples generally improve statistical information, but one could not promise that every larger random draw would land closer than every smaller draw. Nor could a large sample automatically repair a biased selection method.

Ástríðr demonstrated the latter without a formula. She filled a tray by deliberately choosing only heavy counters. The tray could become very large and still fail to represent the population's proportions. Its sample mean would accurately describe the selected counters while misleading anyone who assumed they were representative of the whole.

Ástríðr: “More of my favorite kind doesn't tell us more fairly about everything.”

Jackson wrote HOW SELECTED? beside HOW MANY? on the sampling record. The station had a space for count but none for selection method. An omitted process could change what the result meant even when every tally and division was correct.

Natalie placed a line down the center of a page. On one side she wrote “mean of these observed model atoms.” On the other, “estimate of the wider population mean.” The arithmetic might look similar. The claim extended farther on the second side and needed support about sampling.

Ms Luminara did not turn the visit into a course on statistical inference. There were no confidence intervals to calculate, no probability distribution to memorize. The useful boundary was already visible: a description of a selected group did not become an exact statement about everything beyond it merely because it used the word average.

Kàra returned her first nine-to-one sample to its tray. She had initially treated its disagreement with the population mean as evidence of malfunction. Now she could preserve the observation without forcing the labels to agree.

Kàra: “This is another place where a different number can be correct for a different boundary.”

Henry: “As long as the boundary is actually stated.”

They sat by the sampling window while the counters settled. Natalie was thinking about the notebook itself as a kind of selection. She tended to copy the clean example, the one with the neat proportions and the convenient decimal. If she kept only that, she might remember a world in which collections always behaved as tidily as the example chosen to explain them.

She copied all three sample compositions. Then she wrote why the third was not the only legitimate one. That took more space than the mean formula, but it kept the formula from becoming a superstition about tidy samples.

Jeff closed the sampling drawer and returned its original receipt to the holding slot with the collection identified. Jackson left the larger population label in place. He had arrived ready to collect another incorrect number; he left with two different means and a record of why both belonged. He clipped that record beside the returned fifty-percent slip. They would need more than a pile of disagreements to diagnose dispatch.

The transfer line ran onward past a cabinet labeled REAL REFERENCE DATA. Its outgoing register included the neon card from the weighing warehouse. Jackson stopped there to compare the source with the version on their board before they reached the central machine.

## 16. Three quantities with similar numbers

The reference cabinet had no moving parts. Its cards were plain, dated, and linked to named scientific sources. Henry matched the neon entry to their weighing card by name. He was about to call it checked when Ástríðr tapped the drawer heading. The reference used a different quantity label.

He withdrew the stamp and set the two cards flat. The digits could look reassuringly similar without the cards saying the same thing.

Henry found three drawer labels: ATOMIC MASS; RELATIVE ATOMIC MASS; MOLAR MASS.

Henry: “These are related, but the drawer names aren't synonyms.”

Ms Luminara set a blank example card beside the two source cards. The mass of an individual atom could be expressed in the atomic mass unit u, also called Da. An average atomic mass for a stated sample could use the same mass unit. Relative atomic mass, traditionally also called atomic weight, compared an average atomic mass with the atomic mass constant and was dimensionless. Molar mass related mass to amount and used units such as g/mol.

Ástríðr put a small frame around the units on each card.

Ástríðr: “If the numbers look familiar, I might swap the labels without noticing.”

Natalie: “Which would be the same habit we've been repairing all day.”

They returned to the neon card from the weighing office. Its approximate 20.18 g/mol was a molar mass used for the story's ordinary atmospheric-neon example. It was not a claim that every neon atom had exactly the same mass of 20.18 u. The real element had isotopic composition, and a mean needed the relevant composition information.

Kàra asked whether the decimals in a periodic-table entry were always just percentages peeking through. Ms Luminara separated the issues. Weighted isotope composition contributed to average atomic values. Individual isotope masses themselves also generally had noninteger values in u. A decimal alone did not reveal which quantity had been printed or why its value had that form.

Ms Luminara: “Read the heading and source. A decimal point is not a complete explanation.”

Jeff held the artificial 10 u and 11 u card beside the real-data cabinet. He did not put it into a drawer. Its clean integers had isolated the average calculation. They had never been measured isotope masses for a real element.

Jeff: “We should keep the artificial label even when the arithmetic is correct. Otherwise somebody could remember the scene and think these are actual data.”

Jackson attached that label permanently to the teaching tray. The source note would travel with any copy of the exercise, not rely on a warning elsewhere in the station.

Natalie wrote one question in the margin: why didn't integer particle counts require integer masses in u? Ms Luminara distinguished mass number, the integer count of protons and neutrons, from measured atomic mass. A deeper note on constituent masses and binding energy waited in the optional support.

Ms Luminara: “You can keep that question without solving a nuclear-physics problem before we leave. For this repair, mass and particle count must remain different quantities.”

Natalie marked the question for later and returned to the two cards. Nothing they needed for the neon conversion required treating mass as an exact nucleon tally.

Kàra looked back toward the drawing machine. Its fractional-neutron sketch now seemed less like a bizarre accident and more like the final product of several compressed distinctions. Count had been treated as mass; average as individual; a unit as an instruction for assembly.

Kàra: “It kept making one kind of statement do another kind's job.”

Ástríðr opened the oxygen drawer. A note explained that naturally occurring isotopic composition could vary between materials, and standard atomic-weight information could therefore be represented with a range rather than one universally exact value. The introductory O₂ molar mass of approximately 32.00 g/mol remained suitable for their supplied exercise. The reference's greater detail did not turn the rounded classroom value into a deception; it showed the precision and scope at which that value was being used.

Natalie: “So a convenient rounded card can be appropriate if its approximation is visible. The danger is pretending it settles every sample to unlimited precision.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly.”

They left the very small modern distinction between the numerical atomic-mass scale and the corresponding molar-mass scale in an optional reference drawer. It did not change any conclusion at the precision of their cards. Ms Luminara would not make a tiny advanced qualification into a toll they had to pay before using the main bridge responsibly.

Jackson copied the source names and card dates onto the data register. Henry finished his comparison, keeping the quantity headings visible. The approximate molar-mass card could stay with the neon calculation; it needed no invented claim that every neon atom had that mass in u.

Natalie put the checked card back in its sleeve. She had expected the reference cabinet to send her away with longer numbers. Instead she had a question reserved for later and the same useful approximate value, now with a clearer account of what it described. She closed the cabinet gently so the card corners would not catch in the hinge.

A bell sounded from the lower rail. One parcel had refused the station's new MOLECULES label. Its formula was CaCl₂, and its contents were an extended ionic solid. The mole could count specified entities beyond molecules. The station, proud of its recent success, had forgotten that word beyond.

## 17. The parcel without separate molecules

The CaCl₂ parcel sat on a siding under a sign that read MOLECULE COUNT UNAVAILABLE. Its transparent wall showed an extended arrangement of model ions. The station had sent a little pair of scissors down the inspection track, as though separate molecular packages merely needed to be cut out before counting could begin.

Henry removed the scissors from the track.

Henry: “The formula doesn't say this ionic solid consists of little CaCl₂ molecules waiting for packaging.”

Ms Luminara set the parcel's full description beside it: a pure, idealized calcium chloride solid for composition accounting. The formula specified the ratio of one calcium ion to two chloride ions. A formula unit was the stated composition unit used in that accounting; it did not select an isolated molecular object inside the extended solid.

Ástríðr remembered the city where no ion belonged exclusively to one partner. The memory helped, but she checked the new formula instead of importing sodium chloride's one-to-one ratio. Here the composition ratio was one to two.

Ástríðr: “The earlier city taught us not to invent couples. It didn't make every ionic ratio the same.”

Kàra studied a small model section. The display let her mark a composition count without drawing boundaries around private trios. When she tried to move one selected set of ions out as if it were an independent molecule, a notice asked whether a physical process had been specified. It had not. The counting representation did not authorize a claim about disassembly.

Kàra: “The bracket is for bookkeeping again. It isn't a fence the ions built around themselves.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly. The mole can refer to specified formula units as well as atoms, molecules, ions, or other specified entities. We must choose and name the entity appropriate to the description.”

Jackson read the shipment amount: 0.300 mol CaCl₂ formula units. The receiving ledger asked how many moles of each ion were represented in the solid's composition. Henry wrote 0.300 mol Ca²⁺ ions and 0.600 mol Cl⁻ ions. Together that was 0.900 mol ions in this idealized accounting.

Jeff noticed the superscripts and the subscript on the same line.

Jeff: “The two below chlorine counts chloride ions per formula unit. The two plus above calcium describes its charge. Those twos aren't a factor I should multiply together just because they're both visible.”

They checked the charge bookkeeping separately. One Ca²⁺ and two Cl⁻ gave a neutral formula-unit composition. The charge check explained why the stated ratio was compatible with neutrality for those ions. It was not a new multiplier for counting moles of atoms or formula units.

Natalie drew three rows: formula units, calcium ions, chloride ions. Below them she added constituent atoms by element. In this monatomic-ion case, counting calcium ions also counted calcium atomic nuclei, and counting chloride ions counted chlorine atomic nuclei. The atoms were present as ions in the solid, not as free neutral atoms sitting in separate boxes.

Natalie: “I need to be careful even with a familiar phrase like ‘moles of chlorine atoms.’ It can describe elemental composition without saying neutral chlorine atoms are the actual species here.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. State whether you're describing the present species or counting the constituent atoms by element. Both can be useful.”

The station proposed a universal rule: MULTIPLY BY TOTAL SUBSCRIPTS TO GET MOLECULES. Jackson stopped it before it printed. Adding the implied one calcium and two chlorines gave three constituent atoms per composition unit, not three molecules. The same arithmetic operation could preserve or destroy meaning depending on the requested entity.

Ástríðr asked the parcel to show only its formula for a moment. Without the supplied statement that it represented an ionic solid, a novice might rely on a composition shortcut. Ms Luminara kept the supplied identity visible. Today's task was amount and count, not a universal method for inferring every substance's physical structure from a formula alone.

The class replaced the station's master label MOLECULES with SPECIFIED ENTITIES. Beneath it they placed a required description field. The field could hold O₂ molecules, neon atoms, CaCl₂ formula units, or a more precise description when needed. It was broader without becoming vaguer, because each actual receipt still had to name its entity.

Henry looked pleased until Jeff asked whether “entities” alone would pass the gate.

Henry: “No. That's the category of the question, not the answer to which entity.”

Jeff held out his hand for the correction pen. He added EXAMPLE REQUIRED to the training sign, then changed it to ACTUAL ENTITY REQUIRED. An example could also be copied in the wrong place.

They spent a few minutes watching the lattice model without trying to extract packages from it. The mole had initially felt like a count of tiny objects one could, in principle, line up. Formula-unit accounting showed that a specified composition unit could be useful even when the material did not consist of separate molecules bearing that formula.

Natalie: “The counting unit depends on the model of the substance. I can't choose it only because it makes the arithmetic familiar.”

Ms Luminara: “That is the bridge back to structure.”

The parcel moved off the siding with a complete receipt. Its ions had not acquired exclusive partners. Its formula had not changed. What changed was the station's willingness to count the appropriate specified entity without making the substance look like something it was not.

Above the central square, all the repaired desks rang at once. Jackson looked up. They had fixed each local description. The master dispatch board was still trying to squeeze them all into a single box.

## 18. The one-box machine

The central dispatch board was larger than the bus and much less spacious. Every receipt entering it passed through a slot labeled VALUE. Beneath the slot, a crank selected one of four destinations: count, amount, mass, or average. The value box kept the digits. Most of the words were shaved off by a little brass blade.

Henry stared at the blade.

Henry: “We have been collecting its offcuts all afternoon.”

In a bin underneath lay scraps reading molecules, atoms, grams per mole, within the molecules, by number, by mass, artificial, and approximately. Jackson found the missing strip from their most recent receipt near the top. Its cut edge and printed border fitted the returned fifty-percent copy. He had kept that copy flat all the way from the pans. Here was the part they had watched disappear.

Jackson lifted the first receipt from their board, the original ONE MOLE label. Its missing entity had passed through this machine. So had the fifty-percent mass fraction that became an atom fraction, and the average that became an individual-atom instruction. The local errors shared a route.

Jackson: “If we repair only the desks, this can damage the next set of receipts again.”

Jeff reached for the pause handle. This time he did not hesitate, and he did not perform the gesture dramatically. The belt stopped before the next receipt reached the blade. Several carts remained in the waiting square. Their brakes held. There was no punishment for declining to continue a process that discarded necessary information.

Ms Luminara: “What does a useful value need to travel with?”

Nobody answered with a single word. Natalie opened a new page and began with the question being answered. Henry added the entity or substance. Kàra added the quantity and its unit. Ástríðr added the population for an average or fraction. Jeff added whether a number was exact in the model or based on approximate data. Natalie: “We don't need every possible detail on every receipt. We need the details that determine what the statement means.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. This is a reasoning repair, not a contest to make the longest label.”

They tested the new surface with three earlier tickets. The O₂ molecule count retained its entity through dispatch. The isotope mean retained its artificial-model label and its population. Kàra wanted to disconnect the brass blade entirely. Henry suggested leaving it in a drawer as a demonstration object rather than destroying it. Shortening text was not intrinsically wrong. Dropping information essential to meaning was the problem. They could still abbreviate O₂ after defining it, or use n and M in a clearly labeled calculation.

Ástríðr: “The machine needs to know the difference between fewer words and less meaning.”

Ms Luminara: “Which is why we won't pretend a blade can decide that on its own.”

They replaced automatic shortening with a review slot. A receipt could be concise, but its interpretation had to remain checkable. Jackson tried deliberately separating a unit from its value. The board flagged an incomplete record rather than borrowing a nearby unit. Jeff tried putting an average into the mass field. It asked for the quantity relationship that justified the move. None had been supplied.

Natalie: “The fence posts didn't catch this earlier. What stops it now?”

Kàra ran a finger across the board's separate checks. No single test guaranteed the answer. Units, identity, composition, magnitude, and source assumptions each guarded a different door. Their combination made the station more dependable without making it infallible.

For a quiet moment, the class looked at the heap of discarded words. Henry picked up within the molecules and flattened its curled edge. Jeff found artificial. Natalie found approximately. The scraps were modest. None contained a spectacular formula. Yet losing them had changed what the station claimed to know.

Ms Luminara: “The model changed when you stopped treating a number as a complete statement.”

Jackson clipped that sentence above the dispatch surface. He did not print it on every receipt; that would have turned a useful principle into another slogan people learned to ignore. Instead he used it to inspect the next actual order.

The order was from the far warehouse. It requested exactly half a mole of CO₂ molecules in a counting thought experiment, a constituent-atom report, and an approximate mass estimate from a supplied molar-mass card. One sample, several questions, no missing words.

Jeff picked up the ticket.

Jeff: “Let's see whether the whole route works when we don't stop after the first answer.”

## 19. Half a mole, all the way across

The CO₂ parcel waited where the unlabeled cart had first circled the square. Rain no longer fell into the old puddle; the station's roof had extended over the transfer point. The parcel carried three empty receipt panels and one clear instruction: EXACTLY 0.5 mol CO₂ MOLECULES IN THIS COUNTING THOUGHT EXPERIMENT.

A separate card supplied an approximate molar mass of 44.01 g/mol for the mass estimate. Its placement mattered. The exact amount in the idealized count did not transform the approximate molar mass into an exact physical value.

Jeff took responsibility for the molecule count. He had volunteered to climb the enormous number's staircase at the beginning. Now he could use the number without trying to picture its every member.

Jeff: “Half the Avogadro count is 3.01107038 × 10²³ CO₂ molecules.”

Ástríðr checked the result using a tiny model first. Half of something enormous was awkward to picture, but each whole CO₂ molecule still contained the same one-to-two atom relationship. Scaling the number of molecules could not change that composition.

Henry checked that the word approximately stayed attached when she slid the panel into its sleeve. Kàra estimated independently. Half a mole at roughly forty-four grams per mole should be roughly twenty-two grams. She compared that with the calculated mass before checking the detailed multiplication. If Natalie had inverted the factor or used the O₂ card, the estimate and substance check would have raised different alarms.

The cart moved onto the bridge.

At its midpoint, an old side switch flicked toward the isotope drawing room. The local selector still routed decimal numbers there, regardless of their quantity. The new dispatch board had kept the complete record, but this downstream switch had not yet read it. Henry held up the amount panel: n, unit mol, specified entity CO₂ molecules.

Jeff stopped the cart before it left the route. Kàra compared the selector's instruction with the panel and disconnected the shortcut. She sent the complete quantity record through the local reader. The switch returned to the counting-and-mass track.

Kàra: “The new board sent it correctly. We still had to see what this switch did with it.”

Ms Luminara: “A considerable achievement for a parcel, and often for a sentence.”

They stood on the bridge while the cart rolled into its bay. The number remained enormous. The mass estimate remained ordinary enough to imagine in a hand, though they were handling no real sample. Between those scales lay not a single magic fact but a chain of specified relationships. Each had kept its own job.

Natalie closed her notebook for the first time since the isotope window. Jeff held the receiving acknowledgement against the original order while Jackson checked the last entry on the route card. The cart's bell sounded from inside its bay. They had heard it ask to be let through often enough that the silence afterward took a moment to trust.

<a id="ch05-scene-20"></a>
## 20. A good gate sometimes stays shut

Before they left, Jackson asked to inspect the waiting tray. Most of the carts had moved. One small parcel remained beneath a lamp, carrying only a mass of 10.0 g and a torn corner of a chemical label. Its destination requested a number of molecules.

The old station would have borrowed the nearest molar-mass card. The repaired station displayed INSUFFICIENT IDENTITY INFORMATION and kept the gate closed.

Jeff looked disappointed for a moment.

Jeff: “After all that, we've left one stuck.”

Ástríðr read the torn label. It contained a capital C and enough blank space to suggest many possible continuations. Guessing a substance would make the arithmetic easy and the conclusion unreliable.

Ástríðr: “It was stuck before too. The difference is that now it tells us what it needs.”

Natalie found that unexpectedly satisfying. A successful method was not one that printed a number for every input. It was one that distinguished a supported result from a result produced by a hidden assumption.

Ms Luminara: “A good gate sometimes stays shut. We can leave a clear request for the missing identity instead of manufacturing it.”

Jackson wrote that request in plain language: “Please identify the substance and intended entity; mass alone does not determine number of molecules.” He attached the original torn label so nobody would mistake the request for a complaint about the sender's handwriting. The missing information was specific, and so was the next step.

Henry used the remaining time to test another boundary. He placed a molecular formula card N₂O₄ beside a proposed abbreviated card NO₂. The naming station from an earlier journey had taught them not to reduce a molecular formula automatically. Here the consequence would be a different count of atoms per molecular entity and a different molar-mass calculation.

Henry: “If the entity is a molecule, preserving its molecular formula is part of preserving the count relationship.”

Kàra built one model of each specified molecular composition without claiming either model predicted the sample's conditions. The N₂O₄ model contained two nitrogen atoms and four oxygen atoms. The NO₂ model contained one and two. The ratio of elements was the same, but the molecular entities were not interchangeable merely because a ratio could be reduced.

Natalie added this to the receipt checklist under entity identity. A formula was not decoration beside a number. Changing it could change the counting unit itself.

Jeff chose a different test. He gave the isotope desk an artificial two-isotope receipt with fractions 0.80 and 0.30. Both were individually plausible-looking percentages. Together they summed to 1.10, more than the whole population if those were the only two mutually exclusive isotope categories.

The station returned a request to check the fractions rather than quietly normalizing them. It could not know whether one percentage was mistyped, a category was defined differently, or the source intended another quantity. Dividing both by 1.10 would create a new mixture not necessarily authorized by the data.

Jeff: “It needs to report the inconsistency before choosing a repair.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly. A correction requires evidence about what went wrong.”

Ástríðr checked the station's explanations for tone. The first version read USER FAILED TO PROVIDE CORRECT DATA. She replaced it with THE SUPPLIED FRACTIONS DO NOT SUM TO ONE FOR THIS TWO-ISOTOPE MODEL. The latter described the actual problem without guessing who had caused it or how.

Jackson took a final walk along the route. The controls now separated pausing from deleting. An uncertain receipt could be held while the source was checked. A first calculation could remain beside its revision. A rejected assumption did not have to vanish from the record to make the corrected answer usable.

Natalie watched him preserve the incomplete and inconsistent cards. Earlier she had wanted a notebook full of clean conclusions. Now the first versions showed which distinctions the conclusions depended on. They were not trophies of error. They were evidence of what had changed.

Kàra leaned against the rail and asked whether the station would ever become immune to confusion. Ms Luminara said no. New materials, new questions, and new kinds of evidence could require other relationships or expose another hidden assumption. What they had built was a method of making meaning inspectable, not a promise that every possible problem had been anticipated.

Kàra: “That sounds less reassuring than a perfect station.”

Jeff: “It's more reassuring than a station that calls itself perfect while throwing labels away.”

The class laughed. The little brass blade sat safely in a drawer with its own complete description: FORMER AUTOMATIC SHORTENER; REMOVED ESSENTIAL CONTEXT. It had finally received a label that did not flatter it.

The station printed no certificate. Instead, it gave each traveler a small blank receipt with spaces for a quantity, a unit, an entity or substance, and a source or assumption when needed. Natalie tucked hers into the notebook. A blank place to ask a good question was more portable than an enormous number painted across the sky.

Behind them, the unidentified parcel remained on hold. Its next step was clear. Ahead of them, the bus waited with an ordinary door, ordinary seats, and a dashboard that no longer claimed its questions were excess weight.

<a id="ch05-scene-21"></a>
## 21. Six biscuits, later

The bus left the Counting Station by the road it had arrived on, although the road had not existed when it arrived. Nobody asked Ms Luminara to reconcile that transport detail with the scientific models. The class had learned to distinguish the vehicle of the story from the claims the story was helping them inspect.

For a while, nobody spoke. Jeff kept the successful CO₂ receipt in his lap. Kàra watched the warehouses recede until HOW MANY and HOW HEAVY looked like two small marks on the horizon. Between them, the bridge was visible only as a thin line.

Natalie opened her notebook to the first page. “I understand a dozen” sat beneath its original heading. Around it had grown questions, arrows, corrections, and a small drawing of a cart with no useful label.

She did not cross out the first sentence. It had been a good starting point. She had needed to discover what she understood about a dozen and what she had never asked it to do.

Natalie: “The weighted averages felt familiar once I knew what population we were averaging over. Before that, the chemistry words made me doubt a statistical idea I already had.”

Ms Luminara: “And what did the familiar idea need from the chemistry?”

Natalie considered before answering.

Natalie: “What an isotope is. Which masses were artificial. Whether the fractions were of atom counts or mass. What an average atomic mass can and can't describe.”

Henry nodded. The bridge went both ways. Prior understanding helped the new subject; the new subject required precision about what the old method was operating on.

Kàra took out the receipt from the first small isotope sample, nine light and one heavy counter. It had disagreed with the larger population's mean without being a malfunction. She liked keeping that one. It reminded her to ask about the boundary before trying to force agreement.

Kàra reached across the aisle for the portable pause handle. It had become a small wooden token now that they were aboard. She turned it over and passed it back to Jeff.

Kàra: “I was glad you had that at the last switch.”

Jeff: “I nearly let the cart go because we'd already fixed the board.”

He fitted the token into his pocket beside the reference card. From the window he could no longer distinguish the little switch from the bridge. He could still remember where he had been standing when it moved.

Ástríðr retrieved her first halfway prediction. Beside it she had written “true for equal atom counts in the artificial two-isotope model.” She had no wish to erase the guess. It showed how an intuition could be preserved by finding its conditions, rather than defended against every contrary observation.

Ástríðr: “This card needs the same little collection beside it when I get home. Otherwise I'll forget why I kept it.”

Jackson smiled at that. His board of receipts rested against the seat beside him, each first version clipped to the revision that explained what changed. He would have to choose which parts to keep on a compact carry-home reference. For now, the chain of repairs mattered more than neatness.

Henry picked up the small blank receipt the station had given him. He wrote ONE MOLE and stopped, allowing the blank space after it to remain visible.

Henry: “This question still comes first.”

Natalie supplied it without looking up.

Natalie: “Of what?”

The bus bell rang in exactly the relieved way the first cart's bell had rung. Ms Luminara glanced at the dashboard.

Ms Luminara: “Apparently the vehicle has been listening. That is either excellent or a warranty concern.”

At the back of the bus, Ástríðr opened the biscuit tin. Its lid still said SIX BISCUITS. Underneath, in smaller print, someone had added CONTENTS MAY SETTLE.

Ástríðr: “Do settled biscuits count differently?”

Henry: “Not unless some have been eaten.”

Ms Luminara looked thoughtfully at the empty space beside her teacup.

Ms Luminara: “A remarkably important condition.”

There were two biscuits left. The label had described an earlier state; the present count required a present observation. Ástríðr broke one biscuit to share it, then laughed when Henry raised an eyebrow. Whether there were now three pieces or two original biscuits represented by pieces depended on the question. They did not turn the snack into another full investigation. They already knew how to ask if it became important.

The classroom appeared beyond the windshield. Tables regained ordinary sizes. The rooftop digits were gone, but Jeff still had them on a reference card. He did not feel able to imagine 6.02214076 × 10²³ objects. He no longer considered that a requirement for reasoning responsibly with the count.

Natalie wrote four questions on the final page: What entities? How many or how much? What mass relationship? What population does the average describe? Beneath them she left a fifth line blank for the question a later problem might reveal.

Ms Luminara opened the door. Nobody had counted every particle, invented a fractional neutron, or weighed a mole as though it were one universal kind of parcel. They had learned to carry a statement across scales without dropping the information that made it true.

The station had sent them home with numbers. What they kept beside the numbers made the greater difference.

# Optional vocabulary support

# Optional support for the Mole Counting Station

Story ID: luminara.adventure.005

The story stands alone. Choose a reference, picture, worked example, or explanation before, during, or after reading. Nothing here is a required quiz, timer, or demonstration of confidence. A calculator, speech, a drawing, and written calculations are equally legitimate tools.

## Start with the question

A useful receipt says what quantity is wanted and what entities or substance it concerns. “0.5 mol” is incomplete without its entity; “50%” needs a denominator; “10.2” needs a quantity and unit where applicable.

- Number of CO₂ molecules is not the same count as number of oxygen atoms within those molecules.
- Equal amounts of neon atoms and O₂ molecules mean equal numbers of the separately named entities. They need not have equal masses or equal constituent-atom counts.
- An isotope mean describes a stated population or sample. It is not an individual-atom construction instruction.

Story routes: [the missing label](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-01), [the scanner](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-03), [the one-box machine](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-18).

## Contextual glossary

### Specified entity

The object or composition unit being counted: for example, Ne atoms, O₂ molecules, Ca²⁺ ions, electrons, or CaCl₂ formula units. “Particles” may be too vague when different entities are possible. Formal units alone do not prevent substituting the wrong species. See [Scenes 2–4](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-02) and [Scene 17](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-17).

### Number of entities, N

A count of the specified entities. N is dimensionless in formal quantity calculus; words such as “O₂ molecules” keep its interpretation clear. A reported count inferred from a measurement may be approximate even though any actual finite collection contains an integer number of its specified members. See [Scene 7](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-07).

### Amount of substance, n; mole, mol

Amount of substance is the quantity measured in moles. One mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ specified entities. It is not a universal mass, a container, or a fixed volume under unspecified conditions. A fractional number of moles does not require fractional molecules. See [Scene 2](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-02).

### Avogadro constant, N_A

N_A = exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. Its exact defining value links amount and number: N = nN_A, or n = N/N_A. The subscript A is a name label on this constant, not an atom count. See [Scene 2](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-02).

### Mass, m

A physical quantity expressed in units such as g, kg, or u. The mass of a sample alone does not identify its chemical composition or determine its number of molecules. See [Scenes 5–7](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-05).

### Molar mass, M

Mass divided by amount for a specified substance, commonly expressed in g/mol: M = m/n. Consequently m = nM and n = m/M. It is substance-specific and may depend on the sample's isotopic composition. The story supplies approximate values rather than claiming unlimited precision. See [Scene 5](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-05).

### Molecular formula and constituent-atom count

For a specified molecular species, subscripts give the numbers of constituent atoms in each molecule. CO₂ has one C and two O atoms per molecule. Thus 0.5 mol CO₂ molecules contains 0.5 mol C atoms and 1 mol O atoms within those molecules, totaling 1.5 mol constituent atoms. The counting description does not break bonds or claim those atoms are free neutral species. A molecular formula must not be automatically reduced: N₂O₄ and NO₂ describe different molecular compositions. See [Scenes 4](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-04), [19](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-19), and [20](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-20).

### Formula unit

A specified composition unit for formula accounting. In an ordinary ionic solid, it does not imply isolated molecules or uniquely paired ions. The supplied idealized CaCl₂ solid has one Ca²⁺ for two Cl⁻ in its formula ratio. One mole of CaCl₂ formula units represents one mole Ca²⁺ and two moles Cl⁻ in that composition accounting. See [Scene 17](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-17).

### Isotope, proton number, neutron number, and mass number

Isotopes of one element have the same proton number and different neutron numbers. Mass number is the integer count of protons plus neutrons in a nucleus; it is not identical to an atom's measured mass expressed in u. A change in isotopic mixture need not change the masses or neutron counts of any individual isotope. See [Scenes 9](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-09) and [16](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-16).

### Unified atomic mass unit, u; dalton, Da

u and Da name the same unit of mass. The atomic mass constant is one twelfth of the mass of a free neutral carbon-12 atom at rest in its ground state. Real isotope masses generally are not whole-number multiples of u. The 10 u/11 u story model and 24 u/26 u practice model are explicitly invented simplifications, not measurements or claimed isotope data for a real element. See [Scene 16](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-16).

### Average atomic mass

The total mass of atoms in a specified collection divided by their number, expressible in u. Equivalently, sum each isotope mass multiplied by that isotope's atom-number fraction. The result need not equal any member's mass. In the artificial 80:20 mixture of 10 u/11 u model atoms, the mean is 10.2 u, while every member is assigned either 10 u or 11 u. See [Scene 10](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-10).

### Atom-number fraction and mass fraction

An isotope's atom-number fraction is its number of atoms divided by the total number of atoms. Its mass fraction is its mass contribution divided by the total mass. These denominators differ. Use atom-number fractions to weight isotope masses for average atomic mass. The word “weight” in a weighted mean does not automatically mean a mass fraction. See [Scene 14](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-14).

### Relative atomic mass; atomic weight

A dimensionless ratio of average atomic mass to the atomic mass constant. It is related to average atomic mass and molar mass, but their unit labels are not interchangeable. Standard atomic-weight values and intervals are reference information for defined materials and conditions, not a guarantee that every possible sample has an identical isotope mixture. See [Scene 16](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-16).

### Sample, population, and pooled mean

The population is the collection a claim concerns. A sample is a selected part used for description or inference. A sample mean describes the sample; treating it as an estimate for a wider population requires attention to selection and uncertainty. To pool batch means into a mean over all atoms, weight each batch mean by its atom count, not merely by the number of batch cards. See [Scenes 13](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-13) and [15](../chapters/05_moles/STORY.md#ch05-scene-15).

## Optional support A: three relations, different jobs

1. N = nN_A links number of specified entities and amount.
2. m = nM links mass and amount for the specified substance.
3. Constituent count = entity count × constituents per entity, using the supplied composition.

The first relation does not identify the substance. The second needs its appropriate molar mass. The third needs an appropriate formula or composition description. None by itself proves purity, volume, physical state, or measured precision.

## Optional support B: Natalie's dimensional fence posts

Given 8.00 g O₂ and supplied M(O₂) ≈ 32.00 g/mol:

8.00 g O₂ × (1 mol O₂ / 32.00 g O₂) = 0.250 mol O₂ molecules.

Then:

0.250 mol O₂ molecules × (2 mol O atoms / 1 mol O₂ molecules) = 0.500 mol O atoms within those molecules.

For a number of molecules, use 0.250 mol × N_A, giving approximately 1.51 × 10²³ O₂ molecules. For a number of constituent oxygen atoms, use 0.500 mol × N_A, giving approximately 3.01 × 10²³ O atoms within the molecules.

Check four different things:

- Does the relation apply to this substance and entity?
- Do the factors face the direction that cancels the starting units?
- Does the magnitude fit an estimate?
- Does the precision match the supplied data?

Units can catch a reversed factor. They cannot by themselves catch using neon's molar mass for O₂, because both use g/mol. A calculator completing an operation is not independent evidence that the operation was appropriate.

## Optional support C: an average without a new atom

Artificial model: eighty atoms assigned 10 u and twenty assigned 11 u.

Total assigned mass = 80(10 u) + 20(11 u) = 1,020 u.

Mean = 1,020 u / 100 = 10.2 u.

Equivalent weighted form: 0.80(10 u) + 0.20(11 u) = 10.2 u.

Baseline view: start with all one hundred at 10 u, then include 1 u extra for each of twenty heavy members. The extra twenty units across one hundred atoms gives a mean increment of 0.2 u. This spreads the increment in the calculation, not through the actual nuclei.

Checks: fractions sum to one; answer lies between the two masses; answer is closer to the more abundant isotope's mass. The midpoint applies only to equal atom-number fractions in a two-isotope model with those values.

## Optional support D: reverse and pooled means

For two specified artificial masses a and b, with b greater than a and heavy atom fraction f:

mean = a(1 − f) + bf = a + f(b − a).

Therefore f = (mean − a)/(b − a). This recovers a proportion under the stated two-isotope assumptions, not the total number of atoms or an unknown real chemical identity. A result outside 0–1 flags incompatible inputs or model assumptions.

For batches with means μ₁, μ₂ and atom counts N₁, N₂:

pooled mean = (N₁μ₁ + N₂μ₂)/(N₁ + N₂).

The simple mean (μ₁ + μ₂)/2 works when the two batches have equal atom counts, or accidentally in special cases such as equal means. Without relative counts, the pooled mean is generally undetermined.

## Optional support E: exact definitions and approximate samples

The defined Avogadro constant is exact. The story's explicitly exact counting thought experiments can therefore have exact arithmetic results. Measurements, approximate molar masses, purity assumptions, and sampling still limit claims about real specimens. Avoid both extremes: copying all calculator digits as measured certainty, or rounding the defining constant and calling that rounded value the definition.

Rounding tie in Scene 6: the displayed product 2.50 × 20.18 = 50.45 g falls exactly halfway between 50.4 g and 50.5 g at three significant figures. The story explicitly supplies the half-up convention, giving 50.5 g. The ties-to-even convention used by OpenStax gives 50.4 g instead. Both are legitimate reporting conventions; neither is claimed to be the instructor’s rule. Retain the working value and follow the convention specified for an actual task. Significant figures alone do not settle an exact tie or replace a full uncertainty analysis.

Optional advanced note: since the 2019 SI revision, the familiar numerical correspondence between an atomic mass in u and its corresponding molar mass in g/mol is extraordinarily close but not an exact universal identity. This nuance has no material effect at this chapter's introductory precision and is not an additional assessment requirement.

Optional advanced note from Scene 16: free proton and neutron masses are not exactly 1 u, and electrons also contribute to atomic mass. A bound nucleus has less mass than its separated protons and neutrons; the difference corresponds to its binding energy divided by c². A neutral atom additionally includes electrons and their electronic binding contribution. Electrons are not constituents of the nucleus, and atomic mass is not nuclear mass. None of these mass–energy distinctions makes the nucleon count fractional: mass number remains an integer count of protons and neutrons. See source S08 in [SOURCES.md](../chapters/05_moles/SOURCES.md). No nuclear calculation is required here.

## What is deliberately outside this chapter

Gas-law calculations and gas volumes at specified temperature and pressure; chemical-reaction stoichiometry; concentration and solution preparation; laboratory measurements; isotope-separation methods; nuclear procedures; detailed uncertainty propagation; and full statistical inference. The chapter also does not claim to cover all supplied Unit II content, Lewis structures, or molecular geometry.
