# The City Without Couples

Story ID: luminara.adventure.003  
Newly authored fiction for the Ms Luminara Adventures collection

## The invitation problem

The bus received an invitation to the Salt Crystal Dance. Jeff checked the small print twice. There would be no compulsory dancing, and the floor was an illustration rather than an actual sample of sodium metal or chlorine gas. That settled two concerns. He promptly volunteered to investigate the mysterious balcony.

Natalie found Ms Luminara fastening a paper warning to the steering wheel: DO NOT CONFUSE THE STORY OF ARRIVAL WITH THE REASON PEOPLE STAY.

Natalie: “That sounds like it belongs in several subjects.”

Ms Luminara: “Today it belongs to ions. We'll resist making them responsible for our relationships.”

They arrived in a vast three-dimensional hall of alternating sodium and chloride ions. Sodium carried charge plus one; chloride carried charge minus one. The hall extended beyond every window. Near its center, each ion had six nearest neighbors of the opposite charge.

At the entrance, a mechanical usher kept tying a ribbon around one sodium ion and one chloride ion. It announced each pair as a separate NaCl molecule, then tried to arrange the pairs into rows.

Henry: “That isn't what the hall is showing us.”

He turned slowly, checking the neighboring positions instead of trusting the usher's announcement.

Henry: “There isn't a special two-ion couple separated from the rest.”

Ms Luminara: “Correct for this ordinary solid. Sodium chloride forms an extended ionic lattice. Its formula gives the simplest ratio of sodium to chloride, one to one. We describe formula units without pretending the crystal is built from independent NaCl molecules.”

The usher looked at its ribbons as though they had personally disappointed it.

## A bond is not a memory

Kàra hurried toward the balcony, then stopped at its transparent floor. She wanted the better view and disliked the apparent height. Jeff offered the ordinary stairway instead of telling her the feeling was irrational. They climbed together and discovered that the balcony had a perfectly opaque bench.

From there, the lattice's repeated arrangement became easier to see.

Kàra: “My first picture was a sodium atom giving an electron to a chlorine atom, so those two became partners forever.”

Ms Luminara: “Electron transfer can help us account for forming these ions. But the ionic bond concerns attraction between opposite charges. An ion doesn't remember a favorite partner because of that introductory story.”

Natalie: “Like drawing a force diagram: the present interactions matter. A history of how we arranged the objects doesn't replace the interactions now.”

Ms Luminara: “Useful comparison. The detailed quantum description is richer, but the electrostatic picture corrects this particular mistake.”

Jackson discovered why the usher's error mattered. Its seating chart claimed every ion was connected to only one other, so removing one couple left its neighbors unchanged. The real lattice picture showed a network of interactions.

Jackson: “If our model gets the connections wrong, it tells us the wrong story about what changes when part of the structure changes.”

They replaced the seating chart with a labeled neighborhood view. Positive and negative signs distinguished the ions; color was decorative. A text description identified the six nearest neighbors, and no one had to spin a moving three-dimensional model to understand it.

Ástríðr retrieved the ribbons.

Ástríðr: “Can we keep them for actual groupings, rather than pretend this crystal has couples?”

Ms Luminara: “I was hoping you'd ask.”

## Different things deserve different questions

The bus's next room contained three displays: N₂, CO₂, and NH₄⁺. A sorting machine offered only two drawers, IONIC and COVALENT. It was already rattling in distress.

Henry: “Those drawers mix questions. Are we identifying a species, classifying a substance, or describing bonds within it?”

Ms Luminara removed the machine's drawers entirely.

They began with N₂. Two nitrogen atoms formed a neutral molecule. It contained only one element, so it was not a compound, even though the nitrogen atoms were covalently bonded.

Jeff: “Two atoms doesn't automatically mean two elements.”

Natalie: “And covalent doesn't automatically mean compound.”

CO₂ contained carbon and oxygen atoms in a neutral molecule. It was a molecular compound. In their introductory description, covalent bonding involved shared electron density between bonded atoms. The electrons were not miniature ribbons, and the ribbons were not evidence.

Ástríðr placed one ribbon around the entire ammonium display, NH₄⁺. It enclosed one nitrogen and four hydrogens, with the plus sign outside the outline.

Ástríðr: “The group is charged as a whole. Does having covalent bonds inside cancel that?”

Henry: “No. Its overall charge makes it an ion. Multiple atoms make it polyatomic.”

Ms Luminara: “Exactly. The N–H bonds within ammonium are covalent. Calling the whole species a polyatomic ion answers a different question.”

Kàra wrote three headings on the window: WHICH ATOMS? WHAT OVERALL CHARGE? WHAT INTERACTIONS?

Kàra: “The confusion shrinks when the questions stop sitting on top of one another.”

## An entire ion comes along

The last room was a model workshop. Its tiles represented intact ammonium ions, NH₄⁺, and carbonate ions, CO₃²⁻. The workshop needed a formula for their neutral ionic compound.

Jackson set one tile of each on a balance labeled NET CHARGE. The total was minus one: plus one together with minus two.

Jeff picked up a second ammonium tile.

Jeff: “Another plus one makes zero. We need two ammoniums for each carbonate.”

He said it carefully, then set the tile down with unexpected delight. His worry about choosing the wrong piece had not disappeared. It had become small enough to carry while investigating.

The printer produced (NH₄)₂CO₃. Henry pointed to the parentheses before anyone moved on.

Henry: “The outside two multiplies the whole NH₄ group. Two nitrogens and eight hydrogens, together with one carbon and three oxygens.”

Natalie: “If I write N₂H₄ instead, I've repeated the nitrogen without bringing the second ion's four hydrogens.”

Ms Luminara: “Yes. We can change how many tiles we use. We can't quietly change the composition printed on a tile.”

Ástríðr: “And the compound is ionic even though I can't find a metal. Ammonium is the positive ion.”

The old two-drawer machine attempted to return. Henry sent it away with a more precise job description.

They tested another pair: calcium ions, Ca²⁺, and oxide ions, O²⁻. One of each already balanced. CaO described the simplest whole-number ratio. A formula Ca₂O₂ would multiply both ion counts without expressing the simplest ratio for this ionic formula.

Jackson: “But don't let the printer reduce every formula. A molecular formula tells the actual atom counts in a molecule. N₂O₄ isn't permission to replace it with NO₂.”

Ms Luminara: “A rule must travel with the question it answers.”

## Beyond the model's railing

On the balcony again, Kàra leaned against the opaque bench and watched the labeled lattice instead of its apparent depth.

Kàra: “Are ionic and covalent two perfectly separate kinds of reality?”

Ms Luminara: “They are useful models and classifications, with limits. Real bonding can have mixed character. We can learn the dominant pattern here without pretending every bond belongs at a pure endpoint.”

Natalie: “So the correction isn't to throw away every category. It's to know what each category describes, and how confidently.”

Ástríðr tied a ribbon around the pile of retired seating charts.

Ástríðr: “This is a group held together by my knot. I will not submit it as a chemical theory.”

Jeff laughed first. The usher gave one tentative mechanical chuckle and offered everyone a one-to-one ratio of biscuits to napkins.

## The useful souvenir

They took home no ions, no chemical samples, and none of the dance floor. Henry kept the three questions. Jackson kept the corrected network diagram. Jeff kept his balcony sketch, and Kàra kept a note saying ASK WHICH QUESTION THE LABEL ANSWERS.

Natalie added a thought beneath it: An explanation can be tidy and still connect the wrong things.

Ms Luminara checked that everyone had boarded, then turned the invitation over. On the back she wrote: Arrival stories, present interactions, and classification labels are different tools. Use each for its own job.

The bus pulled away. Behind them, the usher removed its final pair ribbon and discovered it could welcome the whole lattice without inventing couples.
