# Ms Luminara and the Compound Naming Station

Story ID: luminara.adventure.004

Newly authored chemistry adventure

## Narrative

The bus's destination sign said TWO.

It had said TWO at the last six junctions. Now it said TWO in a larger font, which did not help.

Ms Luminara: “The station has upgraded its navigation system. Apparently it now knows one thing very confidently.”

She parked beside a platform where enormous formula cards traveled along silver rails. FeCl₂ approached a gate marked IRON TWO. CO₂ followed it. The gate tried to send both down the same track. A padded barrier lowered, and the machinery stopped with a disappointed chime.

Jeff: “Is this the sort of field trip where we're the replacement machinery?”

Ms Luminara: “Only if you volunteer. We're inside a teaching model, and everything can wait. The help drawer opens before anyone answers.”

Jeff opened it immediately. Out slid charge cards, a prefix list, and a cushion.

Jeff: “An unusually well-equipped drawer.”

Natalie unfolded the station map. Having taught physics did not make these naming conventions familiar. She recognized the problem of a symbol doing different jobs, though. Beside the platform stood a brass machine labeled UNIVERSAL NUMBER INTERPRETER. Its instructions occupied one line: FIND A NUMBER AND SAY IT.

Natalie: “That sounds efficient until someone asks what the number means.”

Henry: “Also, it hasn't specified which number.”

A maintenance compartment opened. Two routes appeared: IONIC COMPOUNDS and BINARY MOLECULAR COMPOUNDS. Their wires had been twisted together.

Ms Luminara offered the driving controls to Kàra. Kàra's knee had been bouncing since the barrier fell.

Kàra: “I'd rather check the map before driving. My brain is already taking every exit.”

Ms Luminara: “Excellent driving policy.”

They chose the ionic route first. The bus rolled into a vast repeating arrangement of iron and chloride markers. There were no tidy little FeCl₂ passengers holding hands in separate trios. The pattern continued beyond the windows.

Ms Luminara: “This display represents an ionic solid. Its formula gives a ratio in the extended structure. We aren't touring a box of separate FeCl₂ molecules.”

Henry selected the supplied chloride card: Cl⁻, charge minus one. Two chloride markers illuminated for every iron marker.

Henry: “Those contribute minus two altogether. The iron must balance that with plus two.”

Ms Luminara: “For this introductory ionic description, yes. Iron(II) chloride.”

She spoke the name as “iron two chloride” while printing the Roman numeral in parentheses.

Natalie: “So the little two after chlorine counts chlorides in the formula ratio. The II in the name tells us about iron.”

Ms Luminara: “Specifically its oxidation state, plus two. In this simple ion bookkeeping, that matches the iron ion's charge. It does not say there are two irons.”

They attached separate labels to the two twos. The first track unlocked. Jackson, watching the overhead departures board, saw one destination change and fourteen others remain blocked.

Jackson: “Fixing one sign hasn't fixed the machine. It will make the same mistake with the next compound.”

The next compound was already arriving: Fe₂O₃. Its label read IRON TWO OXIDE.

Kàra stared at the subscript, then at the help drawer.

Kàra: “Can we leave that proposed name visible while we test it?”

Ms Luminara: “Please. Evidence is easier to discuss when nobody erases it.”

Ástríðr picked up an oxide card, O²⁻, and placed three copies on the console.

Ástríðr: “Three minus twos. A very grumpy six.”

Henry: “Negative six total.”

Ástríðr: “My grumpy six accepts the correction.”

Two iron markers waited opposite them. Kàra dragged a plus-six counter into the space between the irons, then split it equally.

Kàra: “Plus three each. Like balancing forces, except this ledger is charge, not force.”

Ms Luminara: “Keep that boundary on the analogy. You've got it.”

They wrote iron(III) oxide. Natalie added the optional equation beneath the picture: 2q + 3(−2) = 0. The picture already explained the reasoning; nobody had to solve the equation to board.

The station's interpreter clicked, considered its error, and produced a new rule: IF THERE IS A METAL, USE IONIC. OTHERWISE, USE MOLECULAR.

Ástríðr: “That feels plausible. Shall we try to break it gently?”

She selected a waiting card: (NH₄)₂SO₄. The machine sent it toward the molecular tunnel. An ion-shaped lock refused to open.

Jeff peered through the window. Inside the ammonium markers, nitrogen and hydrogen were joined. Nearby, sulfate markers contained sulfur and oxygen.

Jeff: “There are no metals, but those groups have charge cards.”

Ms Luminara: “Ammonium, NH₄⁺, and sulfate, SO₄²⁻. The compound is ionic overall. Covalent bonds hold the atoms together within these polyatomic ions.”

Ástríðr: “So my useful-looking shortcut needs an exception before it gets a steering wheel.”

Jackson rerouted the card using the verified ion identities. Two whole ammonium ions supplied plus two; one sulfate supplied minus two. He kept every internal subscript intact.

Jackson: “Ammonium sulfate. And the sign can't say ‘diammonium’ just because the formula uses two ammoniums.”

Ms Luminara: “Right for the naming convention we're using. First identify the language; then interpret its numbers.”

They replaced the machine's rule with a reference drawer and a question: WHAT KIND OF COMPOUND IS THIS KNOWN TO BE? Unfamiliar cases could wait for evidence. The periodic table offered useful clues, not permission to classify every compound by a shortcut.

The bus crossed into the molecular tunnel. Separate illuminated models drifted inside display chambers. One carbon marker and two oxygen markers traveled together in each CO₂ model.

Henry: “Now the formula describes the contents of one molecule.”

Ms Luminara: “Carbon dioxide. The di- counts two oxygen atoms per molecule. We usually omit mono- for one atom of the first element.”

A larger model floated past: two nitrogen markers and four oxygen markers. Ástríðr unfolded the prefix list like a restaurant menu.

Ástríðr: “Di- for two nitrogen atoms. Tetra- for four oxygen atoms. Dinitrogen tetroxide?”

Ms Luminara: “That's the familiar contracted spelling. The counts are two and four.”

The universal interpreter suddenly brightened. It had discovered that two and four shared a factor. A mechanical eraser extended toward N₂O₄.

Jeff leaned forward, then hesitated with his hand above PAUSE.

Jeff: “I want to stop it. I also want someone to check why.”

Natalie: “Tell us what you noticed.”

Jeff: “It's turning the molecule's formula into NO₂. Dividing a written formula doesn't cut the molecule into equivalent versions of itself.”

He pressed PAUSE. The eraser stopped a comfortable distance from the sign.

Ms Luminara: “Exactly. N₂O₄ and NO₂ are different molecular formulas. NO₂ gives the simplest atom ratio for N₂O₄, its empirical formula. That wasn't the question we asked.”

The station fell quiet. Its fault was larger than a mistaken name: it had been answering several different questions as if they were one.

Together they rebuilt the console. Henry gave charge and atom count separate fields. Natalie added a place to show reasoning. Ástríðr labeled predictions as predictions. Kàra installed a pause lever reachable from every seat. Jeff stocked the help drawer. Jackson checked that correcting one route could not silently rewrite another.

The barrier rose. The formula cards reached their proper platforms with their identities intact.

On the way home, Ms Luminara changed the bus's destination from TWO to CLASSROOM.

Kàra: “Very bold. A destination with meaning.”

Ms Luminara: “I thought we'd try it.”

Jeff kept the cushion. Nobody charged him two of anything.
